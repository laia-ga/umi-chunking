## SUMMARY

Ophthalmic professional organizations such as the American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus (AAPOS) strive to support the optimal detection, prevention, and treatment of amblyopia to reduce the community burden of vision loss. To support population-level monitoring with regard to outcomes of childhood amblyopia screening and treatment, the AAPOS Vision Screening and the Research committees propose a set of guidelines for assessing population prevalence of persistent vision impairment due to amblyopia. The committees defined “residual amblyopia” as persistent vision impairment with best-corrected visual acuity worse than logMAR 0.3 (20/40) in one eye with an interocular difference, or visual acuity worse than logMAR 0.3 in both eyes, attributable to unilateral or bilateral amblyopia respectively, with visual acuity deficits not due to other organic causes. Epidemiological assessment of residual amblyopia should be made at an age when amblyopia should have already been detected and treated (8 years). A standardized assessment of residual amblyopia has the potential to promote public health interventions, including quality-improvement projects, for amblyopia screening and subsequent treatment. Pediatric vision screening requires coordinated efforts and resources with ongoing collaboration among pediatric eye specialists, orthoptists, pediatricians, and families. Balancing resources between early screening and needed therapy remains a critical consideration. There is currently no ongoing effort to measure the broad impact of amblyopia screening and therapy. Nonstandardized vision screening methods in preschool and school-age children often precludes direct comparison of outcomes.

The phase early in a child’s life when neuroplasticitythe brain’s ability to change—is maximal allows for both the development of and therapeutic recovery from amblyopia. 1 Vision screening is helpful early in this “latent phase,” because therapy can improve vision. 2 Later in life, neuroplasticity wanes, and therapeutic intervention yields diminishing improvement. 3 , 4

Residual amblyopia, sometimes referred to as “persisting amblyopia,” 5 is amblyopia not cured by the end of the critical period, 6 which is roughly the child’s first decade of life. It can be defined as diminished visual acuity associated with amblyopia risk factors for which anatomical or physiologic reasons for diminished vision (eg, optic nerve hypoplasia, media opacity, cerebral visual impairment) are not found. Preferred practice guidelines have been published regarding early childhood vision screening for amblyopia,7,8 confirmatory eye examinations,9 and amblyopia therapy.10 With consistent, optimized early vision screening followed by thorough and appropriately timed treatment, the prevalence and severity of residual amblyopia at the population level should be minimized,11,12 without disparities according to factors such as race and ethnicity.13-15 Conversely, one would predict that communities lacking adequate screening and amblyopia therapy resources will experience higher rates of residual amblyopia.

Left untreated or undertreated, amblyopia results in monocular and/or binocular vision impairment for life. There is at the same time a substantial societal cost for pediatric vision screening and for initial and ongoing amblyopia therapy. Therefore, there is a need from a public health perspective to monitor how well the prevalence of vision impairment due to amblyopia is being addressed by existing screening and treatment approaches.

An exemplary community effort began in Haifa, Israel, in 1968, where Eibschitz and colleagues16 implemented modified retinoscopy for childhood screening. Referred children underwent eye examinations and amblyopia therapy. Comparison at age 8 years with an unscreened cohort of children in nearby Hadera during the years 1988-1990 revealed significantly lower amblyopia prevalence (amblyopia worse than 20/32 found in 1% of the Haifa cohort vs 2.6% in Hadera, and residual amblyopia measured in logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution [logMAR] lines of visual acuity worse than 20/40 per 1,000 children was 61 in unscreened Hadera and 6 in Haifa) despite similar ethnic backgrounds and access to eye care. In another populationbased study, children born in Israel with good access to vision screening had less vision loss and amblyopia than those who immigrated to Israel from Russia, where widespread vision screening was less available.17

Although screening for amblyopia and other refractive conditions that could affect childhood vision development is generally recommended, and efforts to quantify the population prevalence of residual amblyopia have been made in Israel16,17 and in parts of Scandanavia,18,19 the effects of screening are not consistently monitored worldwide. Population-level monitoring of outcomes is essential to improve the quality of screening, diagnosis, and treatment services.

In this commentary, which is a joint effort of the Vision Screening Committee and the Research Committee of the American Association for Pediatric Ophthalmology and Strabismus, we propose a uniform set of guidelines for assessing the community prevalence of residual amblyopia. Using standardized methods of monitoring the population-level efficacy of amblyopia screening and therapy, registries could collect, analyze, and distribute uni-formly gathered data enabling comparisons between regions, highlighting effective strategies for reducing residual amblyopia, and identifying regions in need of improvement in public health efforts.

# Proposal

Standardized assessment of the population prevalence of residual amblyopia requires selection of an appropriate cohort to study, population-level data collection, and a uniform definition of the residual amblyopia outcome.

Cohort Selection

Selection of children for residual amblyopia assessment ideally involves randomly sampling from the general population to ensure population representativeness.14 Cohorts or samples may include those who did not participate in early childhood vision screening versus those who underwent clearly defined active screening and treatment. Issues of random sampling and sample size should be considered. The cohort or sample may represent a school district, a large interconnected medical practice, a state, or a country.

Various early childhood vision screening approaches are amenable to outcomes assessment in this manner. Those chosen for study should adhere to American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines20 and can include scheduled combinations of newborn red reflex, infant fixation behavior, cover testing and corneal light reflex testing, Bruckner€ testing, photoscreening, autorefraction, binocular retinal polarization scanning and sensory tests of visual acuity and stereopsis, seamlessly integrated into pediatric care settings, schools, community health centers, or dedicated health screening clinics. Residual amblyopia outcomes should be assessed in cohorts of individuals for whom the types and quantity of their early childhood vision screening and amblyopia therapy was standardized or is documented or can be recalled by parents and caregivers.

Data Collection

It would be prohibitive to conduct a comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation for every individual in a cohort of interest. Therefore, for the purpose of determining residual amblyopia prevalence, assessments could take place in two stages. The first would consist of monocular visual acuity testing in a general health care, educational or community setting, or in an eye doctor’s office. This step (hereafter “screening for residual amblyopia”) would filter out individuals without significant residual amblyopia, leaving a mixture of those with residual amblyopia and those with vision abnormalities due to factors other than amblyopia, such as uncorrected refractive error or ocular disease. Screening for residual amblyopia would then be followed by confirmatory eye examinations for individuals with vision abnormalities in order to determine whether or not they were attributable to amblyopia.

## Outcome Measures

Measurement of the population prevalence of residual amblyopia should occur in individuals old enough to have largely completed amblyopia treatment, assuming they were identified by typical early childhood vision screening efforts, and old enough to be unlikely to have irreversible visual acuity loss due to cessation of treatment. On the other hand, a resource-intensive screening effort should ideally also capture individuals while they are yet in the critical period of visual neuroplasticity, in order that they may still benefit from identification of amblyopia in the event that they were not previously diagnosed and treated. The outcome age could be as early as age 8 years (third grade), with better potential to detect some children with a hope of treatment, or later than age 10 years (fifth grade). The committees recommend 8 years of age.

Acceptable optotypes for screening for residual amblyopia should conform to accepted examination standards and could include Lea Symbols, Lea Numbers, Lippman’s HOTV or Parr 22 or Sloan letters or also rotating Landolt C and Tumbling E. With regard to comprehensive confirmatory outcome examinations, residual unilateral amblyopia is defined as best-corrected logMAR visual acuity worse than 0.3 in at least one eye with a significant interocular difference in visual acuity and a corresponding unilateral amblyopia risk factor for the eye with worse visual acuity ( Table 1 ), in the absence of ocular disease sufficient to explain the reduced visual acuity. Residual bilateral amblyopia is defined as best-corrected logMAR visual acuity worse than 0.3 in both eyes with a corresponding bilateral amblyopia risk factor ( Table 1 ), in the absence of ocular disease sufficient to explain the reduced visual acuity. Patients should be tested wearing a current glasses prescription; if not available, pinhole testing should be performed. Monocular visual acuity testing should be performed using an occlusive eye patch, with a testing distance of 3 meters or greater.

Optotypes used for testing should comply with accepted examination standards and can include Lea symbols, Lea numbers, Lippman’s HOTV, JVAS, 25 Parr, 22 or Sloan letters. Identical, symmetric rotated optotypes (eg, Landolt C or Tumbling E) may also be used. Optotypes should be chosen and presented to optimally identify amblyopia. Sequential linear lines of optotypes and/or single optotypes with crowding bars achieve this desired crowding phenomenon and should be used. 26 The testing paradigm should minimize the possibility of a child “guessing” the correct optotype. Ideally, the chance of an accidental “pass” should be less than 1 in 20.

In addition to visual acuity testing results, data to be collected at the confirmatory examination include the history of prior vision screenings, prior eye care, family history of eye disease, ocular or neurologic trauma and/or surgery. A complete eye examination should be performed, including pupillary reflexes, confrontation visual fields, and ocular motility, specifically noting any constant or intermittent strabismus, ocular suppression, and stereopsis. The anterior segment and retina should be thoroughly examined. If a given patient has combined mechanisms of vision impairment, such as anisometropia, nystagmus, and mild optic nerve hypoplasia, a clinical judgement should be made as to whether that case qualifies as “residual amblyopia.”

Standardized reporting of findings should include the total number of children screened for residual amblyopia, the number referred for confirmatory examination, the number completing confirmatory examination, the number diagnosed with unilateral amblyopia (stratified by subtype), and the number diagnosed with bilateral amblyopia (stratified by subtype). In addition, a “residual amblyopia severity and prevalence metric” could be derived, defined as the number of logMAR lines worse than 0.3 (20/40) in one or both eyes in the total number screened adjusted to a prevalence for 1,000 children.

In summary, we propose standardized guidelines for estimating community-level residual amblyopia prevalence. This metric can be used to assess the results of early childhood amblyopia screening and treatment practices at the population level. It can also be used to track changes within communities over time, in wellresourced communities as well as in areas with limited screening initiatives and/or few pediatric eye specialists. 16 Given racial and ethnic differences in early amblyopia prevalence 13 , 15 and disparate amblyopia treatment outcomes, 11 , 12 standardized collection of data across regions is needed to identify the trends and correlations that permit generalizable conclusions. Data collection should be efficient and simple and should be compatible with existing pediatrician, community, and school-based practices. Comparing the prevalence of residual amblyopia across different populations will help identify gaps in screening and treatment. It will also help in the planning and design of future multicenter clinical trials that test new amblyopia treatments that may be effective beyond the critical period.
