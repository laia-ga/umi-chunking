## Conservative surgical treatment for adenomyosis: New options for looking beyond uterus removal

A B S T R A C T

Adenomyosis is a common benign uterine disorders and patients may present dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) and infertility. The treatment is very complex, including medical, surgical or radiological approaches. Hormonal drugs represent the first line therapy of adenomyosis, highly effective on symptoms and uterine volume reduction. Radiological procedures (UAE and HIFU), RFA and hysteroscopy may be proposed in those cases in which medical therapy is ineffective. Considering surgical treatment, hysterectomy remains the only existing definitive treatment but in the last decades the desire of uterus preservation is becoming more and more diffuse. On the other hand, surgical conservative treatments of adenomyosis are very effective in ameliorating AUB and pelvic pain and in reducing uterine volume, with some post-operative risks and obstetrics complications. Cytoreductive surgery for adenomyosis may be very complex, therefore it should be performed by experienced surgeons in dedicated centers, above all in case of concomitant endometriosis.

## 1. Introduction

Adenomyosis is a common benign uterine disorder characterized by the presence of endometrial glands and stroma into the myometrium [ 1 ]. Macroscopically, the disease may result in a diffusely enlarged uterus or as focal adenomyosis [ 2 ].

Historically, the diagnosis of adenomyosis was typically performed in peri-menopausal women during a histopathological evaluation following hysterectomy for chronic pelvic pain and abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB). More recently, it has also become a condition identified in young fertile-age women thanks to the recent advancements in imaging techniques. Patients affected present dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, AUB and infertility, or may be asymptomatic, in about one third of cases [ 1 , 3 ]. Data from ultrasound units show a prevalence of about 21% of sonographic signs of adenomyosis in the general population, while the percentage is higher in women undergoing assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) [ 1 , 3 ].

## 2. Adenomyosis: the medical treatment

Currently, there are no established guidelines for the treatment of adenomyosis. However, like endometriosis, treatments are used off-label to manage adenomyosis. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and tranexamic acid have limited roles in therapy, mainly providing support for mild symptoms or symptom exacerbation during hormone therapy [ 1 , 3 , 6 , 7 ].

Progestins such as LNG-IUS (levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system) or dienogest are the most used in adenomyosis management, showing improved symptoms and reduced uterine size. Decreased expression of progesterone receptors (PR) B has been observed in adenomyotic tissue, similar to endometriosis. Although adenomyotic tissue has lower levels of PR, a good response to highdose of intrauterine progestin has been shown: LNG-IUS is a highly effective and safe contraceptive approved for continuous use for up to 6 years. The use of LNG-IUS in the treatment of dysmenorrhea and AUB related to adenomyosis could be explained both by the direct effect of LNG on adenomyotic foci and by its modulatory action on altered endometrial factors. The local action of LNG, by downregulating estrogen receptors, induces decidualization and atrophy of the ectopic endometrium, preventing further estrogenic stimulation [ 1 , 3 , 6 , 7 ]. LNG-IUS has been associated with improvements in quality of life comparable to those achieved with more invasive surgical techniques, high rates of symptom relief (pelvic pain and AUB), and minimal local and systemic side effects. It is effective in reducing uterine volume with rapid improvement in vascularity and relief of symptoms. Also Dienogest (DNG) is highly effective in reducing uterine volume and blood flow within the uterus, as well as alleviating symptoms of adenomyosis. Some patients treated may present side effects like vaginal dryness, loss of libido and alopecia. Moreover, DNG has not a complete contraceptive activity. In those cases in which women present severe side effects or need a contraceptive pill, DNG can be combined with 30 μ g of ethinylestradiol [ 1 , 3 , 6 , 7 ]. Considering that progesterone resistance in the adenomyotic endometrium and stroma is typical of adenomyosis, the beneficial effect of progestins may progressively decrease [ 1 , 3 , 6 , 7 ].

Combined oral contraceptives (COC) or progestin only pill (POP) are also used off-label in the management of adenomyosis, to treat the symptoms. They induce a pseudo gestational state, leading to decidualization and subsequent atrophy of the endometrium and adenomyotic lesions. Amenorrhea or reduced menstrual volume cause a relief from dysmenorrhea, providing benefits to patients with adenomyosis [ 1 , 3 , 6 , 7 ].

Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone ant (GnRH-a) are used as second-line treatment for adenomyosis when progestins are not efficacious or not tolerated by the patients. Administration of GnRH-a initially causes a flare-up effect but subsequently suppresses FSH and LH secretion, leading to the blockade of sex steroid production by the ovaries. GnRH-a are used in the treatment of sex steroiddependent uterine disorders such as adenomyosis, reducing uterine volume and severe dysmenorrhea.

Oral Gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonists (GnRH-ants) are peptide compounds acting by blocking GnRH receptors in the pituitary gland, resulting in the immediate suppression of reproductive function, by inhibiting the secretion of FSH and LH by the adenohypophysis. Currently, elagolix, relugolix and linzagolix are being considered for the treatment of endometriosis and uterine leiomyomas. The successful use of linzagolix in patients with adenomyosis has been shown [ 10 ].

## 3. Adenomyosis: the radiological treatment

When medical treatment fails, a spectrum of radiological interventional procedures are proposed, such as uterine artery embolization (UAE) or high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) ( Table 2 ) [ 11 , 12 ]. After many years of positive results reported by randomized controlled trials in the treatment of uterine leiomyoma, multiple case series have been performed investigating UAE as a possible treatment for patients with symptomatic adenomyosis. AUB and dysmenorrhea seem to respond equally well to UAE in the first year after treatment. A post procedure shows uterine volume reduction (50% at 12 months after treatment) and areas of devascularization. Various degrees of necrosis have been speculated to be effective in reducing or eliminating symptoms [ 11 , 12 ].

HIFU is a modality that allows for targeted thermo-coagulation of adenomyotic lesions. In particular, HIFU ablation disables the ectopic endometrial by local “ thermal resection ” of the lesion, thus blocking the release of bleeding and inflammatory mediators caused by its periodic response to ovarian hormones. The potential for therapeutic HIFU to offer an alternative to invasive surgery is less well known. The ability of HIFU to create discrete regions of tissue necrosis only in precisely targeted positions by careful placement of the focus, without the need for any surgical intervention, has made HIFU of interest to those seeking noninvasive alternatives to conventional abdominal surgery. Several studies have shown that the treatment efficacy and recurrence rate after HIFU are related to the type of adenomyosis: HIFU treatment for focal adenomyosis is superior than for diffuse adenomyosis [ 13 ].

HIFU reduces symptoms in these women with a significant improvement of Qol. The blood flow signal within adenomyotic tissue is significantly reduced after treatment, with a reduction of symptoms at 6 months. LNG-IUS can be used after HIFU to improve clinical outcomes [ 14 ]. Available data shows at 2 years of follow-up a significant reduction of menstrual volume and a complete relief of symptoms in 68% of women treated with HIFU [ 15 ].

The safety of HIFU treatment has been demonstrated in many studies. Common severe adverse effects or complications of HIFU treatment for adenomyosis include skin burns and nerve injury. Fertility has always been a main consideration in the development of HIFU treatment for patients with adenomyosis: the treatment is performed under guidance of ultrasound or MRI, so the lesion can be precisely ablated, thereby preserving surrounding myometrium and endometrium [ 16 ].

The first uterine sparing treatments for adenomyosis was introduced in 1952 by Hyams [18] and consisted in adenomyomectomy. Adenomyomectomy is useful for focal adenomyosis, in which adenomyoma is separated from the normal myometrium and excised. Compared to myomectomy, the procedure is more difficult because the plane between adenomyoma and normal myometrium is not well defined [19,20]. A cytoreductive surgery may be useful in the treatment of both focal and diffuse adenomyosis. The procedure consists of an extensive removal of adenomyosis foci with a large amount of healthy myometrium and several techniques have been developed, all with the objective to obtain access to the adenomyotic mass [19,20]. A modification of the technique is that in which a transverse H incision is made on the uterine fundus and the adenomyiotic tissue is removed by cutting it into thin slices. After that, myometrium is sutured in one or two layers and serosal flaps are closed with interrupted subserosal sutures. The procedure may be performed both by laparoscopy and laparotomy [21,22].

The triple-flap method was developed with the aim to introduce a safe procedure with complete cytoreductive surgery for eventual future pregnancies. The uterus is bisected in the mid-sagittal place until the endometrium and then the adenomyotic tissue is completely removed. After that, uterine cavity is sutured (first flap), muscolaris and serosa are closed anterior-posteriorly on one side of the uterus (second flap) and finally the seromuscolar layer of the opposite side is brought to partially cover the second flap (third flap) [19,21,22]. Considering that the suture is very difficult and the removal of adenomyotic tissue needs hand palpation, laparotomy is preferable [22].

A complete cytoreductive surgery alternative to triple-flap method is the asymmetric dissection. It is characterized by a longitudinal uterine incision followed by a diagonal myometrial dissection and a transverse incision to open the uterine cavity. Then, uterine cavity is closed and the uterine reconstruction is done by covering the right side with the left one [22].

Conservative surgery for adenomyosis seems to be effective in ameliorating the associated symptoms, in particular pelvic pain (>70% at 12 months) and AUB (>70% at 12 months), while uterine volume is estimated to be reduced by a factor of 3.1. A difference between the different procedures seems to be not significative [20]. Osada et al. described a uterine rupture rate of 5% during pregnancy in patients that previously underwent adenomyomectomy [15]; variations of abortion, pregnancy complications, placentation abnormalities (e.g. placenta previa and placenta accreta spectrum) are also described, even if without a high prevalence [23].

Uterus sparing surgery of adenomyosis may be suitable for women with multiple fails of medical treatment and desiring to preserve uterus. Considering that several conservative surgical options for adenomyosis are available and no one of these is demonstrated to be superior, treatment should be individualized and based on patient needs and imaging pre-operative evaluation (estimation by pelvic ultrasound and MRI of localization and extent of disease). It should be taken into account that, above all in case of diffuse adenomyosis, surgery should guarantee a good removal of adenomyotic tissue and a good reconstruction of uterine wall. Furthermore, it should be noted that, considering that the removal of adenomyotic tissue is more challenging than conventional myomectomy and that several of the procedures described need very difficult sutures, adenomyosis surgery could be result in a not neglectable risk of uterine rupture during pregnancy [21]. Surgeons should pay attentions to avoid heat denaturation of the incision edges, resulting in an increased risk of suture dehiscence [22].

Moreover, adenomyosis is often associated with pelvic endometriosis, in particular DE, or uterine myomas, that may request a synchronous treatment, often very complex and multidisciplinary [24–26].

## 5. Adenomyosis: radiofrequency ablation

An alternative therapeutic approach for premenopausal adenomyotic patients with symptomatic focal adenomyosis resistant to hormonal treatment is the laparoscopic radiofrequency thermal ablation or radiofrequency ablation (RFA). The procedure is performed during laparoscopy through a radiofrequency needle electrode connected with an extendible prong. The needle tip is inserted inside nodular adenoymosis under laparoscopic guidance with a target temperature of 98 ◦C. After the treatment, the needle track is coagulated with the aim to ensure hemostasis. The electrodes generate heat energy and induce thermal fixation and coagulative necrosis in the target lesion, which preserves the cellular architecture. Secondary to the effects in the target lesion, there are also changes in the periphery. These changes further cause uterus volume shrinkage through ablation-induced tissue contraction and dehydration [27]. These thermal effects also induce changes on a cytomolecular level, with decreased expression of ER and PR [28]. RFA under ultrasound guidance is another valid alternative treatment [29–31].

RFA provides a good reduction in uterine volume, ranging from 41% to 57% in the different studies, with a low risk of severe complications (perforation, infections or thermal injuries of abdominal organs) [30]. More frequently patients report vaginal discharge, abdominal pain and low-grade fever [32]. RFA is associated with clinically meaningful improvement of adenomyosis-related pain in the short term (dysmenorrhea, dyschezia, dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain, bulk symptoms) and AUB, similar to that of conventional uterus-sparing surgeries [17,32,33] and allows for hysterectomy avoidance in several cases [33]. Data about effectiveness on AUB, uterus morphological changes, pregancy and fertility are inconclusive [32].

## 6. Adenomyosis: hysteroscopic treatment

Traditionally, several hysteroscopic patterns were described to be associated with adenomyosis, even if it is difficult to identify the specific role of hysteroscopy in the disease’s diagnosis, also because the technique does not explore the deeper layer of myometrium, that is usually affected [34]. In the last years, several authors have proposed the hysteroscopic treatment of adenomyosis in those cases in which the disease (focal or diffuse) is found close to the endometrium [35], while where the adenomyotic lesions are deeper it is not considered the appropriate approach [34].

In case of focal adenomyosis less than 1.5 cm and close to the endometrial cavity, it may be enucleated by mechanical instruments or bipolar electrodes in an ambulatory setting, similarly to the enucleation of submucosal myomas. Conversely, in case of bigger lesions or diffuse superficial adenomyosis, resectoscopic treatment may be a feasible option. It may be performed in an office setting when a mini-resectoscope is available [35]. Finally, endometrial or endo-myometrial ablation may be performed in women not desiring future pregnancies [35,36].

Even if medical treatment seems to be more effective than hysteroscopy in adenomyosis related symptoms, an improvement of dysmenorrhea and AUB is described in about 50% of treated patients [34].

## 7. Conclusions

Medical, radiologic and surgical conservative treatments of adenomyosis are effective in ameliorating AUB and pelvic pain and in reducing uterine volume. Hormonal drugs are considered the primary approach in women during reproductive life, considering radiologic or conservative surgical treatment only when the medical treatment fail.

Surgical conservative treatment of adenomyosis is very complex and, considering that palpation with fingers may be very important with the aim of finding the boundary between adenomyosis and healthy myometrial tissue, in several cases open surgery may be preferable. On the other hand, literature data suggest that surgical, early post-operative and pregancy complications rates (hematomas, suture anastomotic leakage, adhesions formation) seem to be not so elevated, either after laparoscopy and laparotomy. In this optic, several additional techniques such as temporary occlusion or ligation of uterine artery, vasopressin or oxytocin intramyometrial injection and others were proposed with the aim of reducing the intra-operative blood loss and may be an option in some cases. The data about complications of conservative surgical treatment of adenomyosis may be underestimated mainly because all the techniques described are reported from excellence centers with very high surgical experience (limited surgical bleeding, goods surgical knots, reduced risk of bowel and urinary damage). Moreover, adenomyosis is often associated with pelvic endometriosis (in particular DE) and uterine myomas, requesting a synchronous multidisciplinary treatment. Thus, some authors speculated that these results are not applicable in a general gynaecological setting and limited experience surgeons should not replicate these complex procedures. We can conclude that conservative surgical treatment for adenomyosis should be performed by experienced surgeons in dedicated centers, above all in case of laparoscopic approach and concomitant endometriosis. RFA and hysteroscopy may represent, in selected cases, an alternative for premenopausal women with symptomatic focal adenomyosis resistant to hormonal treatment.

Practice Points.

- ● Hormonal and non-hormonal treatments are the first approach to manage adenomyosis. Progestins (LNG-IUS, dienogest), GnRH-a or oral GnRH-ants represents the first line therapy, very effective on symptoms and uterine volume reduction.
- ● Radiological procedures (UAE and HIFU), RFA and hysteroscopy are valid alternatives in those cases in which medical treatment fails.
- ● Surgical conservative treatments of adenomyosis are effective in ameliorating AUB and pelvic pain and in reducing uterine volume in those cases in which medical treatment fails. These procedures may be associated with obstetric complications in limited cases (e.g. abortion, pregnancy complications, placentation abnormalities).
- ● Traditionally, surgical therapy of adenomyosis is hysterectomy, the only existing definitive treatment in premenopausal patients who do not wish to preserve their uterus.
- ● Considering that all conservative surgical treatments are often technically complex and adenomyosis is frequently associated with endometriosis, they should be performed by experienced surgeons in dedicated centers.

Research agenda.

- • Comparing the different surgical strategies in terms of outcomes, complications and obstetrical outcomes;
- • Evaluating the long-term effect of conservative surgical treatment for adenomyosis, above all in term of abnormal uterine bleeding and pelvic pain;
- • Comparing the outcomes of conservative surgical strategies for adenomyosis and medical treatment, in particular the new GnRH antagonists;
- • Comparing the outcomes of conservative surgical strategies for adenomyosis and radiological procedures, in particular Radiofrequency Ablation.
