Best Practice Guidelines for Neuromodulation in Pain Management: Insight From the Neuromodulation Society of Australia and New Zealand

Tim Ho, MBBS, MSc 1 ; Michelle O ’ Brien, MBBS, M.Med. 2 ; Richard Sullivan, MBChB 3 ; Jane Standen, MBBS 4 ; Andrew D. H. Weiss, MD, MSc 4 ; Dan Bates, BMed, BSc 5 ; John Salmon, MBBS 6 ; Nick Christelis, MBBCh 7 ; James Yu, MD 8 ; Murray Taverner, MBBS 9 ; Marc Russo, MBBS DA(UK) 10

Objectives: The Neuromodulation Society of Australia and New Zealand (NSANZ) developed evidence-based best practice guidelines for spinal cord stimulation (SCS) in chronic pain management. The primary objective was to provide clear guidance to support evidence-informed care across Australia and New Zealand, with a focus on patient selection, procedural techniques, and complication management.

Materials and Methods: A systematic literature review was conducted using Medline, EMBASE, Trip Pro, and Google Scholar, covering publications from 2010 to July 2024. A total of 283 studies and relevant clinical guidelines were included on the basis of level 1B evidence or strong expert consensus, consistent with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation criteria. Clinical questions were developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome framework, and a Delphi process was used to achieve consensus among NSANZ board members.

Results: The guidelines provide comprehensive recommendations on patient selection, preparation, procedural techniques, postimplantation care, and complication management. Additional guidance is included on training standards, quality improvement processes, and the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration. Key recommendations emphasize optimizing patient selection, enhancing procedural safety, and improving treatment outcomes.

Conclusions: These guidelines offer practical, evidence-informed recommendations for the safe and effective use of SCS in chronic pain management. By promoting consistency, transparency, and collaborative care, they aim to guide clinical decisionmaking and support the delivery of evidence-informed neuromodulation services across Australia and New Zealand.

# INTRODUCTION

Neuromodulation has transformed the management of chronic pain, offering relief to patients with limited options after conventional therapies have failed. As the field advances, the increasing complexity of devices, techniques, and patient populations underscores the need for standardized practices to ensure safety, ef ficacy, and equitable access. Among neuromodulation modalities, spinal cord stimulation (SCS) has shown promise in treating neuropathic pain and other challenging chronic pain conditions. However, variability in patient selection, procedural approaches, and postimplantation care has led to inconsistent outcomes.

In response to these challenges, the Neuromodulation Society of Australia and New Zealand (NSANZ) has developed a guideline to provide clinicians with evidence-based recommendations that optimize patient outcomes and minimize complications. The guideline covers critical areas such as patient selection criteria, procedural protocols, training requirements, and management of complications, bridging gaps in clinical practice with current evidence and expert consensus. ## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search Strategy

A systematic review of the literature on SCS was conducted to inform guideline development. The search, designed with the assistance of a medical librarian, covered multiple data bases including Medline (through OVID), EMBASE, Trip Pro, and Google Scholar. Search terms included combinations of “ spinal cord stimulation, ” “ indications, ” “ complications, ” “ ef ficacy, ” and “ guidelines, ” using Boolean operators, wildcards, and MeSH terms to ensure comprehensiveness. Studies from 2010 to 2024 were included, with an updated search performed on July 12, 2024. No language restrictions were applied.

Selection Process

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, with con flicts resolved by a third. Full-text reviews were conducted for studies meeting the inclusion criteria. A total of 1201 citations were identi fied, and after screening, 283 studies were included. Additional guidelines were identi fied through manual searches. The process was summarized according to standard methods ( Fig. 1 ). 1

Eligibility Criteria

Included studies provided at least level 1B evidence (randomized controlled trials [RCTs] with narrow con fidence intervals) or strong recommendations based on expert consensus, consistent with Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations criteria. 2 Position statements from recognized organizations, such as the Australia and New Zealand Faculty of Medicine, also were considered.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Key clinical questions were developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome framework. Data were extracted and synthesized to generate evidence-based recommendations. Con flicting recommendations were resolved through a Delphi process facilitated by the NSANZ board.

NSANZ Recommendations for Best Practice

Patient Selection

Pain Phenotypes Suitable for Neuromodulation. NSANZ recommends SCS for appropriately selected patients with refractory neuropathic or mixed pain in whom adequate pain relief or functional improvement has not been achieved despite comprehensive multidisciplinary management ( Table 1 ).

Indications

SCS is recommended for patients with chronic, refractory pain unresponsive to standard medical therapies ( Table 2 ). NSANZ emphasizes that patient selection and adherence to de fined criteria are critical for optimizing outcomes. 6

Contraindications

SCS is contraindicated when the risks outweigh potential benefits ( Table 3 ). NSANZ emphasizes individualized assessment, interdisciplinary input, and shared decision-making to address modi fiable factors and optimize outcomes. 6 – 8

Target and SCS/Peripheral Nerve Stimulation Modality Selection Targets for SCS and Peripheral Nerve Stimulation. The entire accessible length of the spinal cord, from C2 to the conus, offers potential targets for SCS based on the pain mechanism, whether neuropathic or nociplastic ( Table 4 ). 4 NSANZ emphasizes individualized targeting to maximize therapeutic outcomes and minimize risks.

Pain Phenotype, Indication, and Stimulation Target with Limited Evidence

NSANZ acknowledges that certain pain phenotypes, clinical indications, and stimulation targets may currently lack robust supporting evidence. In such cases, treatment may be considered on the basis of expert opinion, incorporating multidisciplinary input, clinical need, the absence of viable alternatives, and comprehensive informed consent. This process should include clear documentation of the risk-bene fit rationale. Such an approach supports transparent shared decision-making and promotes patient-centered care.

Cervical Neurostimulation

NSANZ supports the use of cervical SCS in carefully selected cases such as cervical radicular pain, upper extremity complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), cervical axial pain, and persistent spinal pain syndrome (cervical), on the basis of evidence indicating moderate bene fit. 13 , 14 Optimal lead placement is between C2 and C6, guided by imaging and precise surgical techniques. 13 , 15 NSANZ supports epidural entry at C7 – T4 or T11 – L1 to ensure safe lead navigation to cervical targets while considering anatomical factors. 11

DRG Stimulation

NSANZ recommends DRG stimulation as an advanced neuromodulation technique for managing focal neuropathic pain, particularly in conditions such as CRPS and persistent spinal pain syndrome (PSPS). 22 , 23 Some evidence suggests DRG stimulation offers superior ef ficacy for focal neuropathic pain to that of traditional SCS, with sustained long-term bene fits. 22 , 24

The procedure carries speci fic risks, including implantable pulse generator (IPG) site pain, lead migration, fracture, infection, and a higher risk of vascular and neurologic injury in cervical and thoracic placements. Careful patient selection and procedural planning are critical to minimizing complications. 22 , 25 NSANZ emphasizes the importance of specialized training, including accredited fellowships, hands-on workshops, and supervised practice, to ensure practitioner competence. 26 NSANZ advises that DRG lead placement above T10 up to a maximum of the C6 level should be undertaken only by clinicians with recognized expertise in DRG stimulation. Such procedures should be supported by multidisciplinary input, comprehensive informed consent, and clear documentation of the clinical rationale and risk-bene fit assessment. 27

Timing and Context of Delivery

Duration of Conservative Management Before Neurostimulation

NSANZ recommends a trial of conservative management with a multimodal approach within a multidisciplinary framework, typically six to 12 months, before considering neurostimulation, with the duration tailored to the condition and patient response. This period helps identify patients whose conditions are truly refractory to standard therapies and more likely to bene fit from neurostimulation. Prolonged conservative treatment without meaningful improvement may delay effective interventions, particularly for conditions such as PSPS and CRPS, when early neurostimulation can reduce pain and opioid use. Although noninvasive treatments may bene fit some patients, timely neurostimulation should be considered for those with refractory pain to optimize outcomes and minimize opioid dependence. 28

Patient Engagement and Neurostimulation Outcomes

NSANZ emphasizes the importance of patient engagement for successful neurostimulation. Candidates should indicate adequate cognitive ability, realistic expectations, and motivation to participate in postimplantation rehabilitation. 4,29 Patients unable to actively engage in their care, such as those with significant cognitive impairment, should not undergo implantation. Engagement in follow-up appointments, adherence to prescribed therapies, and participation in psychosocial support are essential for optimizing outcomes and managing complications. A strong support system further enhances patient engagement and therapeutic success.

Opioid Rationalization and SCS Implantation

NSANZ recommends the rationalization of opioid use in managing chronic noncancer pain, in line with the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists (ANZCA) Faculty of Pain Medicine ’ s guidance. Speci fically, before SCS trial/implant, rotation to atypical opioids (tapentadol and/or buprenorphine) is advisable, and if opioid intolerant, a dose of < 90 mg oral morphine equivalent daily dose (OMEDD) is preferred. Evidence supports weaning patients off opioids before SCS implantation to improve long-term outcomes and reduce the risk of late device failure. 28 , 30 Higher opioid doses are associated with reduced SCS success, likely owing to opioid-induced hyperalgesia impairing neuromodulation ef ficacy. Atypical opioids such as tapentadol and buprenorphine have a lower risk for opioid-induced hyperalgesia. Studies such as that by Nissen et al show better pain control and lower failure rates in patients weaned off opioids before implantation. 28 A multimodal approach incorporating behavioral therapies, physical rehabilitation, and nonopioid pharmacotherapy is recommended to facilitate tapering, minimize withdrawal symptoms, and prepare patients for SCS.

Appropriate Context for Delivery: A Sociopsychobiomedical Model

NSANZ recommends a multidisciplinary approach for neuromodulation, integrating expertise from surgical and nonsurgical specialists. Teams should have competencies in patient selection, implantation, and follow-up, supported by a robust on-call system to manage complications. Arrangements for urgent neurosurgical or spinal referrals should be in place, and teams must maintain suf ficient caseloads to retain skills. Continuous professional development, peer review, and outcome assessment are critical for delivering high-quality care. 31 , 32 Effective communication and collaboration among team members are vital to ensuring optimal neuromodulation delivery. 32

Comprehensive psychiatric and social assessments are essential when evaluating candidates for neuromodulation. Stable mental health and adequate social support are critical for adherence and success. Unstable psychiatric conditions, poor coping, or unresolved social issues such as ongoing medical litigation should be addressed before proceeding because they negatively affect SCS outcomes. 5 , 18

Excess weight, smoking, and poor physical conditioning can impair neurostimulation outcomes by increasing risks such as infection, lead migration, and poor wound healing. NSANZ recommends lifestyle modi fications, including smoking cessation, weight loss, and physical therapy, to improve surgical success, reduce complications, and enhance functional outcomes, particularly for conditions such as low back pain. Further research is needed to establish preoperative protocols incorporating these interventions. 28 , 29

SCS Trial

Predicting Long-Term Success of SCS

NSANZ recommends that the success of an SCS trial, de fined as > 50% pain reduction on the numeric rating scale or visual analog scale and improved function, be used as a key predictor of longterm outcomes with permanent implantation. Patient-reported quality of life and activities of daily living should complement objective measures in evaluating trial success. Functional improvements should be assessed independently, focusing on mobility, endurance, and other relevant tasks. 7

In addition to trial success, lower baseline opioid use and favorable psychosocial factors are critical predictors of long-term success. Patients on opioid doses < 90 mg OMEDD are more likely to experience better outcomes. 30 NSANZ recommends reducing opioid use before SCS trials when feasible to enhance therapy effectiveness.

Optimal Duration of SCS Trials

NSANZ recommends an SCS trial duration of five to seven days, providing suf ficient time to evaluate pain relief and functional improvements across daily activities. Trials > ten days increase the risk of complications, particularly infections, without added predictive value. A five-to-seven-day trial offers a balance between thorough assessment and patient safety, serving as an effective predictor of long-term success. Clinicians should individualize trial durations on the basis of patient needs while maintaining a focus on safety and ef ficacy. 33

Trial Vs Direct SCS Implantation

NSANZ maintains a trialfirst principle, given that screening trials have traditionally been recommended to assess patient response. However, NSANZ acknowledges that recent level 1B evidence from the UK-based TRIAL-STIM study shows no signi ficant difference in long-term outcomes between trialfirst and direct implantation. Therefore, NSANZ endorses that for suitable patients, particularly when multidisciplinary assessment strongly supports the likelihood of bene fit, SCS implantation may be considered without a prior trial, if the implanter has an implant-to-trial ratio > 80% and has performed > 100 implants. 34 , 35

Preoperative Staphylococcus aureus Screening and Decolonization

Staphylococcus aureus , often of endogenous origin, is the leading cause of surgical site infections in orthopedic and neurosurgical procedures. 39 Decolonization protocols involving nasal mupirocin and chlorhexidine baths have been shown to effectively reduce surgical site infections, particularly deep infections in implanted devices.

NSANZ recommends that patients who undergo SCS trials or implantation be screened for S aureus colonization. Practitioners should adopt a decolonization protocol for carriers and seek guidance from infectious disease specialists for cases involving mupirocin resistance or allergies to mupirocin or chlorhexidine, ensuring effective and tailored infection prevention strategies. 39

Surgical Placement of Paddle Lead

NSANZ recommends paddle leads for SCS in cases requiring enhanced stability or when percutaneous access is unsuitable. Although percutaneous leads are associated with fewer complications and reduced need for revisions, paddle leads offer superior ef ficacy and reduced migration risk in appropriate cases. 44 Placement of paddle leads requires advanced surgical skills and should be performed by a trained neurosurgeon. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is essential for precise planning, and meticulous technique, including proper anchoring, is critical to minimize complications. 13 , 45

There is an absence of trials producing level 1 evidence speci fic to the use of implanted neuromodulation devices for pain to inform these guidelines. Antibiotic Prophylaxis

The primary pathogens associated with SCS-related infections are S aureus and S epidermidis . Evidence supports cefazolin as the first-line antibiotic for surgical infection prevention. In cases of resistance, alternatives such as clindamycin, vancomycin, or teicoplanin may be used on the basis of local resistance patterns, with guidance from infectious disease specialists. Antibiotics should be administered within 60 minutes before skin incision, or 120 minutes prior when using vancomycin for mono-prophylaxis. 39

NSANZ endorses the Neurostimulation Appropriateness Consensus Committee Recommendations for InfectionManagement Practices, recognizing the extrapolation of evidence from other surgical contexts to epidural neuromodulatory leads and SCS IPG placement ( Table 8 ). 39

Bleeding and Coagulation Management

SCS procedures carry a moderate-to-high risk of bleeding, particularly during lead placement or removal, owing to the proximity of the epidural venous plexuses and the use of large-gauge needles. NSANZ emphasizes the importance of effective anticoagulation management to minimize complications, such as epidural hematomas, and ensure safe practices 32 , 38 ( Table 9 ).

These strategies aim to ensure safe SCS practices by minimizing bleeding risks while addressing the cardiovascular needs of patients on anticoagulants.

Epidural hematomas are a rare but serious complication of SCS procedures that require urgent attention. NSANZ underscores the importance of early recognition and immediate action to prevent permanent neurologic damage. Diagnosis should be confirmed promptly with MRI to assess the extent of the hematoma and guide intervention. Timely surgical decompression, ideally within 24 hours of symptomonset, is critical to preserving neurologic function and ensuring favorable patient outcomes. 32

Complications

Complications associated with SCS are categorized as early, occurring during or shortly after implantation, and delayed, which may arise weeks to months later. Most complications occur within the first six months after implantation or revision. 46 Hardware complications, such as lead migration or device malfunctions, are more common than biological issues such as infections or dural breaches. 37 , 45 – 50

These complications can signi ficantly affect patient quality of life and increase healthcare costs, underscoring the need for effective prevention and management strategies. 46 However, advancements in technology and improved surgical techniques have contributed to a decreasing incidence of complications. 51 , 52

NSANZ emphasizes the importance of understanding, preventing, and managing complications to ensure patient safety and optimize the long-term success of SCS therapy.

Delayed Complications

Delayed complications of SCS, such as infection, lead migration, and device-related issues, can signi ficantly affect long-term outcomes. NSANZ emphasizes the importance of preventive strategies, timely recognition, and effective management to minimize risks and maintain therapeutic ef ficacy ( Table 11 ).

Infection Management. Infection is a signi ficant complication of SCS, with overall rates ranging from 2% to 4% and deep infections occurring in 1% to 2.5% of cases. 56 , 57 NSANZ emphasizes timely recognition, accurate diagnosis, and effective management to minimize risks and optimize patient outcomes ( Table 12 ).

Physical examination alone may not reliably differentiate between super ficial and deep infections in patients with SCS. NSANZ recommends the use of blood tests and imaging, such as computed tomography or MRI, to con firm deep infections and detect complications like epidural abscesses. 36 , 56 , 57

In cases requiring device removal, reimplantation should be deferred for a minimum of 12 weeks to ensure complete resolution of the infection. The new device should be implanted on the contralateral side to minimize the risk of recurrence. 36

Long-Term Complications

Long-term complications of SCS include tolerance, when stimulation becomes less effective, and changes in pain perception over time. Regular follow-ups and device reprogramming are essential to address these challenges and maintain therapeutic ef ficacy. 58 NSANZ emphasizes a proactive approach, including regular follow-ups, patient/caregiver education, and device adjustments, to minimize the impact of long-term complications and optimize treatment outcomes.

Physical Therapy and Movement After SCS

Physiotherapy after SCS implantation offers potential bene fits in pain relief, mobility, and quality of life. However, there is no formal expert consensus or high-quality evidence directly comparing structured physiotherapy with standard postoperative care. 59

NSANZ recommends a multimodal rehabilitation approach combining physical therapy focused on safe mobilization, motor control, and cognitive-behavioral techniques to optimize functional improvement. Patient counseling is essential to manage expectations, encourage a gradual return to activity, and minimize risks such as lead migration or hardware complications. 60

Return-to-work outcomes after SCS remain suboptimal, with only 9.5% to 14% of patients successfully resuming work. Personalized rehabilitation programs addressing both physical and psychosocial factors may improve these outcomes. Ongoing research, including the OPERA RCT, is expected to inform evidence-based post-SCS rehabilitation protocols, particularly for patients with PSPS type 2. 61

Appropriate Training and Mentorship

NSANZ recommends structured mentorship during fellowship or societal engagement to ensure competency in neuromodulation. Training should include patient-centered care (including cultural sensitivity), patient selection, implantation techniques, device knowledge, and complication management. 31 , 42 Comprehensive education should cover anatomy, imaging, procedural planning, troubleshooting, and postimplantation care. Trainees are encouraged to supplement fellowship with societal programs, online courses, cadaver workshops, and training. 42 Developing these skills through mentorship and structured training is essential before independently performing SCS procedures to ensure safety and optimal outcomes. 31

Avoiding Poor Clinical Practice

NSANZ emphasizes that SCS must adhere to relevant legislation, ethical codes, and professional standards set by governing entities, medical boards, and colleges. 72 Clinicians performing SCS procedures must have appropriate training, maintain accreditation and

Poor clinical practice arises from several factors, including inadequate training, which is a primary concern. Only clinicians meeting endorsed training standards should perform SCS procedures. 29 Noncompliance with ethical or professional standards, failure to participate in peer evaluation, neglect of surgical skill maintenance, and lack of outcome audits undermine patient care and safety. Audits should ideally involve registries but also may include individual practice reviews in the absence of a registry. 29 , 74

NSANZ recommends that SCS procedures be performed only in clinical settings that uphold sound medical practices. Exceptions are made for ethically approved research trials involving emerging indications or new devices. 29

Ethical and Legal Considerations

NSANZ emphasizes that practitioners in Australia and New Zealand must adhere to the principles of the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency Code of Conduct for Doctors and the Medical Council of New Zealand Good Medical Practice. 72 , 75 Clinicians must practice within their scope of competence, maintain skills and knowledge for safe care, and balance bene fit versus harm in evidence-informed clinical decisions. Accurate recordkeeping, clear communication, and transparency about risks and alternatives are essential components of a shared decision-making approach.

## CONCLUSIONS

NSANZ guidelines standardize neuromodulation practices, focusing on patient selection, training, procedures, and follow-up to improve outcomes. These evidence-based recommendations provide a foundation for advancing neuromodulation and meeting patient needs across Australia and New Zealand.
