This plenary discussion centered on the recently published AUA microscopic hematuria (MH) guideline revision. 1 Dr Barocas opened the session with an overview of the evolution of the MH guidelines starting with the initial 2012 publication. At that time, the guideline contained nonrisk-adapted recommendations to evaluate all patients over 35 years old with any degree of MH with cystoscopy and CT urogram. Because MH has a high prevalence with a low incidence of underlying malignancy, this approach was expensive with a low diagnostic yield. As a result, risk stratification was introduced in the 2020 AUA MH guidelines as a tool to triage MH patients and tailor the intensity of evaluation to the risk of malignancy. The 2025 guideline update sought to further refine the existing guidelines based on interval published evidence.

Dr Barocas then reviewed the revised risk stratification system shown in the Table. Two notable changes were pointed out. The first was that the low-risk group was renamed low/ negligible risk based on the exceptionally low incidence of malignancy in that cohort (0%-0.4%). The other notable modification was a change in classification by age for women due to the lower risk of malignancy in women. Women < 60 years old with no risk factors are now classified as low/negligible risk, while women 60 years and older are classified as intermediate risk in the absence of other high-risk factors. Each panelist then walked through an index patient for each risk group emphasizing what changed in the evaluation and management algorithm in the revised guidelines (Figure).

###### Low/Negligible Risk

First, Dr Downs reviewed the management of the low-/negligiblerisk patient in 2025 and emphasized the exceedingly low likelihood of underlying cancer detection in this group. Across the recently published Sancı et al, 2 Saxon et al, 3 and Woldu et al 4 studies, the incidence of cancer detection in the 2020 low-risk group was 0.0% to 0.4%. Therefore, the revised guidelines now recommend a repeat urinalysis (UA) within 6 months, whereas previously the recommendation was to engage in shared decision-making as to whether to repeat a UA or immediately proceed with cystoscopy and renal ultrasound. Similar to the prior guidelines, those individuals with persistent MH on repeat UA should then be offered an appropriate risk-based evaluation.

###### Intermediate Risk

Next, Dr Boorjian discussed the intermediate-risk patient. He emphasized that cystoscopy and renal ultrasound remain the recommended evaluation for intermediate-risk patients. However, in appropriately counseled intermediate-risk patients who want to avoid cystoscopy and accept the risk of forgoing direct visualization, clinicians may offer urine cytology or urine-based tumor markers (UBTMs). The rationale for incorporating UBTMs only in the intermediate-risk group was explained by demonstrating how the test results change the posttest probability of malignancy and impact clinical management.

For example, the risk of an intermediate-risk patient having cancer is approximately 1% (0.2%- 3.1%). If an intermediate-risk patient has a negative UBTM/cytology (a test with a high negative predictive value [NPV]), then the chances of detecting cancer drop to 0.1% to 0.4%, similar to the low-/ negligiblerisk risk group. Therefore, such a patient may reasonably avoid cystoscopy. Evidence supporting this recommendation was drawn from studies where MH patients represented at least 25% of the total cohort and there were at least 100 MH patients in the cohort. 1 The highest level of evidence was from the recently published randomized control trial comparing a marker-based approach with Cxbladder Triage vs routine cystoscopy to evaluate MH. 5 Cxbladder Triage had an NPV of 99% and the marker-based approach reduced cystoscopy use by 59%. Several other UBTMs and urine cytology also had reported NPV of 95% to 100% in the target population and are included in the new guideline as options as well. Importantly, patients with intermediate-risk MH who forgo cystoscopy based on marker results should have a repeat UA within 12 months and undergo cystoscopy if MH persists.

###### High Risk

Next, Dr Westerman reviewed the management for high-risk patients and appropriate upper tract imaging selection. While the diagnostic yield of CT imaging appears low in a large cohort study, 6 high-risk MH patients are reported to have increased rates of upper tract cancer in recent cohort studies. 3,7 Thus, for high-risk patients, multiphasic CT remains the gold standard for evaluation because it allows for visualization of both the renal parenchyma and urothelium. It was also highlighted that there are very seldom absolute contraindications to CT urogram as newer studies report a negligible risk of contrast-induced nephropathy even at estimated glomerular filtration rate < 45.

Finally, modification to the recommended follow-up for patients who have a negative MH evaluation was discussed. Most patients who have an appropriate risk-stratified negative hematuria evaluation do not require ongoing urologic monitoring and may be safely discharged from the urology practice after shared decision-making and discussion of the best available evidence. Dr Westerman highlighted recent stu dies showing that a history of MH does not increase an individual's risk for subsequent cancer development. 8 Likewise, recent studies show a negligible incidence of subsequent cancer diagnoses among MH patients, and those that are found are all more than 3 years from the negative evaluation. 9 These data should thereby be used to inform the shared decision-making with patients regarding whether or not to repeat a UA and if so, at what time point. ###### Key Takeaways

- • Low-risk group renamed low/ negligible to highlight minimal risk of malignancy and support repeat UA rather than immediate evaluation in this group
- • In the absence of high-risk features, women should not be categorized as high risk based on age alone
- • UBTMs or cytology may be utilized for further risk stratification of intermediate-risk patients who wish to avoid cystoscopy
- • UBTMs/cytology should not be used in low-/negligibleor highrisk patients to inform decision for cystoscopy
- • Following a negative MH evaluation, shared decision-making regarding need for and timing of a repeat UA should occur

Countless patients are diagnosed as “prostatitis” or “epididymitis” and given repeated doses of antibiotics, despite negative cultures and no improvement in pain. Scrotal pain, for instance, represents up to 2.5% of all outpatient urology visits and 55 million health care dollars per year. 1,2 Thankfully, a new AUA guideline has been developed to address these challenging patients.

###### History and Review of Symptoms

CP/CPPS and CSCP are diagnoses of exclusion, so other pathology must be ruled out. It is critical that not only pain and urinary symptoms be queried but also sexual and bowel symptoms. For example, pelvic floor myalgia (PFM) or dysfunction is very common in CPPS patients; pain with ejaculation is a clear indication of PFM. Questionnaires such as the Genitourinary Pain Index or the Chronic Orchialgia Symptom Index may be useful for CPPS and CSCP patients, respectively.

A review of symptoms should assess for other chronic pain conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia, or chronic fatigue syndrome. Neurological problems such as a herniated vertebral disc may contribute to the pain. Musculoskeletal issues, for example from the hip, may be associated with pain or PFM.

###### Treatments

###### Medications

Oral treatments can include α blockers, which are best used in men with both pain and obstructive voiding symptoms. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications are best used as part of multimodal therapy. Medications for neuropathic pain are an important part of treatment. This includes tricyclic antidepressant medications and either gabapentin or pregabalin. Additionally, in CPPS patients (with or without erectile dysfunction) daily tadalafil improves urinary symptoms, QOL, and may reduce pain. 6 Phytotherapeutics including saw palmetto, quercetin, and pollen extract have been studied and may be useful.

###### Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

In men with PFM or abdominopelvic muscle myalgia, a referral to a physical therapist with specialized training related to the pelvic floor can be very helpful. The physical therapist, as part of multimodal care, offers diagnostic confirmation as well as therapeutic treatments such as myofascial release and biofeedback to lower resting pelvic muscle tone.

###### Stress Management

A multimodal approach utilizing psychiatrists for cognitive behavior therapy and the development of coping skills for stress management can help limit exacerbation of these patients’ symptoms. Relaxation techniques, such as guided meditation or diaphragmatic breathing exercises, are also useful.

###### Acupuncture

Acupuncture is well studied and one of the oldest medical modalities. Several studies including a recent large multicenter sham-controlled trial performed in China showed a reduction of ≥ 6 in total NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index at week 8 and week 32 in 60.6% and 61.5% of men, respectively, while the corresponding sham response rates were 36.8% and 38.3%, demonstrating a significant positive effect for acupuncture. 7

###### Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy

As part of the guidelines, a metanalysis of 11 extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) trials was performed. Heterogenous techniques of applying lowintensity ESWL over a period of 2 to 12 weeks demonstrated improvements in the NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index, pain, and urinary symptoms in trials outside the US. 8 ESWL is not readily available and the indication for CPPS remains off label, though further study is clearly warranted.

###### Surgical Treatments for CSCP

In patients with isolated orchialgia, a spermatic cord block is a diagnostic maneuver. Pain relief is temporary, but a good response indicates potential success with a microsurgical denervation of the spermatic cord (MSDSC). Success rates for MSDSC vary in trials, but complete or significant pain relief ranges from 77% to 100%. 9

Initially, MSDSC involved ligation of all structures except the testicular artery and a few lymphatic vessels. Parekattil et al 10 performed biopsies of the spermatic cord demonstrating Wallerian degeneration of small fiber nerves in 3 predominant areas: cremasteric muscle fibers, the peri-vasal sheath, and the lipomatous area in the posterior spermatic cord. 10 Based on this data, a targeted approach has been developed preserving the vas deferens and the lymphatic vessels by dividing only these particular areas.

##### The Problem

In the US, ureteroscopy is the most common procedure for treating upper urinary tract stones. 1 Upon completion of ureteroscopy, the urologist may elect to place a temporary ureteral stent, which can help facilitate kidney drainage and prevent pain from ureteral trauma or obstructing fragments. However, ureteral stents may also result in morbidity to the patient. In Michigan, through our work in the Michigan Urological Surgery Improvement Collaborative (MUSIC), we found that stent placement increased the odds of a postoperative emergency department visit after ureteroscopy by 25%. Many of these visits were for modifiable symptoms such as pain, urinary discomfort, or hematuria. 2

###### What Does a High-Quality Trial Mean?

Surgical clinical trials face several challenges in integrating patient-centered approaches and ensuring real-world applicability. A major barrier is that only a minority of patients agree to take part in RCTs. To better understand patient perspectives on ureteral stents and clinical trial design, we engaged with patient advocates in MUSIC. Consistent with prior studies, only 1 in 3 of our advocates stated they would agree to randomization in any future trial of stent omission. When outcomes from those who decline randomization are not captured, it introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of findings. 5 Additionally, many trials fail to systematically assess patient-reported outcomes and impact on health-related quality of life, or capture the complete spectrum of postintervention health care utilization. 6,7

###### What Is the Solution?

To address these limitations, MUSIC launched the Stent Omission After Ureteroscopy and Lithotripsy (SOUL) trial on June 1, 2023. 8 Funded by PCORI (the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute), SOUL is a single-blinded, pragmatic, multicenter combined RCT and observational study comparing stent placement vs omission after uncomplicated ureteroscopy for patients undergoing upper urinary tract stone surgery. Our hypothesis is that stent omission is associated with improvements in health-related quality of life and 30-day health care utilization. Furthermore, through a mixed methods approach, participating surgeons and patients are interviewed and surveyed about their attitudes and preferences around stenting and stent omission. This has never been done before.

###### How Can You Facilitate Stent Omission in Your Practice?

The key to successful stent omission is a comprehensive approach that begins with patient selection and extends through the entire care process. Ideal candidates for stent omission include non-pre stented patients with stones ≤ 10 mm regardless of location, no mechanical ureteral dilation > 12F, or ureteral access sheath used.

Effective counseling is the cornerstone of the patient’s health care experience. MUSIC has developed standardized patient education materials and multimodal pain management pathways designed to set expectations and promote effective postoperative symptom control (visit www.stentornot.com).

###### What Do We Hope to Achieve?

SOUL seeks to provide patient-centered outcome data on stent vs no stent after uncomplicated ureteroscopy and support shared decision-making between patients and urologists. We are excited about this work, and thank our participating sites, patient champions, and advisory board for their partnership. The introduction of aspiration devices over the past few years has provided urologists a fascinating opportunity to render patients stone free in a revolutionizing way. Aspiration devices utilize suction through existing devices that have been augmented. Specifically, the channels of devices that were intended to permit passage of devices into the kidney (ureteroscopes, wires, lasers) are now being used to aspirate out of the kidney. There are currently 3 classes of devices that have become available to urologists.

##### Direct In-Scope Suction

Direct in-scope suction (DISS) is a modification of existing ureteroscopes (semirigid or flexible) where two 3-way stopcock adapters have been added to the irrigation channel. 1 This completely versatile attachment can be connected to any scope working inlet. Standard operating room wall suction tubing is connected to one port and standard operating room irrigation tubing is connected to the other port for irrigation. The suction tubing is connected to wall suction or portable suction, while irrigation can use pressure bags or continuous irrigation systems. The addition of these stopcocks permits alternating aspiration during or after laser lithotripsy using the scope as a conduit to remove the fragments or dust. Gauhar et al 1 demonstrated in their study that DISS technique was feasible with an acceptable rate of re-treatment compared with suction ureteral access sheaths. Their review highlighted that retrograde intrarenal surgery with DISS was possible even with larger stone

###### Flexible and Navigable Suction Ureteral Access Sheath

The flexible and navigable (FANS) suction ureteral access sheath approach represents another advancement in vacuumassisted technology for stone removal. This technique employs a flexible vacuum-assisted ureteral access sheath that can be directed into various calyces to directly suction stone fragments and dust. Huang et al 2 demonstrated in their study that the vacuum-assisted dusting lithotripsy technique significantly improved stone-free rates compared with traditional flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy. Their research showed immediate stonefree rates of 78.6% vs 50.5% for traditional methods, with follow-up stone-free rates of 94.2% vs 75.7%. These impressive outcomes were consistent across dif ferent stone sizes, including challenging 2- to 3-cm stones. Similarly, Zeng et al 3 described a modified access sheath for continuous flow ureteroscopic lithotripsy where an oblique suction-evacuation port with pressureregulating mechanism was added to allow active egress of irrigation fluid and stone fragments. This approach created a continuous flow where irrigation coming out was immediately aspirated through the sheath, providing a clearer visual field and potentially reducing intrarenal pressure, which may decrease infectious complications. Recent advances in this technology continue to show promise for addressing complex stone cases that might otherwise require more invasive approaches.

###### Comparison With Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy

When comparing these novel aspiration technologies with traditional percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), several advantages become apparent. The primary benefit is the minimally invasive nature of these techniques, which significantly reduces morbidity compared with PCNL. While effective for large stone burdens, PCNL carries the risk of bleeding, injury to surrounding organs, and longer hospital stays. Stern et al 5 demonstrated that SURE could effectively treat large stone burdens that would traditionally require PCNL, with 90.5% of patients avoiding previously planned staged procedures. The study showed stone clearance ranging from 93.8% to 98.9% regardless of stone size, with no device-related complications and no postoperative admissions. Similarly, Huang et al 2 showed that FANS could achieve immediate stone-free rates of 57.1% for stones 2 to 3 cm in size, challenging the traditional recommendation for PCNL in stones > 2 cm. While PCNL remains the gold standard for stones > 2 cm according to many guidelines, these aspiration technologies offer promising alternatives, especially for patients who are poor PCNL candidates due to anticoagulation, obesity, anatomical abnormalities, or comorbidities. As these technologies continue to develop and more data become available, the traditional thresholds for PCNL may need reconsideration, particularly given the reduced morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and promising stone clearance rates of these newer approaches.

In conclusion, the development of DISS, FANS, and SURE represents a significant advancement in the management of urolithiasis, potentially reducing the need for PCNL in selected cases. While PCNL remains an essential tool in the urologist's armamentarium, these new technologies offer minimally invasive alternatives that can achieve impressive stone clearance rates with reduced morbidity. #### Testosterone Supplementation After Localized Prostate Cancer: The Current State of the Literature

Gal Saffati, MD

For decades, testosterone therapy (TTh) was considered contraindicated for men with a history of prostate cancer. This belief came from the seminal work of Huggins and colleagues in the 1940s, which demonstrated that castration led to prostate cancer regression. 1 However, more recent evidence has challenged this idea, suggesting that TTh may be a viable option for carefully selected patients.

The 'dogma' that prescribing TTh to men with a history of prostate cancer would inevitably lead to disease recurrence led to numerous men with hypogonadism being denied treatment that could have improved their quality of life. The saturation model, proposed by Morgentaler and colleagues, offered an alternative framework, suggesting that prostate cancer cells respond to testosterone only at very low concentrations. 2 Once the androgen receptors in prostatic tissue become saturated at these relatively low testosterone levels, additional testosterone does not accelerate cancer growth. This new model encouraged researchers to reevaluate the risks of TTh in this patient population. Additionally, newer studies have demonstrated that TTh does not appear to increase prostate cancer incidence in men without a previous cancer diagnosis.

##### Nadofaragene Firadenovec

This intravesical gene therapy uses a nonreplicating adenovirus vector to deliver the interferon alfa-2b gene into bladder urothelium. 1 FDA approval in 2022 followed a phase 3 trial in bacillus CalmetteGuérin (BCG)- unresponsive highrisk (HR) NMIBC with carcinoma in situ (CIS) ± papillary disease. 1 Among patients with CIS, the complete response (CR) rate at any time was 55%.
