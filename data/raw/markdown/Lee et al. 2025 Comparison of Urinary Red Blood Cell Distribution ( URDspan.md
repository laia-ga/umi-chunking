## ABSTRACT

Background: The clinical utility of urinary red blood cell (RBC) distribution (URD) remains limited. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of URD and dysmorphic RBC (dRBC) in a multicenter study.

Methods: This study enrolled 703 patients who visited four tertiary medical centers in Korea. Patients were classified into glomerular diseases with biopsy ( N = 169), renal diseases including chronic kidney disease ( N = 194), nephrotic syndrome (NS; N = 88), tubulointerstitial diseases ( N = 36), acute kidney injury ( N = 32), others ( N = 10), and extrarenal diseases ( N = 174). Renal parameters, urine microscopic examination, urinalysis, and URD assessments were conducted. The diagnostic performances of dRBC and URD were evaluated.

Results: Median values of both dRBC and URD were significantly elevated in patients with glomerular diseases. URD exhibited a significant correlation with dRBC ( r = 0.536) and albumin creatinine ratio ( r = 0.186), while no significant correlation was observed with specific gravity ( r = − 0.03). Among renal diseases, dRBC and URD values were notably higher in patients with NS. The agreement rate between dRBC and URD results was 78.3% (112/143), with 31 instances showing discrepancies. ROC curve analysis comparing glomerular and extrarenal diseases yielded cutoff values of 18% for dRBC and 31.9% for URD, resulting in corresponding areas under the curve (AUC) of 0.79 and 0.83, respectively.

Conclusions: URD exhibited a comparable diagnostic performance, as indicated by a similar AUC value to that of dRBC, while offering the added advantage of providing objective and standardizable results. This attribute enhances its utility as a parameter for distinguishing between patients with glomerular hematuria (GH) and those with nonGH.

## 1 | Introduction

Hematuria, characterized by the presence of red blood cells (RBCs) in the urine, can manifest either visibly (macroscopically) or under microscopic examination. It stands as a fundamental symptom of glomerulonephritis (GN), and its most effective detection method involves scrutinizing urine sediments under a microscope.

In 1979, Birch et al. [1] introduced the concept that urinary RBC could be categorized into two groups based on their morphology: glomerular and non-glomerular origins. Dysmorphic RBCs (dRBCs) are formed as a result of damage [2] to the RBC membrane during passage through the glomeruli.

In 1988, Shichiri et al. [3] documented that patients with glomerular disease exhibited smaller erythrocytes than those with non-glomerular disease. Since then, research focusing on urinary erythrocytes has been ongoing, and Hyodo et al. [4] proposed that a size range of 4–6 μm for RBC could be employed as a discriminative factor between glomerular and nonglomerular RBC.

The occurrence of small-sized RBC in the urine of patients with kidney disease can be elucidated as follows. RBC encounters a markedly different environment in urine compared to the bloodstream. One of the most significant distinctions lies in the considerably low albumin levels and elevated urea concentrations. This unique environment in the urine makes the erythrocyte membrane more susceptible to damage, leading to alterations in their shapes. High urea concentrations, in particular, promote the formation of burr cells [5–7]. Additionally, as RBCs traverse the basement membrane of the glomerulus, their membranes may undergo damage, resulting in the formation of acanthocytes [2]. Lastly, RBCs in patients with renal disease are more prone to various factors, including iron deficiency, osmotic lysis, shape distortions, and vesicle formation [8, 9].

It is worth noting that the accurate identification of dRBC in urine sediments necessitates the expertise of an experienced observer. Variability in interpretation between different observers may underlie the differences observed between laboratories. Continuous education of laboratory staff regarding the atlas of RBC morphology is essential. An alternative approach could involve the integration of deep learning technology for microscopic examinations [10]. Unlike observer-dependent methods, the small RBC fraction can be measured using instruments, making them easily attainable and less susceptible to interpretation bias. Thus, the small RBC fraction stands out as a more objective and standardized parameter for consideration.

Fully automated urine particle analyzers have become standard tools in clinical laboratories for analyzing urine samples. One such instrument is the UF-5000 fully automated flow cytometer, based on fluorescence flow cytometry (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). This device is capable of recognizing and categorizing urine particles, providing morphological details regarding RBCs, specifically non-lysed RBCs (NL-RBCs), lysed RBCs, large RBCs, small RBCs, and RBC-Info, which is an output measure of the UF-5000. The UF-5000 instrument classifies urine particles using four different signals: forward scatter light (FSC), side scatter light (SSC), side fluorescence light (SFL), and depolarized side scattered light (DSS). The “small RBC” represents RBCs having an FSC intensity < 70, and “non-lysed RBC” represents a strong signal by FSC and morphologically stable RBCs by SSC. The URD is obtained by dividing the number of “small RBC” by the number of non-lysed RBCs. Researchers have explored potential clinical applications for these results [11–13].

However, it is important to note that most of these studies have been conducted at single centers, resulting in variable cutoff values for distinguishing glomerular and non-glomerular hematuria (GH). Notably, the cutoff values for small RBC have shown significant variability (ranging from 30.1% to 40.5%), while those for dRBC have exhibited even more pronounced variation (ranging from 0% to 30.0%) [11–13]. Therefore, a multicenter study employing the same equipment is essential to apply these results effectively in clinical practice.

The current study was carried out across four hospitals, with concurrent collection of microscopic urine sediment results, urine parameters, serum markers, and relevant information pertaining to kidney function. The primary objective of this study was to compare the measured values with cutoff values between dRBC and URD gathered from these diverse institutions and to validate their clinical significance.

### 2.2 | Urine Microscopic Examination

Urine samples of 10 mL were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, followed by the removal of the supernatant. The remaining urine sediment was resuspended, and 20 μL of urine was collected. Screening was conducted through microscopic examination in lowpower fields (×100), and the counting of dRBCs was carried out in highpower fields (HPF, ×400) with 200 RBCs. RBCs were categorized into three groups based on their counts: 5–9, 10–29, and ≥ 30. Urine samples were sequentially selected, but samples with less than 5 RBCs per HPF were excluded from the analysis. Dysmorphic RBCs were identified through microscopy in HPF and were defined as acanthocytes or echinocytes and their variants, following Nagahama et al.'s classification [ 17 ]. Burr cells were not included in the analysis. The calculation of dRBC was expressed as a percentage of the total RBC. Urine microscopy was performed within 1 h of specimen receipt, with the examiner blinded to the patient's diagnosis.

### 2.3 | Urine Parameter Measurement

Routine urinalysis, along with measurements of urinary protein, albumin, and creatinine concentrations, was performed. Urine particle analysis was conducted using a fully automated urine particle analyzer, the UF5000 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan), in four tertiary clinical laboratories.

Specifically, at the P institution, the AU5800 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was utilized, while at the D, K, and S medical centers, the COBAS C702 (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) was employed. The urinary albumintocreatinine ratio (ACR) and proteintocreatinine ratio (PCR) were measured in some patients. Urine strip analysis was conducted using the UC 3500 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) at the K, P, and S medical centers, while the Uriscan Super+ (YD Diagnostics, Yongin, Korea) was employed at the D institution. It is important to note that both urine particles and chemical analyses were completed within 30 min of receiving the specimen.

### 2.4 | Serum Parameter Measurements

Levels of total serum protein, albumin, creatinine (Cr), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were assessed using a chemical analyzer. The AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) was used at the P institution, while the COBAS C702 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) was employed at the other three medical centers. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKDEPI) formula. We replaced eGFR values exceeding 90 mL/min with 90 mL/min.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at each participating center (CR21012L), and the requirement for obtaining informed consent from the patients was waived.

### 2.6 | Statistical Analysis

Descriptive data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used for the normality test. The performance of laboratory parameters in predicting GH was assessed through ROC curve analysis. In this analysis, the AUC, cutoff value, sensitivity, and specificity were estimated to predict the differential diagnosis between GH and NGH. Categorical variables were compared using the chisquare test, while continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. The significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The URL for the software is https:// www. Rproje ct. org .

## 3 | Results

### 3.1 | Patient and Laboratory Characteristics

Among the 703 patients, 371 (52.8%) were female, and 332 (47.2%) were male, with a median age of 59 years (range, 44–73). Comprehensive patient characteristics are detailed in Table 2 . dRBC, URD, Cr, eGFR, and ACR revealed significant differences among the three groups. BUN showed no difference between glomerular and extrarenal diseases.

The median values of dRBC, URD, PCR, and ACR were highest in patients with glomerular diseases, while those of BUN and Cr were highest in patients with renal diseases. The median values of dRBC and URD in Groups 1, 2, and 3 were as follows: dRBC (%), 60, 40, and 5; URD (%), 46.4, 40.1, and 10. The shapes of various RBC identified as dRBC are depicted in Figure 2 .

### 3.2 | Demographic and RBC Characteristics in the Four Institutions

Demographic and RBC characteristics in the four institutions are summarized in Table 3. In the four institutions, the median values of dRBC and URD ranged from 7.0% to 60.0% and from 27.2% to 74.1%, respectively. The cutoff values of dRBC and URD ranged from 5.0 to 67 and from 25.0% to 35.2%, respectively. Through ROC curve analysis, we determined the AUC for distinguishing between glomerular and extrarenal diseases, aiming for an optimal balance between diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. The AUC for dRBC and URD was 0.79 and 0.83, respectively, with no statistical difference observed. However, URD exhibited higher sensitivity and specificity compared to dRBC.

### 3.3 | Correlation of URD and dRBC Results With Renal Parameters

As the size of urine RBC might be influenced by the osmolality of the urine, we measured the correlation of URD with renal parameters. URD exhibited significant correlations with dRBC (0.536), ACR (0.186), BUN ( − 0.12), and Cr ( − 0.09), but not with specific gravity ( − 0.03) (Table 4 ). Dysmorphic RBCs demonstrated a significant correlation with URD and ACR (0.13).

### 3.4 | URD And dRBC Results Among Renal Diseases

We analyzed the results of renal parameters specifically in patients with renal diseases (Table 5 ). The results revealed a significant increase in BUN and Cr levels among patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Conversely, dRBC and URD demonstrated a significant increase in patients with nephrotic syndrome (NS). URD decreased significantly in patients with AKI.

### 3.5 | Comparison of dRBC and URD Results

A comparison between dRBC and URD results was conducted in institution D (Table 6 ). The agreement rate was 78.3% (112/143), with 31 results showing discrepancies. In eight cases with high URD and low dRBC, small isomorphic RBC ( N = 6) and yeasts ( N = 2) might have contributed to the inconsistent findings. In 23 cases with low URD and high dRBC, dRBC were within the normal size range. The prevalence of normally sized dRBC contributed to the lower URD value.

Mizuno [ 13 ] and Cho [ 12 ] used the same UF5000 equipment as the present study, but their study was conducted at a single institution, and the results were compared with those of the present study, which was a multiinstitutional study. In this study, individual diagnoses for glomerular disease, renal disease, and extrarenal disease were presented, but previous studies did not present individual diagnoses and simply classified them into GH and NGH. Compared to the other two studies, this study had the largest number of subjects (This = 703, Mizuno = 203, and Cho = 263) also showed the best AUC value of URD (This = 0.827, Mizuno = 0.649, and Cho = 0.814,) between the GH and NGH groups. In addition, (1) comparison of the distribution of URD and dRBC results in the renal disease group and (2) analysis of the cause in samples with discordant URD and dRBC results were performed only in this study.

In Table 2 , median values of URD and dRBC were significantly higher in patients with glomerular diseases, while those of BUN and Cr were significantly higher in patients with renal diseases than those in the extrarenal diseases. These findings are consistent with a previous study that reported elevated parameters of small RBCs and lysed RBCs in the GH group compared to the NGH group [ 13 ].

In Table 3, the AUC of URD was found to be similar to that of the dRBC, with better sensitivity and specificity. The cutoff values of this study were determined at > 18% and > 31.9%, respectively. In previous studies, the cutoff values of clinically significant dRBC have been reported to span a wide range, from 10% to 90%, distributed within a wide range [19-21]. In our results, the median value of dRBC was low in two institutions and high in two others, while that of URD was high in one institution and similar in the remaining three. For identifying dRBCs in urine sediments, an experienced observer is essential. Interobserver variation may be the reason for the between-laboratory variability. URD parameters can be measured using an instrument, easily obtained, and are less affected by the observer interpretation. URD can be utilized as more objective and standardized parameters. In a separate study, the presence of > 25% dRBC exhibited both high specificity and a high positive predictive value in diagnosing glomerular disease or GH, serving as a significant predictor of GH [22]. Given that the AUC of URD was comparable to that of dRBC and demonstrated less variability across different hospitals, URD may hold broader applicability than dRBC in ruling out non-glomerular causes of hematuria [23].

To elucidate the effects of urine osmolality on the size of the urinary RBC, we attempted to analyze the correlation between URD and osmolality. As osmolality was not measured in all patients, we utilized the results of specific gravity. URD values were not found to be influenced by specific gravity. Instead, they correlated with ACR, BUN, and Cr, suggesting glomerular damage.

In the discrepancy analysis, it was observed that the URD value was lower in the presence of normal-sized dRBC and higher when small-sized isomorphic RBC were present. Notably, URD and dRBC, while sharing similar clinical significance, represent distinct types of RBCs. In two previous studies on URD, Cho et al. [12] did not study the correlation between dRBC and URD, and Mizuno et al. [13] reported a correlation coefficient of 0.465, showing similar results to the present study (0.536).

The authors conducted an analysis to compare URD and dRBC for distinguishing between GH and NGH by combining various tests. A notable limitation of this study is that the variability in the results of the dRBC analysis across different institutions, with over half of the cases originating from institution K. Although efforts were made to minimize bias by employing the same guidelines for determining dRBC, the presence of ambiguous dRBC resulted in a lack of consensus, as clinical laboratories had to report patient results. It is important to recognize that this study mirrors real-world conditions, analyzing results as reported by these clinical laboratories. Despite the majority of data coming from institution K, it is believed that it did not impact the key results of this study, namely the similar AUC values of URD and dRBC, as well as the absence of correlation between URD and SG.

This multicenter study entailed a comprehensive comparison of serum and urine parameters collected from four different hospitals, confirming their clinical relevance in distinguishing between patients with GH and those with NGH. Within this study, URD demonstrated an AUC comparable to that of dysmorphic RBCs but offered the advantages of being more objective and easier to standardize. Consequently, URD is anticipated to find broader utility compared to dysmorphic RBCs.
