A B S T R A C T

Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) is a chronic, progressive, and potentially sight-threatening form of ocular inflammatory disease that primarily affects children and young adults. Prevalence varies by region, ranging from < 2 per 10,000 in the United States to as high as 1,100 per 10,000 in parts of Africa. The rarity of VKC in developed countries can make differential diagnosis challenging, and treatment is often delayed until the disease is advanced, and symptoms are significantly impacting patients ’ quality of life. Although once viewed primarily as an immunoglobulin E-mediated condition, approximately 50% of patients with VKC do not exhibit allergic sensitization. It is now recognized that the immunopathology of VKC involves multiple inflammatory pathways that lead to the signs, symptoms, and conjunctival eosinophilic and fibroproliferative lesions that are a hallmark of the disease. We examine the evolution of our understanding of the immunopathology of VKC, the expanding VKC treatment armamentarium, the clinical implications of emerging treatment approaches, and future directions for VKC research and practice.

## 1. Introduction

Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC) is a chronic, progressive, and potentially sight-threatening form of ocular inflammatory disease. 32,41 VKC primarily affects children and adolescents ( < 20 years), with an age of onset less than 10 years in most cases. 29,98,172 Although VKC typically resolves with puberty, prospective and retrospective analyses indicate that up to 12% of patients with VKC are adults and that up to 82% of adult cases developed after puberty. 57,102,139

As VKC shares many clinical features with other forms of allergic conjunctivitis, misdiagnoses, and treatment delays are an ongoing challenge for practitioners and patients alike. In addition, advances in our understanding of the complex immunopathology underlying VKC has fueled the development of new treatment options. We shall provide an overview of the evolving VKC treatment landscape, including the clinical implications of new and emerging treatment approaches and future directions for research and practice.

## 2. Epidemiology of VKC

Overall, VKC accounts for less than 1% of ocular diseases in the developed world; however, prevalence varies widely by region. 32,41 It is most common in warm, arid, windy climates that are rich in environmental allergens such as central Africa, Japan, India, and South America, but VKC has also been reported in North America, China, Australia, Great Britain, and Sweden. 101 8

## 3. Signs and symptoms

The clinical signs of VKC include a papillary reaction of the upper tarsal conjunctiva and throughout the limbus. VKC is divided into 3 subtypes based on the distribution of papillae: tarsal, limbal, or mixed. 117 Tarsal VKC has large ( > 1.0 mm diameter) cobblestone-like papillae on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. Patients with limbal VKC generally present with Horner-Trantas dots that reflect infiltration of the limbal conjunctiva and release of inflammatory mediators by eosinophils, mast cells, and lymphocytes. The mixed form has both tarsal and limbal involvement. 117,183

VKC is characterized by signs and symptoms that include ocular hyperemia, itching, foreign body sensation, tearing, mucoid discharge, palpebral thickening, and associated pseudoptosis. 172 There may also be pain in patients with corneal involvement. While these overlap with those observed in patients with allergic conjunctivitis ( Table 1 ), 103 intense photophobia and ocular pain are more specific for VKC. 32

VKC should be suspected when agents used for the treatment of allergic conjunctivitis (e.g., topical and/or systemic antihistamines) do not resolve symptoms, and a definitive diagnosis requires the presence of limbal and/or tarsal papillae. 32 Ineffectively-treated VKC may lead to corneal neovascularization or shield ulcers, and tissue reepithelialization has the potential to result in astigmatism, keratoconus, amblyopia, and corneal perforation. 32

Several approaches have been proposed for grading the severity of VKC. The system developed by Bonini ( Table 2 ) has been used extensively to determine disease severity. 28 More recently, Gokhale and colleagues proposed a grading system that includes the severity of ocular signs (e.g., the presence of Horner-Trantas dots, cobblestone pattern, presence of superficial punctate keratitis and of macro-erosions), symptoms, and chronicity that divides severity into 5 categories: mild, moderate intermittent, moderate chronic, severe, and blinding. 72 Another approach to grading from the VKC-Collaborative Longitudinal Evaluation of Keratoconus study (CLEK) is focused on damage detected with fluorescein staining. In this scheme, the cornea and the limbal area are divided into 5 regions: central, superior, nasal, inferior, and temporal limbal. The total severity score is the sum of the staining scores assigned for each region (0 – 4 for the central area 0 – 1 for each limbal area). A total score < 3 is considered mild disease, > 3 to < 6 is moderate, and severe if > 6. 104

## 4. Pathogenesis

VKC has been previously described as an immunoglobulin E (IgE)mediated disease. 41,64 The type 1 IgE-mediated response is an immediate hypersensitivity reaction that triggers production of IgE antibodies by immune cells and culminates in acute symptoms such as itching, swelling, and inflammation. IgE binding results in activation of mast cells, which in turn release inflammatory mediators, including histamine; interferon (IFN), the cytokines interleukin (IL)-4, -5, and -13, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα ); chemokines (CCL5, CCL11, and CCL17), tryptase; and lipid mediators (prostaglandins and leukotrienes). 4,63,143 Leukotrienes are detectable in the tears of patients affected by allergic conjunctivitis, including VKC. 5,121,161

Approximately 50% of patients with VKC do not exhibit allergic sensitization despite involvement of immune mechanisms of allergic conjunctivitis. 26,122,133,143 In one case series of 110 patients with VKC (mean age, 8.3 years) in Florence, Italy, 69% of patients had no other allergies, and the overall prevalence of IgE sensitization was just 45%. 133 A similar retrospective study of 195 children from across Italy (mean age at symptom onset, 7.1 years) found that 59% had no other allergic conditions, and 50% were skin-test negative with no evidence of IgE sensitization. 26 These findings may explain why topical antihistamines and mast cell stabilizers usually fail to control VKC. 142 Allergen sensitization may also provide a basis for differentiation of VKC phenotypes.

## 5. Diagnostic challenges and impact

The relative rarity of VKC can be a significant barrier to appropriate and timely care. As most eyecare professionals in developed countries do not encounter these cases in day-to-day clinical practice, missed or late diagnoses may delay initiation of treatment until later stages of the disease. 32,103

Early in the course of VKC symptoms may be intermittent and of short duration; such symptoms may go unnoticed or be attributed to allergic conjunctivitis, contributing to diagnostic delay. 70 A small-scale study carried out in the United Kingdom indicated that the time between first symptoms and a diagnosis of VKC ranged from 0 to 6 years. 69 The chronic and progressive nature of this condition along with delays in treatment may result in sight-threatening complications, including infectious keratitis, shield ulcers, corneal scarring ( Fig. 1 ), corneal

In the absence of established diagnostic criteria, VKC diagnosis and management are typically based on assessment of clinical features and use of grading schemes, such as the Bonini grading system ( Table 2 ), to determine disease severity. 28,141

The impact of VKC symptoms on patient quality of life (QoL) is profound. Early detection and prompt treatment are critical to minimizing the risk of sight-threatening sequelae in patients with VKC. ### 6.1. Nonpharmacologic measures

As with other ocular allergic diseases, identification of specific allergens/irritants and reducing exposure represent a cornerstone of managing VKC. Behavioral modifications such as wearing wraparound sunglasses and caps with visors can help reduce contact with nonspecific environmental triggers or irritants like dust and sunlight. Adjunctive measures such as artificial tears can help flush and dilute potential allergens and inflammatory mediators and cells from tear film. Use of cold compresses during acute episodes can also provide temporary relief from sensory complaints as well as from edema and hyperemia. 41,155,169

### 6.2. Pharmacologic measures

Recognition of the complex immunopathology of the various phenotypes of VKC has fueled the investigation of agents with more direct effects on the inflammatory processes underlying the proliferative changes and corneal damage characteristic of VKC.18 As a result, the VKC treatment armamentarium includes a range of therapeutic options—both approved and off-label—that offer varying degrees of efficacy to address the diverse signs, symptoms, and severity associated with VKC.136

#### 6.2.1. Symptomatic treatment

Symptomatic treatments for VKC include topical mast cell stabilizers, antihistamines, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs). These agents provide symptomatic relief but have little to no effect on the complex immune responses that underlie VKC.

6.2.1.1. Topical mast cell stabilizers ± antihistamines. As their name implies, mast cell stabilizers inhibit mast cell activation and degranulation, thereby preventing the release of a variety of mediators, including cytokines and the influx of inflammatory eosinophils, neutrophils, and monocytes.17,23 These agents (Table 3) are among the first prescribed when treating VKC and include cromolyn sodium 2% and 4%, nedocromil sodium 2%, N-acetyl-aspartyl-glutamate (NAAGA) 6%, and lodoxamide 0.1%.41,100,172 Results from a randomized, double-masked trial of topical cromolyn sodium 4% in children and adults with VKC (N = 65) indicated that cromolyn sodium was significantly more effective than placebo in relieving the signs and symptoms of VKC.65 Nedocromil sodium acts on multiple cells, including eosinophils, neutrophils, mast cells, macrophages, monocytes, and platelets.33,163 A study of nedocromil sodium 2% versus cromolyn sodium 2% in children with VKC (N = 36) found that nedocromil sodium took effect more quickly, with significantly greater reductions in itching, grittiness, hyperemia, and keratitis within 6 weeks. Nedocromil sodium was also significantly more effective than cromolyn sodium at reducing hyperemia, keratitis, papillae, and pannus at 22 weeks.171 Results from a second study comparing nedocromil sodium 2%, cromolyn sodium 2%, and placebo in 138 children and adults with VKC indicated that both medications were significantly superior to placebo in reducing disease severity and that nedocromil sodium had a more pronounced effect on symptoms than cromolyn sodium.62

NAAGA is a mast-cell stabilizing agent with anti-inflammatory effects such as decreased effects of complement activation, the production of inflammatory mediators (leukotrienes), and the expression of eosinophil surface-adhesion molecules. In a randomized pilot study of NAAGA eye drops versus levocabastine eye drops in patients with VKC (N = 23), eosinophil cationic protein tear levels were significantly reduced with NAAGA versus levocabastine. A similar trend was observed in change from baseline in total ocular symptom score, but did not achieve statistical significance.100

Lodoxamide inhibits eosinophil activation and degranulation and has been shown to significantly reduce tryptase levels and eosinophils in the tear fluid of patients with VKC.25 Results from a single-arm study in patients with VKC (N = 40) indicated that lodoxamide eye drops significantly decreased signs and symptoms, with 38 of 40 patients becoming symptom-free at the end of the study.49 A comparative study of topical lodoxamide ophthalmic solution 0.1% versus topical cromolyn sodium ophthalmic solution 4% in 30 patients with VKC demonstrated that lodoxamide was significantly superior to cromolyn sodium in reducing numbers of CD4+ and CD23+ cells and decreasing signs and symptoms of the disease.11 Another study of lodoxamide 0.1% ophthalmic solution versus levocabastine 0.05% ophthalmic suspension in 95 patients with VKC showed improvement in both physician- and patient-rated outcomes with lodoxamide over levocabastine. Although both treatments significantly improved symptoms versus baseline, lodoxamide significantly decreased conjunctival discharge, photophobia, lacrimation, itching, and bulbar conjunctiva at one or more time points as compared with levocabastine.170

The most common side effect of these agents is a transient stinging and burning sensation; treatment regimens typically require up to 6 administrations per day.1,86,106

Antihistamines are a diverse group of drugs that inhibit histaminic actions through agonist competition with histamine type 1 (H1) receptors.176 Mast cell stabilizers are frequently prescribed in combination with topical H1 receptor blockers to mitigate histamine’s primary effects of itching and swelling. More recent generations of selective H1 blockers (e.g., levocabastine, emedastine) have demonstrated better tolerability and longer duration of action than their first-generation counterparts (e. g., pheniramine, antazoline) and are recommended in this setting.101,176 Although symptom relief with topical antihistamines is rapid, the duration of effect is relatively short; up to 4 instillations per day are generally required.128

Dual-acting agents combine antihistaminic and mast-cell stabilizing properties to provide both immediate and longer-term relief for patients with ocular allergies, including VKC.59 Topical agents that have demonstrated dual activity include olopatadine 0.1%, 0.2%, and 0.7%; bepotastine besilate 1.5%; azelastine 0.05%; and alcaftadine 0.2%.59 With the exception of unit dose, preservative-free formulations, most of these agents also contain benzalkonium chloride, a surfactant preservative that may cause ocular surface toxicity.59

Olopatadine hydrochloride, the first such agent to be approved for chronic allergic conjunctivitis and seasonal allergic conjunctivitis in the United States and European Union,A,B inhibits anti-IgE-stimulated mast cell up-regulation of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) expression on conjunctival epithelial cells.44,166 It has been found to relieve numerous symptoms (e.g., itching, redness, eyelid edema) and improve QoL.45,59,146,157,166 In a prospective study of 0.1% olopatadine hydrochloride in patients diagnosed with VKC (N = 20), statistically significant improvements were observed at 2 months in clinical signs and symptoms, with concomitant reductions in goblet cell density.45

Bepotastine besilate also exhibits diverse immunoactive properties—including inhibition of IL-5, leukotrienes, platelet-activating factor, and eosinophil migration—and has demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of pruritus associated with allergic conjunctivitis.16 A recent randomized open-label study comparing the efficacy of olopatadine 0.1% and bepotastine besilate 1.5% in patients with VKC (N = 50) found that both agents reduced signs and symptoms after 8 weeks of treatment.157 Olopatadine showed significant reduction in papillary hypertrophy and bepotastine besilate reduced ocular discomfort, watering, and capillary hyperemia.157

Azelastine is another dual-acting agent that has an advantage of quicker action due to histamine blocking and long-term stabilization of mast cells.39 In a prospective study of monotherapy with 0.1% tacrolimus versus combination treatment with 0.1% tacrolimus and 0.05% azelastine in patients with VKC (N = 76) both treatments were associated with significant improvements in Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) scores at all follow-up visits versus baseline.38 The combination therapy group showed a significantly greater reduction in OSDI score from baseline versus monotherapy at 1 week. Combination therapy was also associated with significantly greater improvements in conjunctival hyperemia, conjunctival papillae, and corneal involvement.38

6.2.1.2. Topical NSAIDs. Topical NSAIDs include ketorolac tromethamine (0.5%, 0.3%)—currently approved for treatment of allergic conjunctivitis—and diclofenac sodium 0.1% (Table 3). These agents inhibit the release of the pruritogenic prostaglandins E2 and I2.41,174 A prospective study of 21 patients with VKC demonstrated that topical ketorolac was significantly superior to placebo in decreasing itching.151 Another prospective study demonstrated sustained efficacy of diclofenac sodium 0.1% eye drops in controlling signs and symptoms in patients with VKC (N = 22).46

A 2007 meta-analysis of 8 placebo-controlled studies (N = 712) of NSAIDs in allergic conjunctivitis confirmed the efficacy of these agents in relieving conjunctival itching and conjunctival injection, albeit with a higher rate of burning/stinging.106,159 Prolonged use of topical NSAIDs can lead to corneal toxicity, which limits their utility in patients with corneal involvement.41,74 Use of NSAIDs can also put patients at risk for corneal melt.155

#### 6.2.2. Immunomodulators

6.2.2.1. Topical corticosteroids. Corticosteroids have multiple mechanisms of action that impact both early- and late-phase allergic responses. Corticosteroids are recognized as the most effective anti-inflammatory agents for the treatment of active ocular allergies, including VKC (Table 3)55,106; however, these agents are also associated with potentially serious adverse effects—including increased intraocular pressure, development or worsening of glaucoma, formation of cataracts, delayed wound healing, and an increased risk of viral and fungal “superinfection”—that limit their utility in the management of chronic conditions.106,153,155,160,172

These risks are of particular concern given evidence that steroids are often inappropriately prescribed and inadequately monitored. For example, a retrospective study at a hospital-based eye center in India found that approximately 17% of patients were taking topical corticosteroids without medical supervision at presentation and that mean duration of steroid use was 21.3 months.149 The American Academy of Ophthalmology’s Preferred Practice Pattern advises against “indiscriminate” use of topical corticosteroids,169 while a position paper by the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) notes that topical corticosteroids should not be the first choice of therapy for ocular allergy and recommends that patients receiving topical corticosteroids be monitored by an ophthalmologist.106 For acute episodes of ocular allergy, a “pulse” regimen of 3–4 drops per day for 5 days is preferred. Longer treatment regimens should be limited to 1–3 weeks and tapered slowly over several days.106

As a class, corticosteroids inhibit inflammatory cell infiltration, mast cell proliferation, cell-mediated immune responses, and production of inflammatory cytokines including prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor.17,59 Molecular structure plays a critical role in the efficacy and safety profile of various corticosteroids. Ophthalmic corticosteroids with a ketone group at the C-20 position (e.g., prednisolone phosphate 1%, dexamethasone sodium phosphate 0.1%, difluprednate 0.05%, betamethasone sodium 0.1%, fluorometholone acetate 0.1%, rimexolone 1%) are thought to form corticosteroid-protein adducts that may play a role in cataract formation with long-term use.15,155 In contrast, loteprednol 0.5% was designed with a 17β-chloromethyl ester at the C-20 position, which is rapidly hydrolyzed to an inactive metabolite with less systemic absorption and a lower risk of ocular side effects than ketone corticosteroids.15,82

Corticosteroids used in VKC can also be broadly categorized into 2 groups: low potency (fluorometholone, loteprednol, rimexolone) and high potency (prednisolone, dexamethasone, difluprednate, betamethasone).155 Loteprednol etabonate 0.5% has been compared with prednisolone and fluorometholone in 57 patients with VKC. Both loteprednol etabonate and prednisolone resulted in significantly greater improvements in signs and symptoms (except for chemosis) than fluorometholone.126 Low-potency steroids have relatively low intraocular penetration and are usually the first steroids to be prescribed in active VKC cases that do not respond to nonsteroidal therapies.101 High-potency corticosteroids exert greater anti-inflammatory action but are also associated with higher risks of adverse effects, particularly in children.9 For example, children treated with topical difluprednate are at significantly higher risk of experiencing clinically significant increases in intraocular pressure than are adult patients.21,175 Reported rates of corticosteroid-induced glaucoma in children with VKC range from 2% (Italy)26 to 19% (India).149 In the latter study, 36% of children presented with visual impairment from corticosteroid-induced glaucoma that met the World Health Organization criteria for blindness.9,26, 149,150 As a result, high-potency corticosteroids are recommended for use only when low-potency agents fail to achieve the desired clinical effect.155

A variety of case reports indicate that severe VKC (e.g., palpebral with giant papillae) refractory to topical and/or systemic corticosteroid therapy may respond to supratarsal corticosteroid injection.81,144,154,162 Most recently, a single-center retrospective review found that treatment with supratarsal triamcinolone (median dose, 20 mg; range, 15–30 mg) resolved VKC symptoms (red eye, photophobia, itching, tearing) and increased visual acuity in 25 patients with treatment-refractory VKC (mean age, 9.1 ± 5.7 years).116 Similar results were reported in an open-label prospective study of triamcinolone 20 mg in 17 patients (mean age, 12.3 ± 3.65 years) with severe VKC refractory to topical corticosteroids and dual-acting agents.47 All patients experienced significant improvements in keratitis, reductions in giant papillae size, and complete resolution of lid edema and conjunctival chemosis. Reinjection was required in 5 patients after a mean of 13.1 months; the remaining patients experienced recurrences that could be controlled with topical agents.47 The effect of supratarsal corticosteroids on intraocular pressure remains unclear, with some studies/case series reporting no increases in intraocular pressure,116,144,154,162 and others reporting increased intraocular pressure in 6%47 to 17%81 of treated patients.

6.2.2.2. Calcineurin inhibitors. Calcineurin inhibitors such as cyclosporine A (CsA) and tacrolimus modulate the immune response in VKC by blocking IL-2-mediated Th2 lymphocyte proliferation.41 These agents have a long history of off-label use and have shown good efficacy in the treatment of moderate-to-severe VKC (Table 3).41

6.2.2.2.1. Cyclosporine. Cyclosporine A acts by binding to cyclophilin-1 in the cytoplasm of T cells to form the cyclosporinecyclophilin complex. This complex prevents calcineurin-mediated transcription of IL-2 and other proinflammatory cytokines, thereby inhibiting T cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation.130 When applied to the eye, CsA’s immunomodulatory effects improve the integrity of the corneal surface, while its antiapoptotic action protects conjunctival epithelial cells.130 Because systemic exposure following topical administration is negligible,20,31,105,130 CsA exerts its immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory effects without weakening the overall immune response or damaging ocular structures.20

Compounded eye drops containing cyclosporine have been used offlabel for the treatment of VKC since the late 1980s. They have been shown to be safe and effective in studies evaluating a wide range of formulations and CsA concentrations,134 although their use can lead to increased risk for ocular opportunistic infections,32 burning, and ocular irritation.155 The long-term efficacy of compounded cyclosporine (1% and 2%) was evaluated in a prospective study of 156 children and adolescents with VKC. Patients were followed for up to 7 years (mean, 3.8 years). Ocular objective scores improved significantly over time, with significant reductions observed at the beginning and end of each seasonal treatment period with evaluable patient data.134 Early research utilized a 2% concentration—often diluted in olive or castor oil—that was associated with treatment-limiting side effects, notably transient burning and foreign body sensation at instillation.22,54,75,90,132,148 Formulations containing the much lower concentration of CsA 0.05% were consistently more effective than placebo, but were sometimes unable to fully resolve or prevent exacerbations of VKC.42,83,93,178 Formulations containing CSA 1.0% demonstrated good tolerability and clinical efficacy for 4 months in a double-blind, placebo-controlled study156 and for up to 7 years in a single-center, long-term prospective study.134

The EAACI position paper on the management of ocular allergy recommends use of CsA eye drops as a corticosteroid-sparing agent in patients with corticosteroid-dependent VKC.106 Although the vast majority of research on CsA in VKC has focused on topical administration, at least 1 case report suggests that oral CsA may also have potential as a corticosteroid-sparing agent. Gokhale and colleagues reported on the use of oral CsA (75–150 mg/day) in a 6-year-old child with a 4.5-year history of severe, treatment-refractory VKC.71 Treatment response was dramatic—including resolution of a shield ulcer—and allowed the team to discontinue oral corticosteroids and shift to a lower potency topical corticosteroid.71

Limited drug bioavailability is a universal challenge when developing topical ocular medications. It has been estimated that up to 80% of drug is eliminated via the nasolacrimal drainage system within 2 minutes of instillation into the eye.3,91 As CsA is insoluble and lipophilic, ensuring adequate exposure and bioavailability required a carrier that would enable the drug to spread, remain on the eye surface, and achieve maximal absorption. The development of CsA cationic ophthalmic emulsion (CsA CE)—a cationic oil-in-water emulsion that exploits the fact that the ocular surface is negatively charged at physiological pH has improved the clinical utility of topical CsA.51,91

CsA CE is a positively charged cationic nanoemulsion containing droplets of CsA in an aqueous phase. Each droplet has an oily core that solubilizes the CsA and a coating of surfactants to stabilize the emulsion. When instilled into the eye, the positively charged droplets are attracted to the negatively charged cell membranes of ocular surface structures, improving dispersion and doubling CsA concentration on the cornea, thereby optimizing its bioavailability and therapeutic effects.50,51,91

CsA CE 0.1% is currently approved for the treatment of VKC in multiple countries in Asia, Europe, and North America. It was approved for the treatment of severe VKC in children from 4 years of age through adolescence by Health Canada in 2018. The recommended treatment regimen is 1 drop per eye 4 times daily (q.i.d.).C Treatment can be maintained at the recommended dose or decreased to 1 drop twice daily (b.i.d.) once adequate control of signs and symptoms is achieved.C In June of 2021, CsA CE 0.1% q.i.d. (1 drop per eye) was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of VKC in children and adults.D

Approval was based on the pivotal phase 3 VEKTIS (NCT01751126) and supportive phase II/lll NOVATIVE trials, which established the efficacy and tolerability of CsA CE 0.1% eye drops for treatment of VKC in patients 4–21 years of age.D,E,31,105 VEKTIS was a multicenter, randomized, vehicle-controlled trial that included a 4-month efficacy/safety period and an 8-month, double-masked follow-up period. In the first period, patients with severe VKC (N = 169; age range, 4–17 years) were randomized to CsA CE 0.1% q.i.d. or b.i.d. or to vehicle.31 Efficacy was assessed using a composite efficacy score that incorporated keratitis (assessed by corneal fluorescein staining [CFS] and scored using the modified Oxford scale), need for rescue medication, and occurrence of corneal ulceration; CFS score was a secondary endpoint.105 At

4 months, the composite score increased significantly in the CsA CE 0.1% q.i.d. and b.i.d. groups compared with vehicle (P = 0.007 and P = 0.010, respectively). The primary driver of composite score improvement was the change from baseline in CFS score, which accounted for 70.3% of the treatment effect in the q.i.d. group and 77.6% in the b.i.d. group. Patients in the CsA CE 0.1% q.i.d. group also reported statistically significant reductions in photophobia (P < 0.001), tearing (P < 0.009), itching (P = 0.001), and mucous discharge (P = 0.005), as well as significant improvement in QoL (P < 0.05) versus vehicle.105 Improvements in keratitis, symptoms, and QoL were maintained during the 8-month extension period.31 Of note, the proportion of patients requiring 1 or more course of rescue medication (dexamethasone 0.1% q.i.d. for ≤5 days) during the 4-month efficacy period was significantly lower in the CsA CE 0.1% q.i.d. group than the vehicle group (32.1% vs 53.4%, respectively, P = 0.010).105

The supportive NOVATIVE trial was a multicenter, randomized, double-masked, parallel-group, dose-ranging study in patients ≥4 years of age (N = 118) with moderate-to-severe VKC. The study design consisted of 2 treatment periods. In the first 4-week period, patients were randomized 1:1:1 to q.i.d. CsA CE 0.05%, CsA CE 0.1%, or vehicle eye drops. In the second, patients were administered CsA CE 0.05% or 0.1% q.i.d. for an additional 3 months.E,107

A pooled analysis of data from the NOVATIVE and VEKTIS trials found that the most common adverse reactions reported in CsA CEtreated patients were instillation site pain (high dose, 9.4%; low dose, 7.5%) and pruritis (high dose, 5.2%; low dose, 7.5%). The most common adverse reactions reported in the CsA CE clinical trial program were instillation site pain and pruritis. Most reported adverse events were mild-to-moderate in intensity. Laboratory data indicated that system absorption of CsA was negligible with both the high and low-dose regimens.30

6.2.2.2.2. Tacrolimus. Tacrolimus is a macrolide lactone compound derived from fermentation of Streptomyces tsukubaensis. Tacrolimus binds to immunophilin FK-binding proteins (e.g., FKBP-12), which in turn inhibits calcineurin and interrupts the sequence of activation of nuclear factor of activated T-cells (NFAT) and transcription of IL-2. Tacrolimus has a long history of use as an immunosuppressant following organ transplantation. In this setting, systemic tacrolimus has been shown to be a more effective immunosuppressant than CsA, but limited comparative data are available for ocular use.70,182 Topical tacrolimus use in VKC patients can result in ocular burning upon installation and an increased risk for corneal infections.32

Tacrolimus drops and ointment are widely used off-label, and compounded formulations at concentrations from 0.003% to 1.0% have shown significant improvements in VKC signs and symptoms when administered b.i.d. or q.i.d., with negligible systemic absorption.6,7,36,67, 84,89,90,119,120,135,145,152,173,179,182 Of note, tacrolimus ointment is approved solely as a second-line therapy for moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis, and within this indication, only the 0.03% concentration is indicated for use in children 2–15 years of age.F Tacrolimus 0.1% ophthalmic suspension has been approved in Japan since 2008.80 As of September 2023, 8 trials of tacrolimus for the treatment of VKC were listed on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Much of the available data on tacrolimus is from case reports, cases series, or open-label, uncontrolled studies with diverse dosing and treatment regimens and small numbers of patients. Comparative studies of tacrolimus and compounded formulations of cyclosporine have yielded mixed results, and as of this writing there have been no head-tohead studies of tacrolimus and CsA CE 0.1%. A prospective, doublemasked, randomized trial comparing tacrolimus 0.1% ointment b.i.d. and CsA 2% eye drops q.i.d. in 24 patients with active VKC (mean age, 9.61 ± 2.55 years) found that both treatments significantly improved subjective symptoms of VKC. No significant between-groups differences were detected in efficacy or safety, although tacrolimus was associated with a slightly less bothersome burning sensation on instillation.90 Similar results were reported for a prospective, randomized, double-blind study of tacrolimus ointment 0.03% b.i.d. versus cyclosporine 0.05% q.i.d. in 32 children with VKC (mean age, 7.62

### 6.3. Allergen-specific immunotherapy

When specific allergens have been identified, sublingual (SLIT) or subcutaneous (SCIT) allergen-specific immunotherapy is a diseasemodifying approach that desensitizes the body to allergens through exposure to gradually increasing doses over time.14,34 Although the process takes months to years to complete, immunotherapy can produce systemic improvements and reduce the need for topical or systemic medications.14,34,109 One prospective study of SCIT versus topical therapy found that SCIT was significantly more effective at improving symptoms and reducing serum IgE in patients with VKC (N = 64; mean age approximately 13.5 years).109 In a recent case report of successful SLIT in an 8-year-old boy with treatment-refractory VKC, Bernales and colleagues proposed that immunotherapy should be included as a primary treatment in patients with known reactions to specific allergens;14 however, as approximately half of patients with VKC do not exhibit allergic sensitization, immunotherapy is appropriate in a relatively limited number of patients.

### 6.4. Emerging therapies

#### 6.4.1. Leukotriene inhibition

Leukotrienes have long been considered a promising therapeutic target in VKC.121,142 Montelukast is a potent, orally administered leukotriene inhibitor currently approved for the treatment of asthma and seasonal allergic rhinitis. In a small-scale (N = 12), uncontrolled study of patients with comorbid asthma and VKC, montelukast treatment was associated with significant reductions in ocular signs and symptoms, including hyperemia, tearing, and photophobia (Table 3).92 In a larger, open-label, randomized, and controlled study in pediatric patients with VKC (N = 58), montelukast was associated with clinically and statistically significant improvements in signs and symptoms of moderate-to-severe VKC including redness, itching, foreign body sensation, and tearing versus mast cell stabilizers.76

#### 6.4.2. Interleukin antagonists

Dupilumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody that blocks IL-4, IL-13, and Th2-mediated inflammation by blocking cytokine secretion—all of which are involved in the pathogenesis of conjunctival papillae in patients with ocular allergy. Subcutaneous dupilumab is currently approved for severe, refractory atopic dermatitis.2,41 In clinical trials of dupilumab, conjunctivitis and keratitis occurred more frequently in patients receiving dupilumab,114,131 and a recent claims database analysis of patients prescribed dupilumab (N = 2144) between January 1, 2016 and June 30, 2019 also documented an increase in medical encounters for ocular surface disorders after starting dupilumab13; however, recent case reports of patients with comorbid atopic conditions (dermatitis, asthma), severe AKC, and VKC treated with dupilumab have reported amelioration of signs and symptoms, including giant papillae (Table 3).37,66,165 Recently, a retrospective analysis of dupilumab treatment in 3 patients with corticosteroid refractory VKC reported significant improvements in itching, photophobia, ocular discomfort, foreign body sensation, and tearing, as well as resolution of shield ulcers, reductions in conjunctival hyperemia, decreases in eyelid swelling, and complete resolution of upper tarsal giant papillae in all patients.165

A multicenter, double-masked, randomized, placebo-controlled, parallel-group study (N = 42) that is evaluating the safety and efficacy of dupilumab in the treatment of signs and symptoms of individuals with atopic keratoconjunctivitis (NCT04296864) is currently recruiting.G

Rebamipide, a derivative of quinolone-class antibiotics, is a mucin secretagogue that decreases production of IL-6 and IL-8 cytokines, reduces eosinophilic infiltration, and inhibits TNF-α.167,168 Rebamipide treatment has significantly reduced papillary formation in patients with VKC and AKC who were refractory to conventional pharmacological treatment (Table 3).168 Adverse events linked to this agent include dysgeusia and eyelid pruritus.85

IL-5 is critical to eosinophil activation, and targeting this cytokine has demonstrated efficacy in the management of eosinophil-mediated disorders, notably asthma.41,137 Three IL-5 targeted antibodies currently in use for the treatment of asthma—benralizumab, mepolizumab, and reslizumab—may have utility for the treatment of VKC.137 Benralizumab binds directly to IL-5Rα on eosinophils and basophils and reduces serum eosinophil levels.123 Benralizumab is currently approved as an intravenous adjunct therapy for severe eosinophilic asthma in children over the age of 12.137 Mepolizumab is an IgG1-type antibody targeted to IL-5 that is approved as add-on maintenance treatment of adult and pediatric patients aged 6 years and older with eosinophilic asthma.137,158 Reslizumab is an IgG4-type monoclonal antibody targeted to IL-5 that exerts similar effects on eosinophil levels and is currently approved for use as add-on maintenance treatment of severe eosinophilic asthma in patients aged 18 years and older.113,129,137

#### 6.4.3. IgE blockade

Omalizumab is a recombinant humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that binds to free IgE, an integral trigger of the allergic inflammatory cascade.61 Currently approved for the treatment of severe persistent asthma, case reports of off-label use in patients with treatment-refractory VKC indicate it may have a role in treatment of VKC (Table 3).58,61 A chart review describing 4 boys with severe VKC (defined as persistent corneal inflammation despite continuous topical 2% cyclosporine and steroid eye drops) treated with omalizumab found that 3 of 4 experienced a decrease in symptoms, reduced duration of flares, and decreased need for steroids.58 Monthly or biweekly subcutaneous omalizumab at doses from 225 to 600 mg improved physical exam findings (erythema and cobblestone papilla), decreased ocular symptoms (burning and/or itching), and reduced the overall need for topical antihistamines within 2 months of initiating therapy.52,58,68,78, 111,124,125,180 Treatment regimens and treatment responses were variable, which may reflect the influence of non-IgE-mediated disease mechanisms. ## 7. Comparative data and treatment selection

As treatment options for VKC continue to expand, the task of treatment selection will become increasingly complex. The heterogeneity of trial designs, patient populations, outcome measures, product formulations, and dosing regimens used in studies to date make it difficult to derive conclusions that can help guide treatment selection for patients in real-world clinical settings.

Meta-analyses of available data on topical therapies have generally found no significant differences in efficacy among the most commonly used agents. 110,136,138 A 2007 meta-analysis of 27 double-masked clinical trials that included 1092 patients with VKC found that all the drugs evaluated reduced the signs and symptoms of VKC. More than 70% of the studies compared mast cell stabilizer with other agents. 110 A recent review conducted by Roumeau and colleagues evaluated 45 articles encompassing 1749 patients with VKC treated with 20 different drugs. Cyclosporine and tacrolimus were the most frequently studied drugs,

As seen in the preceding discussion, the heterogeneity of VKC makes it difficult to develop a global algorithm for the treatment of patients with this condition. Although regional/national guidelines and recommendations exist, in practice each treatment plan must account for differences in the periodicity and severity of VKC symptoms, the degree of corneal involvement, and patient-specific needs and treatment goals. However, there is reasonable agreement on overall principles of care.

The most basic goal of treatment is to provide optimal control of symptoms with minimal side effects. 88 There is broad agreement that treatment should be individualized based on disease severity and underlying pathology 19,72,88,117 and that patient education and nonpharmacologic interventions are important components of the overall treatment plan. 88,101,117 An expert group of 7 European ophthalmologists recently convened and used a modified nominal group technique to develop 47 key recommendations for VKC management. A key theme of these recommendations is the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment aligned with disease severity to preventing loss of sight and improving QoL in children with VKC. 48 Similarly, recently released recommendations from the Management of Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis in Asia Expert Working Group advocate a stepwise treatment approach that incorporates identification of triggers, patient education on ocular health, and treatments that address VKC symptoms. 117

## 8. Future directions

Much of the VKC research to date consists of nonrandomized studies with small sample sizes that make it difficult to detect potentially relevant between-group differences. Comparative studies with sufficient power to detect differences in the efficacy and safety of various VKC therapies are needed to establish a strong evidence base that can be used to develop diagnostic criteria and treatment guidelines. ## 9. Conclusions

VKC is a chronic, seasonal or perennial, and potentially sightthreatening ocular surface disorder that has wide geographic differences in prevalence and shares several clinical features with other forms of allergic conjunctivitis. This overlap can lead to misdiagnosis and treatment delays, particularly in regions where VKC is uncommon. Given the progressive nature of VKC, these delays can have profound negative effects on patient functioning and QoL.

Once viewed primarily as an IgE-mediated condition, VKC is now recognized as a complex disorder that involves multiple immune mechanisms, including overexpression of mast cells, Th2 cells, eosinophils, and a range of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules. Until recently, treatment of VKC consisted primarily of topical mast cell stabilizers, corticosteroids, and off-label application of a variety of agents meant to relieve symptoms and modulate the dysregulated immune response. The approval of CsA CE 0.1% for use in patients with VKC represents an important advance in generating corticosteroid-sparing alternatives and may soon be joined by additional topical and systemic options, including monoclonal antibodies targeted to key points in the inflammatory cascade.

Management of VKC often involves a mix of avoiding potential allergens, behavioral modification, and adherence to complex treatment regimens with multiple topical and systemic agents. As this can be challenging for children with VKC and their caregivers, it is important that health care professionals recognize the impact of VKC and be prepared to tailor treatment to each patient’s needs, educate patients and caregivers on the nature of VKC and available treatment options, and support adherence to the treatment plan.

All relevant English-language articles were reviewed and included. Priority was given to prospective studies and randomized clinical trials.
