## Adenomyosis and Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB-A) d Pathogenesis, diagnosis, and management

Jason A. Abbott, PhD, FRANZCOG, FRCOG, B Med (Hons), Associate Professor Gynaecological Surgery UNSW *

Keywords: adenomyosis uterine junctional zone conservative uterine surgery AUB-A

The complex pathogenesis and variable presentation of adenomyosis make it one of the most dif ficult of the FIGO PALM-COIEN abnormal uterine bleeding group to diagnose and treat. Basic clinical parameters such as prevalence are dif ficult to accurately assess because histological con firmation is usually employed; however, because of the access to and accuracy and utilization of transvaginal ultrasound and other advanced imaging techniques such as MRI, noninvasive diagnosis is recognized to be highly accurate.

The clinical symptoms of pain, abnormal uterine bleeding, and subfertility are the primary presentations of adenomyosis with increasing data supporting a substantial role of this disease in reducing fecundity and interfering with assisted reproductive interventions. Treatments have been aimed at managing symptoms and improving fertility options. Management by hysterectomy is not always desired by women, and with many women having children in their fourth and even fifth decades, it is often not reasonable to consider this radical option.

### Introduction

Adenomyosis is a structural pathology singled out in the FIGO PALM-COIEN classi fication system for abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) [1] . Opinion is divided on whether adenomyosis is actually a separate pathological entity at all [2] because some definitions such as irregularity of the endometrialemyometrial junction (the junctional zone) are also found in asymptomatic women. The diagnosis of adenomyosis has been made with increasing frequency in the last 20 years because of access to and accuracy and utilization of transvaginal ultrasound and other advanced imaging techniques such as MRI [3].

The principal symptoms of adenomyosis are pain and heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB). However, even these two seemingly simple presentations are debated, with some studies suggesting that there is no relationship between HMB and adenomyosis at all [4], whereas others report that the disease is associated with increased menstrual blood loss in addition to dysmenorrhea [5]. Researchers have noted that adenomyosis is often not associated with infertility, although this may be in part due to its presentation in the fourth and fifth decades when childbearing is complete [6]; however, with women often delaying pregnancy until this time, there is increasing evidence that there is an impact on both spontaneous and assisted pregnancy when adenomyosis is present [7e10].

This chapter will examine the etiology of adenomyosis and how it may be diagnosed in the current clinical context. Management options will also be examined and critically appraised, denoting current acceptable modes of treatment and which areas require further study.

Etiology

When a wide range of prevalence is reported in the scientific literature, it is often the case that the true facts remain unknown, and this is true for adenomyosis. The disease may occur in as few as only a few percent of women through to 70% of women, with many studies suggesting a 20e35% prevalence [6,11e14] in histological series post hysterectomy of up to 2000 women. Prevalence becomes more difficult to ascertain when varying diagnostic criteria are used during imaging before histological assessment and the differences in pathological diagnosis from center to center and even between pathologists with respect to histology [15].

Risk factors for the development of adenomyosis that have been well documented include multiparity and termination of pregnancy. Increasing parity of any type and cesarean delivery are reported to increase the risk of developing adenomyosis [16], with a study of 594 women finding an odds ratio of 5 for this association. This association was not reported to be as strong in a larger series of 1334 women, although increasing parity did increase the histological confirmation of adenomyosis [12]. In the California Teachers Study, 951 women with histologically confirmed adenomyosis were compared with 79,495 disease-free women, and increasing parity and longer estrogen exposure such as early menarche, short cycles, and obesity were reported as risk factors from this linkage study [17].

It is possible that increasing parity is more likely to breach the endometrialemyometrial junction leading to glandular elements growing within the myometrium. This same etiological hypothesis may be applied to uterine surgery where curettage [18], termination of pregnancy [19], and cesarean delivery [20,21] have been associated with an increased risk of developing adenomyosis.

Not all epidemiological studies have suggested these associations, with a retrospective study of 549 women with histological adenomyosis finding no correlation between parity and previous surgery [11] and a separate retrospective study of 873 women showing no correlation for any individual uterine procedure, although an increased correlation was observed when all of these were combined [22]. Such variation in published data is not unique to adenomyosis and is all the more reason for consensus agreement on diagnostics and methodologically sound studies.

Other risk factors for the development of adenomyosis include the use of tamoxifen [23], whereas cigarette smoking may be protective [18], possibly because of an effect on circulating estrogen levels [24]. The diagnosis may only be truly made when endometrium or endometrium-like structures are present in the myometrium with smooth muscle hypertrophy or hyperplasia [30] . Although the finding of glandular and stromal tissue within the myometrium is generally agreed upon, the depth at which this occurs from the eutopic endometrium where there is no basement membrane is more problematic. Depth of penetration into the myometrium may depend on the angle of sectioning during specimen preparation and the natural irregularity of the endometrial e myometrial border [32] . The requirement for the ectopic endometrium to be directly connected to eutopic basalis [33] is also a variant that is fiercely debated because isolated cystic lesions deep in the myometrium are well recorded, separate from the endometrium. There are a number of pathophysiological theories that may lead to the development of adenomyosis, and it is quite likely that there are multiple different permutations as suggested for endometriosis. The in-folding of the endometrium with direct penetration into the myometrium is a frequently described mechanism on sound grounds [30] . Damage to the endometrial e myometrial interface through pregnancy, myometrial incision, the development of leiomyomas that may breach this area [38] , and increasing age with cellular damage and repair [19,26] are logical, with increasing evidence to support this theory. Basement membrane damage through various MMPs or TIMPs are implicated in initiating damage to this area [39] , with other tissue factors such as VEGF [40] , cyclooxygenase [41] , and stem cell progenitors [42] purported to play a role in initiation, persistence, and progression of disease.

The role of the vascular system in the initiation and progression of disease has been suggested, with a pathological study identifying adenomyosis in 54/434 (12%) uterine blood vessels examined histologically [43] . This suggests a mode of local and more distant spread as well as multipotential perivascular cells as a causative factor for initiation of disease.

Dysmenorrhea is a frequent symptom in women with adenomyosis, and oxytocin receptors may be upregulated in the women in this group [44] compared with women who do not have disease. The role of the immune system has also been considered in the pathogenesis of adenomyosis [45] , with speci fic lymphocyte proliferation and antibody formation being hypothesized to be involved in the tissue injury mechanism that may lead to glandular in filtration as the initiating event in adenomyosis. The effects of macrophages on endometrial stromal cells and subsequent increase in interleukin production has also been reported to be a factor leading to adenomyotic implants and may be a future pathway for immunogenic treatments [46] .

There have been many theories around the causation of adenomyosis and its common association with other benign diseases of the female reproductive tract such as endometriosis and leiomyomas. Presently, these theories remain as theories only with the complex epigenetic, hormonal, and reproductive factors not fully realized with currently available evidence.

Diagnosis

Histology is the ultimate (and some would argue essential) method by which the diagnosis of adenomyosis may be made. However, even this is not simple because the depth of myometrial invasion to categorize the pathology as “adenomyosis” is variable, as noted in Table 2. Debate regarding what is and is not acceptable as a diagnosis hampers the potential classification systems for research nomenclature and possible management options for women. Possibly more important is the requirement of a major surgical procedure for a complete myometrial assessment, which may not be acceptable to women. If current or future fertility is desired, hysterectomy is not an option, although if the primary presenting problem is HMB, then hysterectomy would be both diagnostic and therapeutic, even in the absence of histological confirmation of adenomyosis.

Less invasive methods assessing histology through specific biopsy tools have been developed and used, demonstrating a high sensitivity but low specificity [31]. In a prospective study of 102 women who underwent hysterectomy, an initial ultrasound-guided needle biopsy was performed followed by histological examination of the entire uterus, with 29/102 (28%) women confirmed to have adenomyosis [47]. Sensitivity was 83% for TVUS and 45% for biopsy with specificity of 67% and 96%, respectively. Combining the two tests did not improve diagnostic performance, and the authors concluded suboptimal elements in both tests.

Laparoscopic biopsy has also been suggested, and in a prospective evaluation of 72 women who underwent laparoscopic investigation, a single full-thickness uterine needle biopsy was performed, with 8/42 (19%) women with dysmenorrhea as well as 5/30 (17%) asymptomatic women being diagnosed with adenomyosis, suggesting no advantages in using this technique for the diagnosis of adenomyosis.

Ultrasound and MRI

Noninvasive diagnosis of adenomyosis by imaging is certainly possible, and a recent consensus statement describes the terms, definitions, and measurements that may be used in the sonographic description of uterine diseases including adenomyosis. Such collective collegiate papers are a positive step toward a unified nomenclature for research and classification in uterine diseases [48].

Temporal changes in imaging must be considered when evaluating the evidence for imaging accuracy of adenomyosis. Certainly, early studies reported very poor diagnostic accuracy of adenomyosis using sonography [49]. With changes in 2D transvaginal ultrasound technology, diagnostic accuracy has improved [47,50] and current studies report very high accuracy using this modality [33,51e53]. In a prospective study, 3D ultrasound mapping was undertaken prior to taking biopsies from areas suspected of adenomyosis in 54 symptomatic women scheduled for hysterectomy [51]. The biopsies were guided by sonographic mapping, were full-thickness serosa to endometrium, and resulted in a sensitivity of 92%, specificity of 83%, PPV of 99%, and NPV of 71%. Importantly, from this study, women who had previous endometrial ablation or were receiving medical treatment had substantial reductions in diagnostic accuracy reflecting the changes in the endometrial myometrial interface that may occur in these circumstances.

In a prospective evaluation of 72 women prior to hysterectomy, 2D and 3D TVS was performed, and the histological diagnosis of adenomyosis was made in 32/72 (44%) women. The presence of myometrial cysts was the most specific 2D TVS feature, and the presence of heterogeneous myometrium was the most sensitive. For 2D and 3D TVS, respectively, the overall accuracy for the diagnosis of adenomyosis was 83% and 89%, sensitivity was 75% and 91%, specificity was 90% and 88%, positive predictive value was 86% and 85%, and negative predictive value was 82% and 92% [53]. These data are from expert sonologists using very defined criteria and strict methodology but demonstrating what the best possible accuracy is in the noninvasive diagnosis of adenomyosis.

Ultrasound has also been compared with MRI in a number of studies, again with changes in the diagnostic accuracy over time. A prospective series from 2001 reporting on 106 women and comparing ultrasound with MRI and histology demonstrated a higher specificity using MRI than ultrasound, although the two techniques had similar sensitivities [52]. Although MRI is less operator dependent and may be more accurate, it remains substantially more expensive and less available than transvaginal ultrasound [3,54].

From a meta-analysis published in 2010, 23 studies examining 2312 women using ultrasound and MRI concluded that both techniques have a high accuracy for the noninvasive diagnosis of adenomyosis, although MRI was determined to more often result in the correct diagnosis [55]. The pooled sensitivity of ultrasound from this assessment was 72% and specificity was 81%, whereas the sensitivity of MRI was 77% and specificity was 89%.

New techniques such as elastography have been described where the sonographic evaluation of tissue strain is undertaken and compared with existing ultrasound and MRI diagnosis, with preliminary reports suggesting that this improves the sonographic accuracy, although data on this technique are limited [56].

With improvement in imaging technology and more reliable interpretation of findings from images, data support a high accuracy for the noninvasive diagnosis of adenomyosis using either ultrasonography or MRI. Availability of these techniques and local expertise may guide the choice of imaging technique in diagnosing the suspected disease. Uniform reporting of imaging findings may form the basis of classification systems for future research [48,57].

Symptoms

Reliance on clinical symptoms for the diagnosis of adenomyosis is neither sensitive nor specific, although clinical presentation is the usual starting point for a differential diagnosis list and therefore should be part of the clinical process. Broadly speaking, AUB, pelvic pain, and infertility are the three symptoms that women with adenomyosis might present to their care provider. HMB does not seem to be highly predictive for all forms of adenomyosis, although increasing severity of disease may increase the likelihood of this symptom [4].

Dysmenorrhea and other forms of pelvic pain are present in a variety of gynecological conditions, and studies have reported dysmenorrhea in women with adenomyosis, although there is no information regarding pain that differentiates it from other conditions such as endometriosis or leiomyomas that may also cause dysmenorrhea [16,17,38].

Biomarkers

Biomarkers for diagnosing adenomyosis have not been as successful as imaging technologies, and although there have been a number of studies examining potential biomarkers, none are currently in use [39,40,58,59]. Many of these biomarkers are linked to etiology and are nonspecific, which causes problems in terms of differentiation from both gynecological disease and other systemic disease.

More selective biomarkers are being investigated and onedMALDI-TOF MSdhas been suggested as a potential source of diagnosis of both adenomyosis and endometriosis, although it does not differentiate between these two diseases [60]. Myosin is a protein that is documented to be overexpressed in adenomyosis, with potential for determining the extent of disease within the myometrium, although these are preliminary findings only [61].

Classification

Previous reviews, studies, and consensus meetings have called for a classification system for adenomyosis to assist clinicians in making a diagnosis and planning management in combination with their patients. Although there are several proposed systems, none has been universally adopted, and this is problematic at both clinical and research levels [57,62]. ### Treatment

The treatment of adenomyosis will depend on the presence of speci fic symptoms such as pain, AUB, or infertility. Treatment options will also vary according to these presenting symptoms and fall into medical treatments, surgical options, radiologic or sonographic interventions, or combinations of these choices.

### Medical treatment

Medical options are often first-line treatments, and symptomatic options such as NSAIDs and combined oral contraceptive pill are frequently used for women with AUB; however, there are few data to support adenomyosis-speci fic outcomes for these options. The best studied of the medical options is LNG-IUD, of which a RCT of 75 women allocated to receive either the device or the surgical option of hysterectomy [68] reported equivalency in outcomes for bleeding at short-term follow-up and minor superiority of LNG-IUG in some aspects of quality of life. Larger and longer term data report satisfaction rates of 72% [69] with LNG-IUD, and it provides symptomatic relief, uterine volume reduction, and the possibility of future pregnancy, making this a very reasonable medical option for many women with adenomyosis-related symptoms.

GnRHa, danazol, oral progestins, and aromatase inhibitors are also reported to be ef ficacious for the treatment of adenomyosis [70 e 74] , although the number of women involved in high quality studies is few, and comparative data are lacking at this time. Uterine conservation does allow for pregnancy to occur post treatment [75,76] . Table 3 provides details of studies examining medical treatments speci fic to adenomyosis divided by methodology.

### Surgical Treatment

Surgical management for adenomyosis may be conservative with the intent of maintaining fertility such as excisional or cytoreductive surgery, conservative with no intent of fertility such as endometrial ablation (after which pregnancy is contra-indicated), or extirpative where the entire uterus is removed. Hysterectomy in any form offers the advantage of freedom from bleeding and no chance of recurrence and is the gold standard treatment for women who have no desire for future fertility.

Local treatment by endometrial e myometrial resection is reported to be successful with depth of penetration being an important prognostic factor [82] . A prospective series of 50 women reported universally good outcomes with super ficial adenomyosis, but high failure rates with deep disease. Using GnRHa prior to rollerball ablation did not make a signi ficant difference to outcomes in a retrospective study of 190 women with adenomyosis with signi ficant reduction of both AUB-A and dysmenorrhea when ablation with or without medical treatment was used [83] . Local hysteroscopic excision of cystic disease that conserves fertility is also reported but requires a high degree of surgical skill [84] .

The most challenging of the surgical treatment options is conservative treatment with the view of maintaining (perhaps even enhancing) fertility. A current meta-analysis of these surgical approaches reports on 1049 women, and complete surgical removal of disease resulted in dysmenorrhea reduction by 82%, HMB reduction by 69%, and a pregnancy rate of 60%. Where only partial excision was possible, these results were all reduced to 82%, 50%, and 47%, respectively [62] . It is quite likely that partial excision occurred in a greater proportion of women, with more extensive disease leading to these poorer clinical outcomes. None of these data are from randomized trials, and most are from small and retrospective series.

A high degree of surgical skill is required for such fertility-sparing procedures, and adjuvant procedures such as temporary uterine artery occlusion are reported to decrease blood loss and make the procedure less technically challenging [85]. Retrospective clinical outcomes following laparoscopic uterine artery occlusion and partial resection of adenomyosis in 179 women with up to 3 years followup [86] report a sonographic mean reduction in uterine volume of 58% at 36 months and significant improvements in quality of life. In a separate retrospective study of 37 women using the same surgical techniques, clinical improvements were also reported [87].

One of the largest series reporting fertility outcomes is a 10-year study including 104 women with extensive adenomyosis that describes a technically challenging tripleflap technique for cytoreduction of adenomyosis and uterine reconstruction with the speci fic aim of maintaining fertility [88] . Postoperatively, 16/26 (61%) women desiring fertility became pregnant, with 14/26 (54%) women having a term delivery with no uterine rupture reported.

A laparoscopic approach using a novel radiofrequency ablation needle of adenomyotic nodules is reported in 15 women [89] . This procedure enables to perform biopsy of lesions for histology and then ablate the suspected areas, with preliminary reports mentioning reduced clinical symptoms, although fertility data are not yet reported.

### Radiologic or Sonographic Interventions as Treatment

The use of high-intensity ultrasound and interventional radiological procedures are increasingly reported in the literature as nonsurgical options for women with adenomyosis. Data from these techniques are also largely retrospective, although there is an increasing number of clinical reports and prospective evaluations with a limited number of RCTs that report on speci fic adjuvant treatments to the principal intervention rather than comparing between techniques. ### Infertility and Reproduction

For women with adenomyosis who wish to retain their fertility, there are potential impacts of the disease on spontaneous pregnancy without treatment as well as on ART efficacy and pregnancy outcomes following any procedural interventions. From a large caseecontrol study of 2138 women, those diagnosed with adenomyosis were more likely to have premature delivery (OR 1.84) or premature rupture of membranes (OR 1.98) [100], and both women and their medical providers should be aware of this risk. The issues associated with placentation may be a factor in premature delivery and other major obstetric complications [101] for women with adenomyosis because of changes at the junctional zone. Such changes may result in fetal growth restriction as well as premature delivery with poorer neonatal outcomes [102]. For women who become pregnant following surgical treatment, myometrial thickness may be related to outcome, with uterine rupture occurring in 2/23 (10%) women who underwent surgery, and a myometrial thickness of <8 mm may be a risk factor for this [103].

### Practice Points

 The complex etiology of adenomyosis involves factors that impact the junctional zone, with damage and repair in this area being contributory.

 This results in increased number of menstrual cycles, increased instrumentation of the uterus surgically (miscarriage, cesarean, and surgery), and increased number of deliveries as risk factors.

 Diagnosis can be reliably made by noninvasive means, and this is the preferred choice.

 Ultrasonography by well-trained sonographers is equal to MRI in diagnostic accuracy in current medical practice.

 Medical management options may include simple symptomatic treatments or hormonal choices.

 The LNG-IUD remains the best studied and most efficacious of the medical options for adenomyosis, although data are not strong.

 Longevity of treatment is important when considering medical options.

 Conservative surgical options require a high degree of skill, and data are of low quality in relation to clinical outcomes.

 Hysterectomy is highly successful if uterine removal is desired.

 Interventional sonographic and radiological techniques offer an alternative to conservative surgery with uterine retention, with emerging data to support their success and safety, although comparative data are not yet available

### Research Agenda

- 1. Having established noninvasive diagnostic methods that are reasonable and having proposed terminology for description, a classification system that may be used for research protocols is required that will focus on clinical outcomes for women.
- 2. Methodologically sound comparative medical studies with other medical and surgical options.
- 3. Prospective conservative surgical data that report long-term morbidity and fertility outcomes for women (clinical registry may be an initial choice rather than complex RCTs in this area).
- 4. Prospective interventional sonographic and radiological treatment data that report longterm morbidity and fertility outcomes for women (again a clinical registry may be an initial choice rather than complex RCTs in this area).
- 5. Although RCTs comparing conservative surgical and interventional techniques would be optimal, this may not be achievable in the short term, and higher quality prospective longitudinal data do seem plausible.
- 6. Further examination of reproductive function in women with disease but no previous treatment and women requiring ART, particularly in combination medical treatments that may enhance outcomes.
