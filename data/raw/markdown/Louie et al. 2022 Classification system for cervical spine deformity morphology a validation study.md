# Classification system for cervical spine deformity morphology: a validation study

OBJECTIVE The objective of this study was to initially validate a recent morphological classification of cervical spine deformity pathology.

METHODS The records of 10 patients for each of the 3 classification subgroups (flat neck, focal deformity, and cervicothoracic), as well as for 8 patients with coronal deformity only, were extracted from a prospective multicenter database of patients with cervical deformity (CD). A panel of 15 physicians of various training and professional levels (i.e., residents, fellows, and surgeons) categorized each patient into one of the 4 groups. The Fleiss kappa coefficient was utilized to evaluate intraand interrater reliability. Accuracy, defined as properly selecting the main driver of deformity, was reported overall, by morphotype, and by reviewer experience.

RESULTS The overall classification demonstrated a moderate to substantial agreement (round 1: interrater Fleiss kappa = 0.563, 95% CI 0.559–0.568; round 2: interrater Fleiss kappa = 0.612, 95% CI 0.606–0.619). Stratification by level of training demonstrated similar mean interrater coefficients (residents 0.547, fellows 0.600, surgeons 0.524). The mean intrarater score was 0.686 (range 0.531–0.823). A substantial agreement between rounds 1 and 2 was demonstrated in 81.8% of the raters, with a kappa score > 0.61. Stratification by level of training demonstrated similar mean intrarater coefficients (residents 0.715, fellows 0.640, surgeons 0.682). Of 570 possible questions, reviewers provided 419 correct answers (73.5%). When considering the true answer as being selected by at least one of the two main drivers of deformity, the overall accuracy increased to 86.0%.

CONCLUSIONS This initial validation of a CD morphological classification system reiterates the importance of dynamic plain radiographs for the evaluation of patients with CD. The overall reliability of this CD morphological classification has been demonstrated. The overall accuracy of the classification system was not impacted by rater experience, demonstrating its simplicity.

KEYWORDS cervical spine deformity; classification system; flat neck; focal deformity; cervicothoracic; coronal

s cervical deformity (CD) progresses, patients lose the ability to maintain alignment of their head directly over the pelvis and have difficulty maintaining a horizontal gaze.1 Balance and coordination can also be compromised due to spinal cord compression. Quality of life with CD is negatively impacted as patients are forced to subsequently expend significant energy to maintain a horizontal gaze.2–4 Based on the EQ-5D index, Smith et al. found that patients with CD reported substantial health impact across all age groups compared with an age-matched normative US population.3 In fact, the mean EQ-5D index for these patients was worse than the bottom 25th percentile for other chronic diseases, including malignant breast cancer, malignant prostate cancer, and chronic ischemic heart disease. Although much focus has been placed on the associations between global sagittal alignment and thoracolumbar deformities, the role of sagittal alignment in CD is still being elucidated. Recent studies have shown that alterations in cervical lordosis (CL) may impact outcomes related to both thoracolumbar scoliosis and patients undergoing surgery for cervical myelopathy.5,6 Horizontal gaze, as measured by the chin-brow vertical angle (CBVA), is another recent area of focus related to CD, as the CBVA has been shown to be correlated with overall disability.7 We are in the beginning stages of understanding the role of sagittal alignment and outcomes following CD surgery.

Without an organized method of analysis, CD-focused research findings may be compromised in the setting of wide pathological heterogeneity. Other spinal classification systems provide the fundamental nomenclature that enables systematic organization of these disorders.8–14 More importantly, these classification systems aid in patient treatment and establish the basis for evidence-based care. There is no current universally accepted classification system for CD. Ames et al.15 developed an initial classification that allowed common language to be applied to CD. Although this classification provided an initial mechanism to assess CD within the framework of global spinopelvic malalignment and clinically relevant parameters, the categories were based solely on a consensus among a group of surgeons, not linked to health-related quality-oflife (HRQOL) measurements, and were created a priori.15 Centered on the hypothesis that subgroups of severe CD behave differently because of their sagittal alignment patterns, Kim et al. designed a classification system grouping patients into distinct clinical entities based on specific findings on the dynamic and static cervical radiographs.16 A two-step cluster analysis in that study revealed 3 distinct sagittal CD patterns: flat neck, focal deformity, and cervicothoracic deformity. One unique feature of this classification system is its broad application in various care settings, as the classification is performed based on plain radiographs without the necessity for further advanced imaging. A more thorough understanding of the main drivers of these deformities can aid the preoperative workup and initial treatment-planning algorithm. However, this classification system has not yet undergone any validation process, which is critical to applying these patterns to evaluation, diagnosis, and surgical treatment.

The purpose of this study was to provide an initial validation of the morphological classification of cervical spine deformity pathology by Kim et al.16 To complete the overall evaluation of CDs, we have included a fourth morphological group, in which the dominant CD pattern is in the coronal plane. We hypothesize that this classification system is reliable and accurate across training levels.

## Methods

### Study Sample

The sample was extracted from a prospective multicenter database of patients with CD operated on between 2013 and 2015 at one of the 13 participating centers (see Appendix). All patients in the database are at least 18 years of age with a CD, defined as the presence of at least one of the following: cervical kyphosis (C2–7 sagittal Cobb angle > 10°), cervical scoliosis (C2–7 coronal Cobb angle > 10°), C2–7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA) > 4 cm, or CBVA > 25°. An expert panel from the multi-institutional research group determined and confirmed the broad classification of these patients into the CD database.3,16 Patients with active tumor or infection were excluded. For the current investigation, only preoperative radiographic images were included.

All patients in this cohort obtained full-length anteroposterior (AP) and lateral standing spine radiographs, as well as AP and neutral lateral cervical plain radiographs with flexion and extension views. The radiographic parameters evaluated on the 36-inch long-cassette spine radiographs in this study include pelvic incidence (PI), lumbar lordosis (LL), PI-LL mismatch, pelvic tilt (PT), C7-SVA, and T1 pelvic angle (T1-PA). The following regional parameters were measured on cervical plain radiographs: C2–7 Cobb angle/CL, cervical SVA (cSVA), C2 slope (C2S), T1 slope (T1S), and the difference between T1S and CL (T1S-CL). We measured the maximum focal kyphosis between two adjacent segments and the number of kyphotic levels that had an angle > 5°. The flexibility of each measurement was recorded and refers to the absolute change in a measurement between the lateral radiograph and the extension radiograph. The radiographic measurements were performed for each patient at a single center (Hospital for Special Surgery, New York) using validated and dedicated software for spinal alignment analysis.17 Based on the original publication,16 the research team extracted 10 consecutive patients for each of the classification subgroups (flat neck, focal deformity, and cervicothoracic), as well as 8 patients with coronal deformity only. Patients who underwent surgery at the authors’ institutions were excluded. None of the authors participated in the care of the cases presented in this study.

### Panel Review for Classification Validation

For each patient, full-length spine (AP and lateral) and cervical (AP, lateral, flexion, and extension) plain radiographs were provided with global, cervical, and focal sagittal parameters. The patients were randomized so they were not grouped by morphological classification. ### Statistical Analysis

Describing the cohort in terms of demographic information and sagittal alignment in neutral, flexion, and extension was performed using means and standard deviations (SDs), medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), or rate for categorical variables. Comparisons of sagittal alignment between deformity types were conducted using either ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests.

The Fleiss kappa coefficient was utilized to evaluate intra- and interrater reliability. Kappa values were grouped as follows: 0.00–0.20 = slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 = fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 = moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 = substantial agreement, and 0.81–1.00 = almost perfect agreement.18 Interrater reliability was calculated and reported for the entire group of raters and by training level. Intrarater reliability was calculated for each rater and summarized using mean values for the entire group of raters as well as by training level.

Accuracy, defined as properly selecting the main driver of deformity, was reported overall, by morphotype, and by reviewer experience. A subanalysis on patients with com-bined drivers of deformity was conducted; the accuracy of selecting at least one main driver of deformity was also reported.

## Results

### Cases

The average age (± SD) of the 38 patients with CD was 63.8 ± 11.0 years (range 40.0–83.7 years), and the average BMI was 28.8 ± 7.7 (range 18.5–5.0; Table 1). The sagittal plane cervical parameters for patients in each cervical type are presented in Table 2. In total, 15 individuals reviewed the imaging: 5 were orthopedic surgery residents, 3 were spine surgery fellows, and 7 were spine fellowship– trained attending surgeons.

### Reliability Testing

The overall classification of the deformity demonstrated a moderate to substantial agreement (round 1: interrater Fleiss kappa = 0.563, 95% CI 0.559–0.568; round 2: interrater Fleiss kappa = 0.612, 95% CI 0.606–0.619). Stratification by training demonstrated similar reliability for round 1 among residents, spine fellows, and spine surgeons: residents 0.547, 95% CI 0.527–0.567; fellows 0.600, 95% CI 0.572–0.628; surgeons 0.524, 95% CI 0.515–0.533. Of the 15 raters, 11 reclassified each case for intrarater reliability.

### Accuracy

Of 570 possible questions, reviewers provided 419 correct answers (73.5%; Table 3). Stratification of the correct grouping by type of deformity demonstrated a lower reliability for flat neck and cervicothoracic deformities compared to focal deformities (flat neck 67.3%, focal 84.7%, cervicothoracic 68.7%, and coronal 73.3%; p = 0.002). Lower accuracy was found for patients having a secondary driver of deformity, as 50% of the raters selected the first driver and 50% selected the secondary driver. In cases in which accuracy was less than 75%, most had two major pools of categories selected (i.e., focal deformity with a coronal component).

When considering the true answer as being selected by at least one of the two main drivers of deformity, the overall accuracy increased from 73.5% to 86.0%. To date, there has been one other comprehensive classification system developed for cervical spine deformity. Ames et al. developed a classification as an adaptation of the Scoliosis Research Society–Schwab classification for adult thoracolumbar deformity. 15 To classify patients in this system, full-length standing AP and lateral spine radiographs are required, including the cervical spine and femoral heads. Additionally, standing posteroanterior and lateral cervical spine plain radiographs, a clinical photograph or radiograph that includes the skull for measurement of the CBVA, and modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association scale scores are required. This classification system did an excellent job in providing a common nomenclature in which to describe CD. However, the classification groups were based on a consensus from a group of surgeons and were defined a priori . The CBVA and T1SCL radiographic modifiers were not observed to be specific to subjects with CDs and can occur in asymptomatic subjects without alteration in HRQOL. 22

Thus, Kim et al. worked to develop a new, data-driven classification in which the CD subgroups were based predominantly on sagittal alignment. 16 A two-step cluster analysis was performed using a combination of hierarchical cluster and k-means cluster analysis. The cluster analysis ultimately is a numerical approach that permits identification of natural groups within a data set. These subgroups were largely determined using dynamic radiographs that measure the patients' ability to correct their cervical sagittal alignment, which might ultimately behave uniquely in the context of both nonsurgical and surgical CD treatment. The Ames et al. 15 classification reported moderate inter- and intraobserver reliability (Fleiss kappa coefficients 0.489 and 0.584, respectively). Utilizing a similar reliability analysis, this study found moderate to substantial reliability with interrater Fleiss kappas of 0.563 (round 1) and 0.612 (round 2), as well as an intrarater score of 0.686 (substantial reliability). In both studies, all angular measurements were provided to the reviewers, aiding in the reliability of these classification systems. It is important to highlight that despite having the measurement values provided, in the Ames et al. classification and the current CD morphotype classification (Kim et al. 16 ), specific radiographic parameters do not need to be measured as no thresholds are used. This validation attempt provided additional analyses not presented in the Ames et al. classification. The greater interrater reliability observed in this study (in rounds 1 and 2) may be explained by the fact that the Ames et al. subgroups are just single aspects of the classification system, and so those types may be embedded within different groups of this system, whereas the deformity morphological pattern is the focus of the Kim et al. 16 subgroups. The similar intrarater reliabilities observed in both studies highlight that the raters are consistent with themselves in grouping CD pathology. An effective rating accuracy of 73.5% overall was achieved when the raters were forced to select a single morphological category. Understanding that patients may present with more than two types of deformities, we observed that when accuracy for a specific patient was below 75%, there were often two major pools of answers present. When taking into consideration that the specific CD may contain two main drivers of deformity, the overall accuracy increased to 86%. This finding underscores the fact that CDs have complicated presentations and multiple considerations must be made. Additionally, we observed similar accuracy among orthopedic residents, spine fellows, and spine fellowship-trained attending surgeons, thus highlighting the simplicity of this classification system. The simplicity and accuracy across training levels are key elements of several clinically relevant classification systems commonly applied in spine pathology. 10,13,23

The findings of this validation study are important because they establish the next steps in providing evidencebased care for patients with CD. 24 Potential surgical interventions can be developed from this subset of morphological groups once conservative measures fail. 4,25,26 There are several limitations to this study. Despite the use of reviewers from various training levels, all reviewers were from a single tertiary spine center, which limits the immediate generalizability of the validation results. However, the reviewers who participated in this validation study were not part of the original study in which the classification was developed. We also instructed the reviewers to select one pattern of deformity, while some patients had some secondary drivers. If the question had been 'select all that apply,' we would have allowed for greater accuracy but lost the main results regarding the primary deformity driver. Additionally, some of the trainees that served as reviewers were not part of this single spine center at the time of classification creation. The CD cases are the result of a retrospective review from a database of patients who were preselected by clinicians and/ or researchers for enrollment into studies. The 38 patients selected for classification validation is a small sample size, but we felt that additional patients could cause fatigue for the reviewers. Lastly, as this is a multicenter patient database, the plain radiographs are from various centers; thus, we are unable to standardize flexion-extension radiograph protocols. Similarly, no supine plain radiographs were obtained, which could further capture the flexibility of the CD. Nevertheless, we believe that this patient cohort is representative of the range of CD, and the size of the reviewer panel effectively allows for validation of this classification system.

## Conclusions

The initial validation of a CD morphological classification system reiterates the importance of dynamic plain radiographs for the evaluation of patients with CD. This study has demonstrated the overall reliability of this CD morphological classification. The overall accuracy of the classification system was not affected by rater experience, demonstrating its simplicity. Further analysis of the surgical treatment strategy for these different patterns will guide the most effective treatment.
