# SOGC Statement on Rh and Alloimmunization

Recommendations by health professional organizations before 2021, including the SOGC and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), advised routine administration of RhIg to all Rh-negative unsensitized patients after abortion or pregnancy loss at less than 12 weeks of gestation. However, these recommendations were based on little or very low-quality evidence. The potential benefit of preventing Rh alloimmunization was believed to outweigh negligible medical risks and economic costs of immunization. Since then, the body of evidence has evolved, and recent studies consistently report a very low risk of Rh alloimmunization associated with abortion or pregnancy loss at less than 12 weeks of gestation.

Before 2021, the literature supporting early pregnancy RhIg prophylaxis was largely retrospective and relies on outdated techniques for quantifying fetal-maternal hemorrhage (FMH). These studies primarily measure FMH rather than the actual development of alloimmunization, and they fail to establish a clear threshold of fetal blood transfer that would necessitate prophylaxis. Furthermore, the studies that initially supported the use of RhIg in early pregnancy were conducted in the 1970s using abortion techniques that may have contributed to a greater risk of FMH.

In 2021, a systematic review found no significantly higher alloimmunization rates in cases where RhIg was withheld in the first trimester. As a result, many expert guidelines—including those from the SOGC, ACOG, the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), the World Health Organization (WHO), the Society for Family Planning, and several European professional bodies—no longer recommended routine RhIg prophylaxis for abortion or pregnancy loss at less than 12 weeks of gestation (See Appendix 1 for a summary). Instead, current recommendations have shifted toward a more selective approach, reserving RhIg for later gestational ages (generally beyond 10 to 12 weeks) or for individualized risk-based decision-making. This shift reflects a global trend, where the older the guideline, the more likely it is to recommend routine RhIg, whereas more recent guidelines published in the last five years increasingly support a more nuanced, patient-centered approach.

Beyond the lack of clear evidence supporting RhIg administration in early pregnancy, there are additional considerations that justify a more selective approach:

- • Healthcare System Efficiency and Access to Care: In Canada, RhIg is often administered in hospital settings, leading to increased emergency room visits and clinic burdens for a blood product with no confirmed benefit. Avoiding unnecessary RhIg use could alleviate ER wait times and reduce healthcare system strain.
- • Confidentiality Concerns: In rural areas, obtaining Rh testing can pose a confidentiality risk, as there may be only one facility offering ABO and Rh testing. Patients seeking early abortion care or having a miscarriage may experience unintended disclosure on their pregnancy status.

<!-- page: 2 -->

- • Patient-Centered Decision-Making: The SOGC guideline does not prohibit RhIg administration; rather, it encourages a shared decision-making approach, allowing clinicians to consider each patient’s individual circumstances, including risk aversion and future pregnancy plans.
- • Global RhIg Supply Considerations: There is an international shortage of RhIg, with some low- and middle-income countries struggling to provide it even for high-risk indications (such as administration at 28 weeks and post-delivery). Reducing its use for unproven indications could help preserve global supply for patients who demonstrably benefit from prophylaxis.
- • International Alignment: The SOGC’s updated recommendations align with the current policies of multiple international organizations, including the WHO, RCOG, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), the Society for Family Planning, and the national guidelines of countries such as Sweden, France, and the Netherlands. Notably, the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine (SMFM) is the only major organization still recommending RhIg for all early pregnancy losses and abortions. However, their position is not reflective of broader obstetric guidelines worldwide.

Given the above considerations, the SOGC believes that its carefully worded statement—allowing for the consideration of avoiding RhIg in early pregnancy—is a reasonable and evidence-based step forward. We recognize that research on Rh alloimmunization in early pregnancy continues to evolve and will continue to monitor new data as it emerges.
