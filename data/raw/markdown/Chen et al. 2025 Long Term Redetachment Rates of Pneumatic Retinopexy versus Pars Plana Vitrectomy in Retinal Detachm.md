# Long-Term Redetachment Rates of Pneumatic Retinopexy versus Pars Plana Vitrectomy in Retinal Detachment

## A PIVOT Post Hoc Analysis

Tugche S. Chen, MD(c), 1 , 2 , * Yasmin Motekalem, MD(c), 3 , * Isabela Martins Melo, MD, 2 , 4 Roxane J. Hillier, MD, 2 , 4 , 5 Alan R. Berger, MD, 2 , 4 Louis R. Giavedoni, MD, 2 , 4 David T. Wong, MD, FRCSC, 2 , 4 Filiberto Altomare, MD, 2 , 4 Rajeev H. Muni, MD, MSc 2 , 3 , 4 , 6 , 7

Purpose: To assess long-term redetachment rates of the Pneumatic Retinopexy versus Vitrectomy for the Management of Primary Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment Outcomes Randomized Trial (PIVOT).

Design: Randomized controlled trial.

Subjects: PIVOT trial participants.

Methods: This study was performed at St. Michael ’ s Hospital, Unity Health Toronto, Toronto, Canada. PIVOT trial participants who had undergone either pneumatic retinopexy (PnR) or pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) repair with a minimum follow-up of 2 years were assessed for longterm redetachment by chart review or telephone interview. The latter was the only accepted method for those with < 2 years of follow-up. Patients were only eligible if no reintervention to reattach the retina was performed within the first year of the initial procedure.

Main Outcome Measures: Long-term redetachment rates for PnR vs. PPV after RRD repair.

Results: Sixty-one participants who underwent PPV and 62 who underwent PnR were analyzed. The longterm redetachment rates were 0% and 1.61% (1/62) in the PPV and PnR groups, respectively ( P = 0.32). The mean follow-up duration in years was 5.43 3.60 vs. 5.51 3.03 in the PPV and PnR groups, respectively.

Conclusions: There was no statistically signi ficant difference in long-term redetachment rates for PnR vs. PPV. Both procedures are durable treatment options for RRD over an extended period, rarely requiring additional intervention for redetachment.

Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) remains the most popular surgical treatment of primary rhegmatogenous retinal nopexy versus Vitrectomy for the Management of Primary RRD Outcomes Randomized Trial (PIVOT) suggested that if speci fic inclusion/exclusion criteria were met, pneumatic retinopexy (PnR) should be considered a first-line treatment because it offers better functional outcomes and less morbidity than PPV. 1

The underlying concept of PnR is inherently straightforward. 2 It involves the intravitreal injection of an expansile gas bubble, such as sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) or perfluoropropane (C3F8), which temporarily tamponades the retinal break while the patient assumes a specific head position. This temporary closure of the break prevents new fluid from entering and allows for the resorption of subretinal fluid by the retinal pigment epithelial pump. Subsequently, a chorioretinal adhesion is created with cryotherapy or laser photocoagulation, permanently sealing the retinal break. 2 Although the reattachment rates have already been reported in the PIVOT trial up to 1 year, there are still questions regarding the long-term reattachment rates of PnR as this procedure does not address vitreous traction, which is one important factor in the pathophysiology of RRD.

The first study 3 to investigate the long-term reattachment rate of PnR was a retrospective study published in 1993. Among 57 patients who achieved successful retinal reattachment 1 month postoperatively after a single operation with PnR, 9.7% developed new retinal tears after a followup period of 5.5 years. However, it was not reported if the tears were accompanied by a new retinal detachment, as well as the number of patients with successful attachment at 1 year but subsequent redetachment at a later follow-up. In 1998, a nonrandomized prospective study 4 was published, with a small sample size of 29 patients who achieved retinal reattachment at 12 months after one successful surgical intervention with only PnR for primary RRD. These patients were followed for 7 years, and redetachment occurred in 6% of the cases >2.5 years after the original procedure. 4 In light of the limited data regarding long-term follow-up of patients undergoing PnR, there is still debate over the long-term anatomical reattachment rates of PnR, with some advocating against the procedure because the retina may subsequently redetach over the ensuing years.

This study aimed to assess long-term redetachment rates of patients with a minimum follow-up of 2 years who underwent PnR and PPV in the PIVOT trial, a prospective randomized clinical trial performed with rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria.

### Methods

Conducted as a post hoc analysis, this study examined data from the PIVOT randomized clinical trial in patients with primary RRD referred to the Department of Ophthalmology at St. Michael ’ s Hospital, Toronto, Canada, between January 2012 and January 2017. Ethics Committee approval was obtained, all participants provided informed consent, and the study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Most patients continued to follow-up with their primary retinal surgeon after the first year of the study and were enrolled in the long-term extension study. To be eligible for this post hoc analysis, PIVOT participants could not have had any reintervention to reattach the retina within 1 year of the initial study procedure, as those results have already been reported. Additionally, only patients with a minimum follow-up of 2 years were included in this study. Subsequently, redetachment was determined by electronic medical chart review or direct telephone interview. The number of years from presentation to the last documented follow-up was obtained for each patient. For participants with > 6 years of documented follow-up, redetachment was determined using the electronic medical chart alone. For those with 2 to 5 years of documented follow-up,first a chart review was conducted, and then patients were called to enquire about any subsequent reintervention. For those with < 2 years of documented follow-up, only direct contact via phone call was accepted. Some patients were documented as unreachable and excluded from the final analysis if they could not be reached by telephone.

During the telephone interview, several questions were posed to the participant. First, they were asked whether they had experienced a retinal detachment in their speci fied study eye since the last documented follow-up date. A more speci fic query followed, inquiring whether they had been to an operating theater for any reason related to their study eye since the last follow-up date. Participants were also asked if they had any procedures scheduled for their study eye and whether they had any comments or concerns speci fically about their study eye.

#### Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed with Jamovi for MacOS version 2.0.0 (The Jamovi Project) and SPSS statistics 19 for Windows (SPSS Inc, IBM). Normally and nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean standard deviation or median values (interquartile range), respectively. Categorical variables are presented as absolute values and percentages. A 2-tailed t test was used for statistical significance, with an a of 0.05.

### Results

One hundred seventy-six patients were recruited in the PIVOT trial, with 88 participants in each arm (PPV and PnR). There were 4 ineligible PPV participants (who underwent reintervention during the first year) vs. 11 PnR participants (9 reinterventions and 2 patients who withdrew from the study). Of the 84 eligible PPV participants, 23 were unreachable, leaving 72.6% (61/84) that were included in the PPV group for this study. Of these, 59.0% (36/61) of patients were analyzed with electronic medical chart review and 41.0% (25/61) with phone calls. Of the 77 eligible PnR participants, 15 were unreachable, leaving 62 (80.5%) that were included in the PnR group of this study. Of these, 51.2% (32/62) of patients were assessed through electronic medical chart review and 48.4% (30/62) by phone calls. The mean age of patients included in the PPV group was 68.4 7.74 years and in the PnR group was 69.3 9.74 years.

The mean follow-up duration of these patients in years was 5.43 3.60 vs. 5.51 3.03 in the PPV and PnR groups, respectively. Of the 61 patients included in the PPV arm, 52.5% (32/61) had consecutive visits for 4 years after the initial procedure, and of the 62 patients included in the PnR arm, 54.8% (34/62) of patients had consecutive visits for 4 years. The long-term redetachment rate, resulting from combined chart and telephone data, was 0% and 1.61% (1/62) in the PPV and PnR groups, respectively, with no statistically signi ficant difference between the groups ( P = 0.32). The only patient that had an RRD recurrence in the PnR group was a 68-year-old man who presented with photopsia associated with a visual field defect 6 years after the primary procedure in his left eye. Of note, he had undergone PPV for symptomatic floaters after the initial successful PnR, after which the retina redetached. His macula remained attached throughout, and he maintained a final visual acuity of 20/30. Summary tables of the results of the primary analysis are shown in Figures 1 and 2 .

### Discussion

This post hoc analysis of the PIVOT trial extension study found that the long-term redetachment rate assessed on average 4.5 years after primary RRD was extremely low and similar between the PPV and PnR groups. Both PPV and PnR proved to be durable long-term treatment options with 0% and 1.61% redetachment rates, respectively. To date, limited knowledge has been available on the long-term reattachment rates after PnR for primary RRDs.

Previous studies assessing the outcomes after PnR primarily focused on the initial 6 months after the procedure, as the risk of detachment is highest within the first months, 5 more specifically, 13% to 17% within the first 3 months 6,7 and around 10% within the first year. 7 The closer to the procedure, the higher the redetachment risk. 8 In a retrospective study including 289 patients, 76% of the redetachments occurred within the first 4 weeks after the PnR. Mudvari et al 7 blamed the occurrence of new breaks as the main reason for short-time redetachment. Freyler and Radax 3 found the occurrence of new breaks is significantly higher in the first 3 months, which decreases to around 6% in the first 5 years after successful reattachment with PnR at 12 months. Although the risk of new breaks in the literature varies from 7% to 22%, 7 how many of these are new rather than missed breaks? Missed breaks may explain the higher occurrence of redetachments early in the postoperative course, and their incidence is likely underestimated.

However, in studies with a longer follow-up duration, redetachment rates seem to be much lower. After successful reattachment with PnR at 12 months, Lisle et al 4 found a redetachment rate of 6% up to a 7-year follow-up. Tornambe 6 reported redetachment rates of 1% beyond 12 months for both PnR and scleral buckle (SB). It is interesting to remember that differences in the procedure technique may impact its success rate, as previously noted by Tornambe, 6 which may account for some of the variability of the reported redetachment rates in the literature. After reviewing 310 eyes treated with PnR over 10 years of experience, Tornambe found that complete peripheral retinopexy signi ficantly impacted the success rate, for example. 7 Differences in the PIVOT trial technique may have positively impacted the long-term redetachment rate, which in our case was also very low (1.6%).

The disparity between the redetachment rate reported in our PnR study arm and prior retrospective reports may arise because of the pretreatment of lattice and holes in the attached retina, as well as strict follow-up schedules, 1 which were not mentioned in older studies. We recognize that another limiting factor is that not all participants from the initial PIVOT trial were incorporated into our analysis, as some individuals were not reachable. Consequently, there is a possibility that some redetachments were missed in our analysis. Even so, taken together with our results, findings from previous studies suggest that long-term redetachment rates after PnR are low and comparable to other surgical options.

One retrospective analysis of 155 eyes with a mean follow-up of > 5 years found that the long-term redetachment rate for SB and PPV cases, combined or not, was 3.9%. It is important to note that 3.2% of the eyes had silicone oil at the last follow-up examination. 9 Another prospective study of patients who underwent PPV found a 7.1% redetachment rate within a 30-month period, with all redetachments occurring within the first 3 months, and a final anatomic reattachment rate of 98.6% after reintervention. 10 Additionally, a retrospective chart review of phakic eyes with primary RRD treated with SB found a redetachment rate of 5.7% within 4 years after the initial procedure. 11 Those studies show a higher redetachment rate than what was observed in our PPV group, although it is important to note that this was from nonrandomized data outside of a clinical trial. Patient selection bias impacts success and redetachment rates due to the complexity of the detachments and risk factors for the development of proliferative vitreoretinopathy and other postoperative complications.

Another misconception regarding long-term anatomical results in PnR cases relates to vitreous traction. Whereas SB uses a silicone exoplant to create a permanent scleral indentationtorelievevitreoustractionaroundthecausativetearsand modify the dynamics of the vitreous currents, PPV reduces traction through surgical removal of the vitreous itself. 12 However, PnR does not involve the removal of vitreous traction, but rather the creation of a chorioretinal adhesion induced by laser or cryopexy that can overcome the tractional forces on the retina by the vitreous. This can be successfully achieved in multiple clinical scenarios, even in complex cases, such as using PnR for RRD caused by multiple retinal tears, retinal dialysis, and large or giant retinal breaks. 13 This suggests that laser retinopexy can create a strong long-term adhesion around the break and overcome the counteracting vitreous tractional forces permanently in that location. Horseshoe tears (without detachment) with persistent vitreous traction at their edges are another example of how laser alone may overcome vitreous traction. Most horseshoe tears that are treated do not subsequently detach, suggesting that laser alone is suf ficient to overcome vitreous traction and that removal of vitreous traction or counteracting vitreous tractionwithaSB is likely notrequired in most cases.

The decision to carry out a PnR rather than a PPV is rooted mainly in the minimal invasiveness of the procedure and the functional recovery. Compared with PPV, PnR is associated with improved visual acuity, reduced vertical metamorphopsia and aniseikonia, reduced morbidity, and higher scores in mental health and vision-related functioning. 1 , 14 Retinal displacement after PPV is also much more frequent and severe when compared with PnR. 15

Studies comparing PnR to PPV and SB separately found cataract formation and the subsequent need for surgery to be much lower with PnR. 16 , 17 In addition to superior functional outcomes and improved vision-related quality of life in the first 6 months, PnR is also a more cost-effective treatment option. Estimates suggest a savings of 50.9% compared with SB and 59.4% compared with PPV. 18 , 19 Even greater savings would be found when considering the long-term costs associated with the higher percentage of eyes that require cataract extraction after PPV. 18 Ultimately, even in the context of redetachment after PnR, there were no differences in the anatomic and functional outcomes between eyes with primary reattachment and those that failed and required an additional procedure. 20 Thus, using PnR as a first-line treatment is advantageous for both patients and the health care system in the long-term.

### Conclusion

This post hoc analysis from the PIVOT randomized trial extension study with long-term data demonstrated very low long-term redetachment rates for both PnR and PPV and no statistically signi ficant difference between them. Patients who underwent PPV were followed for 5.43 3.60 years and those who underwent PnR were followed for 5.51 3.03 years. Both procedures are durable treatment options for RRD over an extended period, rarely requiring additional intervention for redetachment. These results demonstrate that relieving or eliminating vitreous traction with PPV or SB is not necessarily required to achieve long-term reattachment for most RRDs.
