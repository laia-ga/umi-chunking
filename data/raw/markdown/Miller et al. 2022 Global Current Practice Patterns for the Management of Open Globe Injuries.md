Ocular traumaisaleading cause of single-eye blindness in the United States of America (USA).1 Further, the global impact of eye injuries is significant. Nearly 55 million eye injuries occur per year worldwide, causing decreased visual acuity for 19 million people and blindness for 1.6 million people.2 The global incidence of open globe injuries (OGI) is estimated to be 3.5 per 100,000 people.2 Certain demographic factors have been associated with increased risk for traumatic ophthalmic injuries, such as male sex, industrial occupations, and lower socioeconomic status.3–5 There are few available estimates for the impact of these injuries in low- and middle-income countries2; for this reason, the true prevalence of OGI is believed to be underestimated.

Currently, management of OGI varies based on regional, or more commonly, institutional guidelines. The Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology System (BETTS), an internationally accepted system for the classification of open and closed globe injuries, was developed in 1996.6,7 BETTS contributed positively to the management of ophthalmic trauma by standardizing their classification but is limited in

While individual institutions and research collaborations have evaluated different therapeutic modalities for these injuries,13–15 controversies around practice patterns and standards of care persist.9

Variations in clinical practice are not inherently disadvantageous. For example, personalized medicine, which innately diverges among patients, allows for novel therapeutic approaches and improved patient responses to treatment.16 However, clinical variations must be thoroughly vetted to ensure the differences in practice are equivalent in benefit for patients or are alternatively justifiable.17 Variations in care that remain uninvestigated may unwittingly lead to inequities in access to care, differences in patient outcomes, and inefficient resource use.17

In this study, we summarize the results of an international survey of ophthalmic institutions on preferred management paradigms for OGI. We aim to establish comprehensive and generally accepted principles for the management of OGI and highlight areas where variations in clinical management exist worldwide.

## METHODS

• SURVEY DEVELOPMENT: The survey was administered online via the Qualtrics (SAP) platform between August 13, 2020, and January 8, 2021. Survey questions were developed based on PPP guidance released by the Academy of Ophthalmology (when guidance was relevant)10–12,18,19 in combination with institutional practices at the Wilmer Eye Institute of the Johns Hopkins Hospital (Baltimore, MD, USA), Wills Eye Hospital (Philadelphia, PA, USA), Bascom Palmer Eye Institute (Miami, FL, USA), and the University Hospitals Birmingham (Birmingham, United Kingdom).

The survey was designed to capture key aspects of OGI management. The survey content was then reviewed and refined by members of the American Society for Ophthalmic Trauma (ASOT) (authors Y.Y., K.C., G.A.J, and F.A.W.), including cornea and retina experts, the International Globe and Adnexal Trauma Epidemiology Study (IGATES) steering committee (R.A., F.A.W., and A.H.), members of the Asia Pacific Ophthalmic Trauma Society (APOTS) (R.A.), and other international eye trauma experts (R.B.). The final survey contained 123 questions, including 26 text entry queries. Of these, 58 questions pertained to OGI management or institution characteristics (Supplemental Document). Zone 1 OGI were considered to involve the cornea and limbus; zone 2 OGI were considered to involve the anterior of the sclera up to 5 mm beyond the limbus; and zone 3 OGI were considered to involve the sclera more than 5 mm posterior to the limbus.8

- • SURVEY DISTRIBUTION: The survey was distributed by email invitation to ophthalmologists specializing in ophthalmic trauma with up to 3 follow-up emails per expert/location. Invitees were instructed to respond on behalf of their institution’s practices rather than their personal preferences. Invited experts were members or affiliates of APOTS and ASOT. Institutions treating a substantial number of ocular trauma cases annually (determined as treating >12 OGI per year based on survey responses) were included in the final analysis.
- • ETHICAL APPROVAL: This study was reviewed by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board and deemed exempt due to it incurring minimal risk to subjects. This study did not collect patient information and adhered to all laws in the state of Maryland. All study activities adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Respondents provided informed consent.
- • DATA ANALYSIS: Data were analyzed using Excel (Microsoft, Inc.) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, 2017) software. Fisher exact testing and χ2 testing compared the proportions of experts selecting each management paradigm as relevant. P values of <.05 were considered significant. All proportions described are shown as percentages of all responding experts, unless otherwise specified. If a range of numerical answers was provided (such as 7-10 days for a duration of therapy), the mean of the range was used for analysis. Mean data are shown with the SD. All time periods relative to operations (such as preoperatively, intraoperatively, or postoperatively) refer to time relative to the OGI repair unless otherwise specified.

## DISCUSSION

While it was previously understood that controversies persist in the management of ophthalmic trauma, 9 the data reported in this study highlight that current practice patterns of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative management of OGI vary. Survey responses diverged in all areas of OGI management, including the use of prophylactic antibiotics and steroids, as well as procedural decisions. Previous investigations have demonstrated that there is variation in visual outcomes after OGI repair, such as in visual acuity and endophthalmitis prevalence. 20–22 Expert consensus guidelines may thus help reduce unnecessary variation in practice and therefore in visual outcomes.

Some initial treatments for OGI, such as antibiotic therapy, are aimed at the prevention of traumatic endophthalmitis that can lead to irreparable vision loss. 23 In the context of OGI, endophthalmitis is infectious in origin and is most often caused by Bacillus species and Staphylococcus epidermidis . 24 However, the preferred routes of administration and antibiotic(s) used vary. In setting of OGI, North and Gardiner 25 recommended initiating 48 hours of IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as vancomycin and ceftazidime (a third-generation cephalosporin), to cover gram-positive bacteria on presentation (this regimen would not, however, delay surgery). 20 Another investigation found that the IV administration of a fourth-generation cephalosporin, cefepime, along with vancomycin was also effective in preventing endophthalmitis (endophthalmitis rate of 0.9%). 26 A separate randomized controlled trial found that the combination oral ciprofloxacin and IV cefazolin, a first-generation cephalosporin, and the combination of oral ciprofloxacin and oral cefuroxime did not differ significantly in preventing endophthalmitis. 27 The rates of IOFB = intraocular foreign body, NLP = no light perception, OGI = open globe injury, PGY = postgraduation year, h = hours.

While 75.8% of survey experts (n = 25/33) in the present study reported administering preoperative systemic antibiotics, the vast majority differed from the regimens in the above trials. A retrospective medical record review of OGI repairs published in 2009 described the results of one institution’s OGI management protocol. The study reported a low prevalence of posttraumatic endophthalmitis (0.9%) over 7 years after using a discrete regimen of IV vancomycin and ceftazidime preoperatively and postoperatively, as well as postoperative topical prednisolone and topical moxifloxacin or ofloxacin and cycloplegics.20 These data may support the value of a standardized protocol in preventing posttraumatic complications.35

Further study into the optimal antibiotic regimen and administration routes is warranted to minimize infection risk, maximize visual outcomes, and resolve discrepancies in the literature and in real-world practice. Additionally, the development of high-level guidelines that consolidate investigative findings and educational campaigns targeted at eye care physicians worldwide are needed.

In addition to antibiotic management, our results indicated the optimal timing of OGI repair or IOFB removal is controversial. It was previously demonstrated that delaying repair more than 24 hours after OGI significantly increases the rate of posttraumatic endophthalmitis and that waiting even 12 hours may increase risk.24,36 In addition, Blanch and associates37 in 2019 found that for every 24 hours that repair was delayed, resulting visual acuity decreased. This relationship corroborated other studies,38 although it was disputed in a smaller data set.39 In contrast to the risks of delaying OGI repair, out-of-hours surgery is associated with increased surgical morbidity and mortality in other areas, although no comparable study has been published in ophthalmology.40

We observed discrepancies in how the responding institutions prioritize OGI, with many (n = 20 [60.6%]) waiting until the morning for the repair if an OGI presented in the middle of the night. This trend persisted for IOFB cases (n = 15 [45.5%]), although 6 experts (18.2%) indicated that certain types of IOFB, such as copper or organic material, would be considered more urgent. Experts in the USA on average treated IOFBs more urgently (n = 9 [81.8%] operate as soon as possible, including overnight), while only 3 experts (13.6%) from international institutions did so (Table 4). The difference in urgency of IOFB removal between the USA and international centers was significantly different (P < .01). Further investigation is needed to definitively determine the necessaryurgency with which OGI should be repaired to minimize patient risk.

In addition to infection and endophthalmitis, sympathetic ophthalmia (SO) is a devastating potential sequela of OGI that can lead to granulomatous panuveitis in not only the traumatized eye but also the contralateral side.41 Whether to prophylactically enucleate or eviscerate ruptured globes to prevent SO is debated.42–44 More USA centers in our study preferred aggressive approaches, including enucleation and evisceration, for the management of eyes with NLP after OGI compared with nonUSA centers (P = .04) (Table 4). This may be secondary to increased access to highly specialized oculoplastic surgeons and high-quality implants. However, studies have shown that in many cases, primary enucleation to prevent SO may be unnecessary42 and that evisceration may be a preferable surgery.45 Randomized and controlled clinical investigations are needed to ascertain whether the risk of SO after OGI outweighs the benefits of preserving the native tissue and what surgical method is optimal.

Additionally, further investigation is needed to understand the definitive role of imaging in the evaluation of OGIs. Previous studies have found the benefits of obtaining CT scans on suspected OGI to be variable. CT can be a valuable in screening for occult OGI cases, IOFB, orbital fractures, and vitreous hemorrhage, particularly where the initial assessment is completed by nonspecialists.46,47 Despite this, it is not clear whether the data gained from the scan provide material information for the initial management of OGI.48 However, conclusions made from the imaging at presentation may be more valuable after the initial repair of the OGI.48 We observed considerable variation in CT protocols for evaluation of suspected OGI, with 48.5% of responding trauma centers (n = 16/33) obtaining CT imaging for all suspected OGI cases. All of the responding institutions from Africa (n = 3/3 [100%]) and many in Asia (n = 10/12 [83.3%]) are more conservative in their approach and only obtain CT imaging for certain indications, such as for suspected IOFB, whereas 10 of 12 (83.3%) in North America and 3 of 4 (75.0%) in South America obtain CTs for all suspected open globes. This paradigm may explain why fewer USA centers obtained B-scans preoperatively when there was no view to the posterior segment of the eye (n = 3 [27.3%] regularly obtained B-scans in the USA, while n = 12 [54.5%] did so internationally) (Table 4), as CT imaging may have been already performed. Because it is especially important for low- and middle-income nations to optimize cost-effectiveness of ophthalmic care,49 evidenced-based guidelines on the role of CT imaging in the initial evaluation of OGI are crucial to ensure highvalue care for patients worldwide. The data shown here may also reflect discrepancies in the availabilities of CT scans.

Lastly, important differences in the roles of residents, fellows, and trainees were elucidated between centers in the USA and internationally. In particular, residents and trainees in the USA were able to participate in the evaluation and repair of OGI earlier in their ophthalmology training than in centers outside the USA ( Table 4 ), with residents at all responding USA institutions participating in surgical repairs (100.0% of USA centers [n = 11] vs 40.0% of non-USA centers [n = 8]) ( Table 4 ). This finding is on trend with another report which revealed that in the United Kingdom, more than 42.5% of surveyed residents had not repaired a traumatic corneal laceration, and 60.0% had not repaired an OGI. 50 Despite earlier participation in OGI management by USA residents, only 64.5% surveyed in a separate investigation reported being “comfortable” managing orbital trauma. 51 Further investigation into the effects of early participation by residents and trainees in OGI evaluation and surgery on patient outcomes and trainee preparedness is needed to optimize training programs while preserving standards of care.

There are several limitations to consider while interpreting results from this study. While the study team attempted to recruit a diverse array of experts, the sample size is limited, and certain geographic regions are underrepresented or missing. For example, we only had 1 included response each from Australia and Europe.
