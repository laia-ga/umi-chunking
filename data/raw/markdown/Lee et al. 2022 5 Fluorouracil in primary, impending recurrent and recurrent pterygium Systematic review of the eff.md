A B S T R A C T

Pterygium is an ultraviolet-related disease characterized by an aberrant, wing-shaped and active wound-healing process. There is nothing quite as disheartening for the surgeon or patient as the recurrence of pterygium, and various adjuvants have been studied to ameliorate this. This systematic review provides a comprehensive summary of the efficacy and safety of 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) as an antimetabolite agent for pterygium management. An appraisal of electronic searches of six databases identified 34 clinical studies reporting recurrence outcomes of 5-FU use in primary, impending recurrent and recurrent pterygia. In vitro and in vivo studies of 5-FU showed doseand duration-dependent cytostatic and cytotoxic effects in human cells. 5-FU is relatively inexpensive, available, and easy to administer, making it attractive for resource-limited scenarios. However, the published evidence demonstrates a recurrence rate of 11.4 – 60% with the bare scleral technique, 3.5 – 35.8% with conjunctival rotational flaps, 3.7 – 9.6% with conjunctival autografts for intraoperative topical 5-FU, and 14 – 35.8% for preoperative and intraoperative injections. This suboptimal efficacy brings the role of 5-FU as an adjuvant for pterygium surgery into question and the authors do not recommend its use. In contrast, postoperative intralesional injections of 5-FU to arrest progression in impending recurrent pterygium and true recurrent pterygia were more promising, with success rates of 87.2 – 100% and 75 – 100%, respectively. Furthermore, 5-FU as a treatment modality, without surgery, effectively arrested progression in 81.3 – 96% of primary and recurrent pterygia. Other treatments such as topical and intralesional corticosteroids, cyclosporine and anti-VEGF agents are discussed. Complications of 5-FU increase with higher doses and range from transient and reversible to severe and sight-threatening. For pterygium, 5-FU has a predilection for causing scleral thinning, corneal toxicity, and graft-related complications. Additional study with extended follow-up is needed to elucidate the optimal dose, frequency, duration, and long-term safety of 5-FU injections. If 5-FU is used in the management of pterygium, it should be with caution, in selected patients and with vigilant long-term monitoring.

## 1. Introduction

Pterygium, the most obvious of the ultraviolet-related diseases of the eye (the ophthalmohelioses), has been characterized as an aberrant, wing-shaped, active wound healing process, with centripetal growth of a leading edge of altered limbal epithelial cells [ 1 ]. This is followed by the development of a squamous metaplastic epithelium with goblet cell hyperplasia and an underlying stroma of activated, proliferating fibroblasts, neovascularization, inflammatory cells and extracellular matrix remodeling [ 2 ]. Conceptually, surgery-associated exacerbation or reactivation of this active process may better explain the process of recurrence of pterygium, compared with the classical degenerative view associated with degradation of Bowman ’ s layer and elastosis [ 3 ]. The role of chronic ultraviolet exposure in the pathogenesis of pterygium is supported by epidemiological studies and its clinical associations with other UV-related conditions, including cataract, climatic droplet keratopathy, photo-aged skin, squamous cell and basal cell carcinoma [ 4 , 5 ], melanoma [ 6 ] and a predilection in inherited disease of DNA repair [ 7 ,

8]. Other contributory factors for pterygium location and laterality identified in previous studies include factors influencing focusing of peripheral light by the anterior segment such as fixation [9,10], ocular dominance and dominant eye closure in sunlight [11], corneal curvature, anterior chamber depth [12], and patient handedness [13].

The aim of pterygium management is to restore ocular surface anatomy, function, cosmesis and alleviate associated symptoms [2]. To achieve these aims, the authors have advocated for reconstructive surgical techniques such as the gold standard of excision combined with a limbal-conjunctival autograft [2,3,14]. However, there is nothing quite as disheartening for the surgeon or patient than the recurrence of pterygium, most likely to occur within the first 12 months after surgery [3, 15]. Pterygium recurrence is characterized by fibrovascular invasion and inflammation [16]. While conjunctival and corneal (where the limbus is broached and cornea invaded) recurrences have been defined, a consensus on recurrence definition has not been reached [3]. Once recurrence is clinically evident, medical interventions are the initial mainstay of therapy to ameliorate, if not halt, progression [3,14,17]. Potential management options include corticosteroids [18–20], non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [2], cyclosporine [21–25], anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) [26–28] and antimetabolites [29].

5-Fluourouracil (5-FU) is an antimetabolite used in the armamentarium of pterygium management [30]. Clinical studies have applied 5-FU topically or via injection as a surgical adjuvant to prevent pterygium recurrence [31–35]. More recently, there has been increasing interest in the administration of intralesional subconjunctival injections of 5-FU as a treatment modality to arrest progression of primary, impending recurrent and recurrent pterygia [18,34,36–45]. However, 5-FU is not without side effects owing to its cytotoxic nature, and the potential for short- and long-term complications needs to be recognized by clinicians. To the best of our knowledge, no review has focused solely on the application, efficacy, and safety of 5-FU in pterygium management.

This systematic review aimed to provide a comprehensive summary of clinical studies using 5-FU for pterygium. The recurrence rates of 5-FU as a surgical adjuvant and intralesional subconjunctival injection were examined. Furthermore, the safety profile and complications of 5-FU in the management of pterygium were detailed. Based on this evidence, the authors provide recommendations on the current and future roles of 5FU in the management of pterygium.

## 2. Method of literature search

### 2.1. Search strategy and study selection

Electronic searches were conducted using the Ovid Medline, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, Scopus, Global Health and Web of Science Core Collection databases from their inception date to July 2022 without language restrictions. The search terms (pterygium OR pterygia) AND (5-fluorouracil OR fluorouracil OR 5-FU OR antimetabolite) were combined as keywords. This was supplemented by searching the reference lists of review articles and all potentially relevant studies.

Two reviewers (BWHL and AS) independently screened the titles and abstracts of articles identified in the search. Full-text manuscripts were subsequently reviewed if either reviewer considered the article potentially eligible. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (MTC).

2.2. Eligibility criteria the literature and experience surrounding the application of 5-FU in pterygium. 5-FU could have been applied at any dose, administered topically or via intralesional subconjunctival injection, with or without surgery, in the pre-, intra-, or post-operative period. 5-FU intervention was categorized into (1) surgical adjuvant to prevent recurrence (topical or intralesional subconjunctival injection), (2) postoperative intralesional injection for impending recurrent (conjunctival) or recurrent pterygium (with corneal involvement), and (3) sole treatment modality for primary and recurrent pterygia. Studies were limited to those involving human subjects and were omitted if an English full-text manuscript could not be obtained. Review articles, conference proceedings and abstracts, editorials, expert opinions, and grey literature were excluded. When institutions published duplicate studies with accumulating numbers of patients or increased length of follow-up, only the most complete reports were included.

### 2.3. Data extraction

All data were independently extracted from the full text, tables and figures by two investigators (BWHL and AS), and any discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (MTC). The primary outcomes examined were recurrence rate and complications. Other data extracted for each study included authors, year, country, study design, pterygium type and number, surgical technique, 5-FU dosing regimen and administration method, control groups, sample size, follow-up period, and recurrence definition.

### 2.4. Results of systematic review

A total of 1135 unique records was identified through electronic searches using six online databases and a review of the reference lists. Of these, 1072 records were excluded after screening for title and abstract content. Following the appraisal of the full texts of the remaining 62 records, 34 studies met the inclusion criteria. This was summarized in a flowchart following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.

The use of 5-FU as a surgical adjuvant in pterygium was examined in 20 (16 topical applications, 4 injections) studies (Table 1). 5-FU was studied as an intralesional subconjunctival injection in the postoperative setting to manage impending recurrent pterygium (4 reports), true recurrent pterygium (6 reports), and as the sole treatment modality for primary and recurrent pterygia (4 reports) (Table 2).

## 3. 5-Fluorouracil

5-FU was first synthesized by Duschinsky et al., in 1957 [46]. Following its first application in ophthalmic practice in 1982 [47], the earliest published use of 5-FU in pterygium appears to be by Radda and Grasl in 1989 [48] and then Carlentini et al., in 1991 [49]. More recently, its use appears to have fallen out of vogue, with a 2019 practice pattern survey of 199 cornea specialists reporting that 0% and 1.7% routinely utilized 5-FU in the management of primary, and early recurrent pterygia, respectively [50]. Furthermore, the role and safety of 5-FU were not discussed in a 2013 Ophthalmic Technology Assessment by Kaufman et al. from the American Academy of Ophthalmology that examined adjuvants in pterygium surgery [29]. Despite this, 5-FU continues to be utilized worldwide, particularly in scenarios where a paucity of health resources or surgical expertise may pose barriers in pterygium management [18,41,42,45,51–55]. This may be because 5-FU is relatively inexpensive, easy to administer, has no requirement for refrigeration, and is readily available [30,53].

The degree of antimetabolite effect is dose- and duration-dependent, with higher concentrations also disrupting RNA synthesis and the actin cytoskeleton [57,58]. Exposing the ocular surface to 5-FU promotes apoptosis of Tenon’s capsule fibroblasts and impedes proliferation of conjunctival fibroblasts and corneal epithelial cells [58–60]. ### 3.2. Ocular in vitro and in vivo studies

Both in vitro and in vivo studies have confirmed that 5-FU, especially with multiple or prolonged exposures, is cytostatic or cytotoxic to ocular cells. Khaw et al. demonstrated that a single intraoperative 5-min exposure to 5-FU (25 mg/ml) resulted in a 50% reduction in human Tenon’s capsule fibroblasts, and that continuous exposure (1 mg/ml) for greater than three days led to death of the entire Tenon’s capsule fibroblast population over a 30-day period [58,60]. 5-FU has also been shown to delay epithelial healing significantly in rabbit corneas [64] and to prevent recovery of subconjunctival and scleral fibroblasts for several days [65]. Nuyts et al. showed that 5-FU (50 mg/ml) caused corneal endothelial toxicity after 30 min of incubation in an in-vitro bovine system [66]. Huhtala et al. found that exposing SV40-immortalized human corneal epithelial cell cultures to 5-FU (5 mg/ml) resulted in a 50% reduction by 24 h and complete cell death by 72 h [67]. Midena et al. reported that 1% 5-FU (10 mg/ml) was shown to have time-dependent cytotoxic changes in human corneal epithelial cells [68]. Even lower doses of 5-FU (0.5, 1, and 5 mg/ml) were found to decrease cell viability and disrupt mitochondrial transmembrane potential, inducing cellular apoptosis of corneal epithelial stem cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner [69]. Recently, an in-vitro study by Gallo et al. demonstrated that 5-FU killed >50% of human limbal stem cells at a concentration of 160 μM [70]. The results of these in vivo and in vitro studies indicate that 5-FU is capable of considerable toxicity to corneal cells, and raises concerns regarding its potential for long-term toxicity.

## 4. Efficacy of 5-fluorouracil as a surgical adjuvant in preventing pterygium recurrence

### 4.1. Topical application in bare scleral technique

The published recurrence rates following intraoperative topical application of 5-FU in primary and recurrent pterygia range from 11.4 to 60% when combined with the bare scleral technique ( Table 1 ) [ 33 , 35 , 51 , 52 , 61 , 71 – 77 ]. After surgical excision, 5-FU is typically applied intraoperatively with a sponge soaked in solution over the region of the bare sclera, and over the surrounding conjunctiva and Tenon ’ s space in some studies. Following this, most studies have reported rinsing the area with a balanced salt solution to minimize excessive residual 5-FU. The concentration and duration of 5-FU application used in previous studies ranged from 10 to 50 mg/ml and 1 – 5 min, respectively.

The first prospective randomized, doubled-masked, placebocontrolled trial of 5-FU, by Maldonado et al., was unencouraging, with a recurrence rate of 60% compared with 35% for placebo (p = 0.11) [ 31 ]. In this study, 5-FU (10 mg/ml, 5 min) was applied intraoperatively following excision of primary pterygium with the bare scleral technique [ 31 ]. Moreover, two patients who underwent bilateral pterygium excision only had recurrence in the eyes that received 5-FU. Maldonado et al. concluded that this low concentration of 5-FU was insufficient to prevent recurrence. However, in contrast to the earlier assertion that low concentrations are ineffective, Hassan et al., in 2019 reported a recurrence rate of 12.8% in a prospective series of 5-FU (5 mg/ml, 5 min) undergoing bare scleral technique with a mean follow up of 43 months [ 71 ].

Following Maldonado et al. ’ s study, higher concentrations of 5-FU (25 to 50 mg/ml) were investigated. Majeed et al. reported a lower recurrence rate of 32.5% for primary pterygium randomized to either 5FU (25 mg/ml, 5 min) and bare scleral technique, compared with 40% for the bare scleral technique alone. Two larger published series of 125 and 75 primary pterygium treated with intraoperative 5-FU (25 mg/mL, 5 min) reported pterygia recurred in 35.8% and 33.3% of cases, respectively [ 78 , 79 ]. Additional studies with smaller sample sizes at this dose and duration of 5-FU have reported recurrence rates of 25.9% and 27.6% [ 35 , 72 ]. While bare scleral technique with adjuvant 5-FU carries lower rates than bare scleral technique alone [ 33 , 61 , 72 , 78 ], the latter is recognized to have unacceptable recurrence rates and has been superseded in pterygium surgery [ 2 , 3 , 14 , 25 ]. Furthermore, Bekibele et al. showed that at this dose, 5-FU was inferior even to β -irradiation at preventing recurrence when both were used as adjuvants to bare scleral technique (25.9% vs. 22.5%) [ 35 ]. Akarsu et al. reported that a reduced intraoperative application duration of 5-FU (25 mg/ml, 3 min) retained a comparable recurrence rate of 25%. However, this study utilized excision with a conjunctival transpositional flap, which is recognized to have a lower recurrence rate than bare scleral technique [ 34 ]. While higher doses of 5-FU (50 mg/mL, 3 to 5 min) combined with bare scleral technique further decreased recurrence rates to around 11 to 20% [ 52 , 61 , 74 ], there was still some variability, with 52% recurrence reported in one study [ 76 ]. Furthermore, Bekibele et al. demonstrated that excision with conjunctival autograft alone was noninferior to bare scleral technique combined with 5-FU (50 mg/ml, 5 min) (12.1% vs. 11.4% recurrence, p > 0.05) [ 74 ].

### 4.2. Topical application in conjunctival rotational flap and conjunctival autograft

5-FU has also been investigated as an adjuvant to pterygium excision combined with conjunctival transpositional flap [32,34,56] or conjunctival autograft [51,53,80,81]. The published recurrence rates range from 3.5 to 35.8% and 3.7 to 9.6%, respectively (Table 1). Silva et al. conducted a prospective controlled trial of 120 patients with bilateral primary pterygium randomized to undergo excision with a conjunctival transpositional flap alone or with intraoperative 5-FU (25 mg/ml, 5 min). This study demonstrated the recurrence rate decreased from 25.5% to 5.8% with 5-FU use [56]. Similarly, Schellini et al. reported a recurrence rate of 3.5% in a prospective case series of primary and recurrent pterygia undergoing conjunctival transpositional flap combined with 5-FU (25 mg/ml, 5 min) [32]. However, both these studies were limited by a short follow-up period of three months. This may have skewed the results by missing further recurrences, which typically occur in the first 12 postoperative months [3,15]. In two studies by Bekibele et al. with follow-up of more than 12 months, 5-FU (50 mg/ml, 5 min) combined with conjunctival autograft had recurrence rates of 3.7% and 8.7% [53,81]. Interestingly, two studies reported that 5-FU was more effective as an antimetabolite than MMC when combined with conjunctival autograft (8.7% vs. 11.8%) [81]. Furthermore, 5-FU was as effective as a single subconjunctival injection of bevacizumab (0.05 ml of 0.5 mg) when combined with conjunctival autograft (3.7% vs. 3.9%) [53]. The effect in conjunctival transpositional flap and conjunctival autograft appears to be sustained even at lower 5-FU doses as shown by Kuldeep et al. who reported recurrence rates of 7.7% and 8.3% for 10 and 20 mg/ml applied for 2 min, respectively [80].

### 4.3. Preoperative and intraoperative intralesional subconjunctival injections as a surgical adjuvant

Only one study has investigated the efficacy of preoperative intralesional 5-FU injections to prevent pterygium recurrence following surgery [82]. In this prospective series, Shiratori et al. injected a single 5 mg dose in 53 patients undergoing excision with a conjunctival transpositional flap at 30 or 10 days preoperatively. The results were not encouraging, and by 60 days of follow-up, recurrence rates of 60% and 39.3% were observed, respectively. This study suggests that a single preoperative 5-FU injection is not sufficient to prevent pterygium recurrence.

Single 5-FU injections given as an intraoperative adjuvant also have limited efficacy with reported recurrence rates of 14 to 35.8% [52,55, 79]. Meneghim et al. reported a recurrence rate of 29% in a prospective case series using a single 5-FU injection (5 mg) combined with conjunctival transpositional flap [55]. In this study, the addition of a short course of cyclosporine (0.05%) administered 10 days pre- and postoperatively was ineffective and had a higher recurrence rate (40%). Valezi et al. used a single subconjunctival injection of 5-FU (5 mg) combined with intraoperative 5-FU (25 mg/ml, 5 min) and conjunctival transpositional flap in 125 patients, and reported a recurrence rate of 35.8% [79]. Ibrahim and Nada directly compared a single intraoperative intralesional injection of 5-FU (25 mg) with topical application (50 mg/ml, 5 min) as adjuvants to the bare scleral technique, and found comparable recurrence rates of 14% to 20% (p > 0.05), slightly in favor of injections, over an average followup of 7.3 months [52]. The injections were directed into the bare scleral bed, conjunctiva, and Tenon’s capsule. In relation to the study by Valezi et al., Ibrahim and Nada postulated that a higher dose (25 mg vs. 5 mg) and wider area of 5-FU injection contributed to a lower recurrence rate.

## 5. Recommendations for 5-fluorouracil as a surgical adjuvant in preventing pterygium recurrence

This systematic review demonstrates that the published literature does not provide convincing evidence for the use of preoperative or intraoperative 5-FU as a surgical adjuvant to prevent pterygium recurrence. It should be recognized that recurrence rates may be influenced by multiple factors such as type and size of pterygium, genetic predisposition, surgical technique, geographical location, and the surgeon’s skill [2,61]. Appropriately, 5-FU is infrequently utilized in this regard, as reflected in recent practice pattern surveys and the limited number of recent large-scale clinical studies [50].

Despite this, it appears that 5-FU is still being used in scenarios where limited health resources, including surgical expertise, may pose a barrier to pterygium management. The authors believe that this may be because of its relatively low cost and availability. However, the authors recommend that 5-FU should not be used as a surgical adjuvant, even in these settings, given its inferior efficacy.

With ongoing innovation and refinement of reconstructive surgical techniques that have extremely low recurrence rates, the authors contend that the adjuvant use of 5-FU in pterygium surgery remains in serious question [3,14,83–85]. Moreover, these surgical techniques avoid many of the potential and substantial risks described in antimetabolite therapy. The authors acknowledge that these reconstructive techniques are time-consuming and technically challenging, and higher recurrence rates have been observed in surgery performed by trainees and those with less experience [86]. To address this, models have been created for teaching and acquiring the necessary surgical and suturing skills [87]. The advent and utilization of femtosecond lasers may also allow less experienced surgeons to produce optimal conjunctival flaps, but suffers from the same issue in resource scarce settings [88,89].

## 6. Efficacy of intralesional subconjunctival injections of 5fluorouracil

More promising results have been reported in studies examining postoperative intralesional 5-FU injections utilized to arrest progression of impending recurrent pterygium and true recurrent pterygium, with success rates ranging from 87.2 to 100% [18,54,90,91] and 75 to 100%, respectively [34,36,37,43–45] (Table 2). 5-FU injections have also been used as a sole treatment modality, without surgery, to arrest progression of primary and recurrent pterygia with reported success rates ranging from 81.3 to 96% [38,40–42].

6.1. Postoperative intralesional subconjunctival injections for impending recurrent pterygium (conjunctival recurrence)

Impending pterygium, conjunctival recurrence, is defined as the reappearance of localized fibrovascular tissue in the conjunctiva at the original excision site after the initial redness and swelling related to surgery has settled [39]. This is characterized by increased vascularity associated with injected and tortuous vessels, and thickening of the conjunctiva without associated corneal extension.

The first prospective, unmasked, randomized controlled trial was conducted by Prabhasawat et al., in 2006, and compared subconjunctival injections of 5-FU with triamcinolone and placebo in impending recurrent pterygium following various excision techniques [18]. They also proposed a criterion for grading the appearance of impending recurrence in the surgical bed (Grade I: normal appearance of the operated site; Grade II: presence of fine episcleral vessels in the excised area, extending to the limbus but without any fibrous tissue; Grade III: fibrovascular tissue in the excised area, reaching to the limbus but not invading the cornea, indicating conjunctival recurrence; Grade IV: fibrovascular tissue invading the cornea, indicating corneal recurrence). In their study, two 5-FU injections (5 mg), administered weekly demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect over placebo (87.2% vs. 48.6% success rate, p = 0.001) and improved the appearance of the local surgical site. Moreover, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis at one year showed that 5-FU significantly increased the recurrence-free period compared to placebo (p = 0.005). These results were confirmed in a randomized, double blinded, prospective controlled trial by Zhang et al., in 2020 [54]. Utilizing Prabhasawat’s criteria, they reported that three 5-FU injections (5 mg), given at days 5, 14, and 28 postoperatively, had an 87.5% success rate of arresting conjunctival recurrence (Grade II/III) and prevented corneal extension at six months.

In 2013, Said et al. performed a retrospective study of 5-FU injections (2.5 to 5 mg) in 15 cases of impending pterygium recurrence, given weekly until a clinical effect was observed, and found that the majority required three injections [39]. The clinical appearance improved in 93.3% of eyes, with decreased vascularity, regression of conjunctival thickening and arrest of progression. This study was the first to tailor the number of injections according to clinical needs, rather than using a predetermined protocol. They also suggested that prompt initiation of the intralesional injections when impending recurrence was detected was critical for success [39].

6.2. Postoperative intralesional subconjunctival injections for true recurrent pterygium (corneal recurrence)

True recurrent pterygium, corneal recurrence, is defined as progression of fibrovascular tissue beyond the limbal border onto the cornea [18]. In 2001, Pikkel et al. conducted a pilot study on six eyes with recurrent pterygium (fibrovascular growth of 0.5 to 1.5 mm onto the cornea) following bare scleral technique excision and reported a success rate of arresting progression of 83.3% [37]. The total 5-FU dose (1 to 3 mg), interval between injections (average 9.8 days), and total number of injections (2 to 4) was not uniform, but no further growth was detected over 12 months of follow-up. In one eye with a 1.5 mm recurrence, progression continued to more than 2 mm in length, despite two injections of 5-FU. Pikkel et al. reported that the patient attended follow-up three weeks later than scheduled, reinforcing the importance of adherence to an injection regimen. In 2003, Akarsu et al. performed weekly subconjunctival 5-FU injections (5 mg), up to a maximum of four injections, in four individuals who had recurrence (fibrovascular growth 1 to 2 mm onto the cornea) after conjunctival transpositional flap, and observed growth arrest and onset of atrophy in 75% of cases [34].

The optimal number and dose of 5-FU injections to arrest the progression of recurrent pterygium is yet to be determined. Garcia Tirado et al. utilized a protocol of 10 intralesional injections in their cohort of 15 patients, 14 with corneal invasion (1.6 ± 0.8 mm, range 0 to 3 mm).

The injections were given weekly, totaling 50 mg of 5-FU, and they reported a 100% success rate of arresting progression [45]. Similarly, Pherwani et al. reported one case of recurrent pterygium (fibrovascular growth 2.5 mm onto the cornea) that was successfully arrested by 10 intralesional 5-FU injections (5 mg) given every one to two weeks over five months [36]. This pterygium recurred following amniotic membrane grafting and progressed despite three cycles of topical MMC (0.02%) given four times a day for seven days. Ul Haq et al. and Sabater-Cruz et al. reported that as little as one intralesional injection of 5-FU (2 to 2.5 mg) to have success in arresting progression of recurrent pterygium [43,44]. It is clear from published evidence that there is a high variability in the total dose and number of 5-FU injections. Generally, it appears that a total of two to four 5-FU injections may be sufficient, but much greater numbers including eight, ten or fourteen injections (total dose 40 to 70 mg) have been used in some cases [36,75]. Due to this, some authors have recommended a tailored approach, and contend that a pre-determined protocol for the number of injections may be disadvantageous in patients who require increased doses to see a clinical effect [18,39].

6.3. Intralesional subconjunctival injections as a sole treatment modality for primary and recurrent pterygium (no surgery)

More recently, 5-FU injections have been investigated as a sole treatment option to arrest progression of primary or recurrent pterygia without surgery, with a success rates of 81.3 to 96% [38–42]. In 2021, Shah et al. published a study involving three intralesional injections of 5-FU (5 mg), given weekly, in 101 patients (54 primary and 47 recurrent pterygia) [42]. No other topical medication was administered. Initially, the pterygia of the recruited patients were graded as 1 (atrophic, n = 0), 2 (intermediate, n = 46) or 3 (fleshy, n = 55). After completing the three injections, a significant improvement in grading by ≥ 1 was observed in 87.1% of the patients (atrophic, n = 42; intermediate, n = 50; fleshy, n = 9; p < 0.001), with comparable efficacy in primary and recurrent pterygia. By six months, 13 patients showed no change from baseline, and only four patients displayed pterygium progression, conferring a success rate of 96%. Similarly, Malik et al. administered 5-FU (5 mg) weekly for four weeks, and reported an improvement in the fibrovascular growth and fleshy appearance in 85.2% and 81.3% of primary and recurrent pterygia, respectively [38]. However, their study had a limited follow-up period of only one month. Finally, Ghoz et al. demonstrated that intralesional 5-FU (2.5 mg) combined with bevacizumab (2.5 to 5 mg) injections, given every two weeks to a maximum of five injections, acted synergistically to arrest progression, induce atrophy, and reduce vascularity in their cohort of 14 patients with primary pterygium (100% success rate) [41].

Studies have also examined the effects of 5-FU injections on secondary outcomes, namely pterygium cosmesis, astigmatism and cytology (Table 2). The eye has been termed the aesthetic center of the face [92] and with low recurrence rates reported in emerging surgical techniques, there is an increasing focus on cosmesis and patient satisfaction as an outcome measure in pterygium management [3,88,93]. In patients treated solely with 5-FU injections, only 25.7% of 101 patients and 53% of 15 patients elected to undergo surgical excision [42,45]. These results may be clinically relevant, as a proportion of patients may elect not to pursue surgical excision if the overall cosmetic appearance can be improved with a less invasive option. Avoiding surgery confers the advantages of reduced scarring, intraoperative risk, postoperative complications, and the need to harvest tissue from limited donor sites [39]. In addition, multiple repeated surgeries may increase the risk of pterygium recurrence [3,94]. Future studies should report whether 5-FU injections had an impact on the rates of patients electing to undergo surgical excision, as this may have cost-benefit implications. It may also be instructive to apply the recently published cosmetic grading systems for pterygia in these studies [93].

7. Recommendations for 5-fluorouracil as a postoperative intralesional subconjunctival injection and sole treatment modality for primary, impending recurrent, and recurrent pterygia

This systematic review shows promising results on the use of 5-FU to arrest progression and improve cosmesis of impending recurrent or recurrent pterygia in the postoperative setting and as a sole treatment modality for primary or recurrent pterygia. Early recognition of impending pterygium recurrence and prompt administration of intralesional 5-FU injection prior to fibrovascular invasion of the cornea is likely to further increase success rates. Typically, the number of injections used to achieve a clinical effect is two to four, but much higher numbers have been described, and the authors believe that patients should be considered on a case-by-case basis. Given that the antiproliferative effect of 5-FU is dose- and time-dependent, clinicians should recognize that higher doses and repeated exposures may predispose patients to more complications. The application of 5-FU injections as a sole treatment modality for pterygia may avoid surgery and its associated risk of recurrence and complications [3], but this requires patients to adhere to follow-up and injection regimes. Long-term safety data for 5-FU in these roles are lacking, and the optimal dose, frequency, and duration of injections remain to be elucidated. In circumstances where 5-FU is used, the authors advise caution, vigilant monitoring, and careful patient selection to minimize complications. The benefits, risks, and potential short- and long-term complications associated with 5-FU should be discussed with the patient.

## 8. Alternatives therapies to 5-fluorouracil

Like 5-FU, corticosteroids, cyclosporine and anti-VEGF agents have been studied as surgical adjuvants or to manage impending recurrent pterygium [25,95,96].

### 8.1. Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids are routinely used in the postoperative management of pterygium and can be applied topically (prednisolone, dexamethasone and fluorometholone) or via subconjunctival injections (dexamethasone, triamcinolone, hydrocortisone) [19]. It has been suggested that inadequate administration of postoperative topical corticosteroids can be associated with higher recurrence rates [97]. Kheirkhah et al. reported that intraoperative triamcinolone acetonide injection combined with bare scleral technique and mitomycin C did not significantly reduce conjunctival recurrence or inflammation [91]. In impending recurrent pterygium, topical betamethasone was ineffective with 100% of cases progressing to corneal recurrence [98]. In contrast, postoperative subconjunctival injections of corticosteroids appears more effective in preventing corneal recurrence in cases of impending recurrence (Grade II/III) following conjunctival autografting or amniotic membrane grafting [19,20]. In a randomized controlled trial by Prabhasawat et al. on halting progression of impending recurrent pterygium, intralesional 5-FU was shown to be superior to triamcinolone injection (87.2% vs. 71.4%), and both were more effective than topical steroids alone (48.6%) [18]. Clinicians should be cognizant and monitor for the development of steroid-induced ocular hypertension [19,20,39,91].

### 8.2. Cyclosporine

Topical cyclosporine appears to be beneficial in reducing pterygium recurrence when compared with placebo. This applies to the bare sclera technique (22% vs. 44% and 20.6% vs. 45.5%) [24,99], conjunctival rotational flaps (23% vs. 46%) [21], conjunctival autografts (7.5% vs. 17.5%) [100], and limbal-conjunctival autografts [101]. Yalcin Tok et al. used cyclosporine in bilateral pterygium, using one eye as a control, and reported a recurrence of 12.9% in the treated eye compared with 45.2% in the control eye [23]. However, one study by Karadag et al. reported that recurrence in limbal-conjunctival autograft combined with cyclosporine was higher than conjunctival autograft alone (26.6% vs. 16.6%) [22]. In a network meta-analysis, topical cyclosporine was reported to be the most successful surgical adjuvant to conjunctival autograft in preventing recurrence in primary pterygium [25]. Complications associated with cyclosporine use in pterygium include irritation, burning sensation, hyperemia, dellen formation and, rarely, graft scarring or scleromalacia [22,24,100,101].

### 8.3. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor

Anti-VEGF agents in pterygium appear to be well tolerated but their efficacy as a surgical adjuvant or to treat impending recurrent pterygium in reported studies is inconclusive [26,27,102]. As an adjuvant, subconjunctival ranibizumab injection (0.5 mg) combined with conjunctival autografting had a 33% recurrence rate at six months [103]. Subconjunctival bevacizumab injection did not significantly decrease recurrence when compared with placebo in the bare scleral technique (1.25 mg) [104], conjunctival rotational flaps (1.25, 2.5, 3.75 mg total) [105–107], or limbal-conjunctival autografts (1.25 mg) [108]. In contrast, Nava-Castaneda et al. reported no corneal recurrences at one year in primary pterygium treated with conjunctival autograft and bevacizumab injection (2.5 to 5 mg) compared with placebo (0% vs. 12.5%, p < 0.04) [109]. A single injection (3.75 mg) after limbal-conjunctival autograft with limbal fixation suture also reduced recurrence [110]. As a topical agent, Karalezli et al. and Ozgurhan et al. reported that bevacizumab at 5 mg/ml did not significantly prevent corneal recurrence [111,112], while Motarjemizadeh et al. concluded 10 mg/ml was efficacious [113].

In impending recurrent pterygium, Fallah et al. reported progression to corneal recurrence in all cases treated with a single bevacizumab injection (2.5 mg) [98]. Lekhanont et al. compared various doses of a single bevacizumab injection (1.25, 2.5 or 3.75 mg) with control, and found a transient regression in vascularity, which was dose-dependent, but no change in thickness or size of the lesions [114]. At three months follow-up, 78% of the patients developed corneal recurrence. In contrast, Bayar et al. reported that one to three injections significantly reduced conjunctival vascularity with a 95.7% success rate (one corneal recurrence) at 12 months [115]. Wu et al. first reported the success of topical bevacizumab (25 mg/ml, four times a day, 3 weeks) in regressing limbal-conjunctival neovascularization in impending recurrence [116]. However, at a lower dose (5 mg/ml, two times a day, 1 week), Fallah et al. noted an initial delay of recurrence, but all impending cases eventually progressed to corneal recurrence [98]. Meanwhile, in true recurrent pterygium, Bahar et al. reported no significant reduction in vascularity with one to two injections (2.5 mg) [117], while Nava-Castaneda et al. reported three injections (2.5 mg) was effective in reducing the corneal and corneal-conjunctival neovascularization and size [118].

Three studies have reported inferior recurrence rates for bevacizumab when compared with MMC [99,119,120]. In regards to 5-FU, Bekibele et al. reported comparable efficacy of a single bevacizumab injection (0.5 mg) to intraoperative 5-FU (50 mg/ml, 5 min) as a surgical adjuvant to conjunctival autograft. Nevertheless, a randomized trial by Zhang et al. demonstrated that three conbercept injections (2 mg) led to no corneal recurrence, and was more effective than 5-FU (5 mg) in impending recurrent pterygium [54]. It has been suggested that 5-FU and bevacizumab may act synergistically, with a study by Ghoz et al. reporting a 100% success rate of arresting progression and inducing atrophy in primary pterygium [41]. Similar to 5-FU, the optimal dose, frequency, duration, and method of administration of anti-VEGF in pterygium remains to be elucidated.

## 9. Safety and complications of 5-fluorouracil in pterygium

The complications of 5-FU reported in published clinical studies on pterygium are summarized in Table 3 . These can range from transient and minor to significant and sight-threatening. Corneal complications linked with 5-FU are common and are secondary to its inhibitory effect on corneal epithelial mitosis [ 122 , 123 ]. It is well documented that 5-FU can cause scleral damage when applied to the bare sclera with a risk of scleral dellen or thinning (2.5 to 10%) [ 31 , 33 , 34 ]. Said et al. proposed that these antimetabolite-induced scleral complications are linked to the presence or absence of a conjunctival cover over the treated area, with the former being protective [ 39 ].

5-FU use, whether by intraoperative application or intralesional injection, in conjunctival autograft or conjunctival transpositional flap should be carefully considered, as relatively high rates of delayed conjunctival reepithelization (6 to 16%) [ 52 , 71 ], graft injection (6.5 to 56%) [ 80 – 82 ], wound dehiscence (7.1 to 32.1%) [ 32 , 34 , 56 , 82 ], and graft retraction [ 80 ] have been reported in several studies. The authors have reported a patient who had undergone previous pterygium excision with adjuvant antimetabolite therapy who subsequently developed necrosis and sloughing of their conjunctival graft [ 124 ]. The authors contended that the antimetabolite compromised the vascular bed of the conjunctiva and episcleral, which led to this result. The development of symblepharon [ 51 , 80 , 81 ] and cicatricial entropion [ 125 ] has also been reported for 5-FU use.

Corneal epithelial cells are rapidly dividing and therefore more susceptible in the short term to the toxic antiproliferative effects of 5-FU on the stem cells in the limbal region [126]. These effects were demonstrated in human in vivo and in vitro studies [67-70]. Clinically, corneal manifestations described include epithelial defects, and punc­ tate, filamentary and whorl-like keratopathy [30,31,33,52,72]. Prab­ hasawat et al. noted that two intralesional injections of 5-FU (5 mg) led to punctate epitheliopathy developing in 20.5% of eyes [18]. They observed three patients with erosions involving >30% of the cornea, with one eye experiencing a reduction in visual acuity. Similarly, Zhang et al. observed corneal abrasions in 17 of 48 eyes following three 5-FU injections (5 mg) [54]. Some of these findings are reversible with cessation of 5-FU, but in some patients, severe secondary complications like keratitis, ulceration, corneal melting and perforation can occur [35, 71,126,127]. These complications are recognized to occur more commonly in patients who have underlying corneal or ocular surface disorders. Given the high propensity for complications, the authors caution against utilizing 5-FU in patients with previous or current intrinsic corneal disorders [127,128].

It has been suggested that the higher incidence of corneal complications is linked to longer periods of corneal exposure to 5-FU during topical application, and that this may be reduced by employing targeted intralesional injections [ 52 ]. When examining the reported complications, studies involving 5-FU injections seem to have lower frequencies than topical application ( Table 3 ) [ 34 , 37 , 39 , 41 – 43 , 54 ]. However, a direct comparison of high dose intralesional 5-FU injections (25 mg) with high dose intraoperative application (50 mg/ml, 5 min) demonstrated no significant reduction in the proportion of complications [ 79 ]. Injection of 5-FU using a no-reflux technique may also reduce corneal complications [ 129 ]. In this technique, 5-FU was infiltrated into the subconjunctival area using a 30-G needle inserted right up to the hub. After the injection, the entry point was pressed with a cotton-tipped applicator to prevent reflux of the drug into the tear film and surrounding ocular surface. It should be noted that even at relatively low concentrations of 5-FU, complications including delayed conjunctival epithelialization (6.4%), and corneal ulceration (6.4%) have been reported [ 71 ].

MMC, another adjuvant antimetabolite used in pterygium and glaucoma surgery, is known to cause or be associated with severe and sight-threatening complications such as scleral ulceration and calcification, corneal oedema and perforation, sudden onset mature cataract, vitreoretinal toxicity, uveitis, secondary glaucoma, and endophthalmitis [ 130 – 133 ]. Despite reduction in dosages following these serious complications, long-term safety issues remain [ 134 ]. Of note, corneoscleral ulceration and corneal perforation have been reported 18 to 25 years after initial pterygium excision using MMC [ 131 ]. Though 5-FU is known to have less potency than MMC, they are both antiproliferative in nature, and the potential for similar severe long-term complications should be recognized with 5-FU use [ 61 , 64 , 135 ].

Another factor is that pterygium may represent evidence of reduced DNA repair capacity in an individual [ 136 ]. In Australia, it has been demonstrated that around 80% of school-age children show evidence of ocular ultraviolet damage [ 137 ]; however the prevalence of pterygium is approximately 7% [ 138 ], providing possible evidence of tissue repair capacity with time and adequate eye protection. If this is the case, further insult to these eyes with 5-FU, an agent that suppresses DNA replication, may be ill advised. Therefore, 5-FU may also be best avoided in patients with a family history of pterygium.

### 9.1. Safety profile of 5-fluorouracil in other ocular conditions

There is considerable experience on the use of 5-FU to augment glaucoma filtering surgery [ 139 ]. As in pterygium, development of punctate corneal epitheliopathy and epithelial defects are common but generally reversible with cessation of 5-FU injections [ 30 , 126 , 140 ]. Similarly, sight-threatening secondary corneal complications can occur [ 126 , 127 ]. 5-FU doses are typically much higher in glaucoma surgery (up to 105 mg) than in pterygium (5 to 20 mg). However, 5-FU toxicity may occur even at lower doses, as evident in a glaucoma study that utilized a reduced 5-FU dose (17.5 to 62.5 mg) and still noted high rates of corneal epitheliopathy (29%) [57]. Another study that used two injections of 5-FU (10 mg) reported development of a large central corneal epithelial defect, with subsequent ulceration and residual scarring [141]. The total dose of intralesional 5-FU injections used in pterygium overlaps within this range [36,45].

Topical 5-FU has increased in popularity as a treatment for ocular surface squamous neoplasia (OSSN). It is typically used at a concentration of 1% (10 mg/ml), applied four times a day for one to four weeks, followed by a chemotherapy-free period. The reported complications of 5-FU in OSSN are usually transient and reversible. These include pain, tearing, photophobia, hyperemia, eyelid erythema and oedema, and superficial punctate keratitis [142,143]. The incidence of epithelial defects in eyes with OSSN treated with 5-FU has been reported as between 1 and 8% [144,145]. Although 1% 5-FU (10 mg/ml) utilized in this manner for 5-FU has been considered safe [146], a case of sight-threatening bilateral corneoscleral ulceration and unilateral corneal perforation was reported in 2022 [147]. This patient developed epithelial defects at week eight of a biweekly cycle and required serial replacement of amniotic membrane rings and eventually a 7.5 mm penetrating keratoplasty. Sun et al. conducted a retrospective study of six OSSN cases that received perilesional subconjunctival 5-FU injections (25 mg/ml, 5 to 7.5 mg doses, mean of 17 injections over 13 weeks) and reported punctate epitheliopathy in all of these patients at four to six weeks, which resolved with tear substitutes or bandage contact lenses [148]. No major side effects were reported over a three year follow up period [102].

### 9.2. Long term safety profile of 5-fluorouracil in pterygium

There is a paucity of evidence regarding the long-term safety profile of 5-FU in pterygium treatment. Published studies are almost exclusively short-term in nature, with many totaling no longer than six months [32, 38,40,42,54–56,78,79,82,90], and very few exceeding 24 months follow-up [45,71,81]. Although it is recognized that pterygium recurrence typically occurs within the first year after excision, these studies may potentially miss important 5-FU-related side effects [15]. Furthermore, while short-term complications may be dose- and duration-dependent, the long-term safety issues remain, especially as 5-FU may continue to have antimitotic effects long after cessation [58, 81]. This is of particular concern if the ocular surface stem cell populations are affected. Indeed, antimetabolite therapy is recognized to cause or is associated with serious side effects in pterygium and glaucoma management, including corneal toxicity, scleral thinning and ulceration, endophthalmitis, and calcification, even many years after treatment [30,130,131,139,140,149,150]. The authors propose that extended follow-up is warranted in future 5-FU studies to capture the potentially deleterious effects of 5-FU.

## 10. Conclusion

This systematic review analyzed the evidence for 5-FU efficacy and safety in the armamentarium for pterygium management. 5-FU is relatively inexpensive, available, and easy to administer, which makes it attractive in resource-limited scenarios. However, published clinical evidence indicates that the preoperative and intraoperative application of 5-FU has suboptimal efficacy in preventing pterygium recurrence. Therefore, the authors do not recommend 5-FU as a surgical adjuvant for pterygium surgery. Furthermore, the recurrence rate of 5-FU is inferior to that of emerging surgical techniques, and it has a predilection for scleral thinning, corneal toxicity, and graft-related complications. In contrast, the use of intralesional 5-FU injection in the postoperative setting or as a sole treatment modality to arrest progression of primary, impending recurrent, and recurrent pterygia and improve cosmesis, showed more promising results. In the short term, complications from 5FU used in this manner are typically transient and reversible. However, more long-term efficacy and safety data are needed, especially given the potential for severe and sight-threatening complications of 5-FU in pterygium and other ocular conditions. If 5-FU is utilized, it should be administered cautiously in selected patients and with vigilant long-term monitoring in those undergoing repeated exposure. Further clinical studies on intralesional 5-FU injections with extended follow-up are needed to elucidate the optimal dose, frequency, duration, and safety.
