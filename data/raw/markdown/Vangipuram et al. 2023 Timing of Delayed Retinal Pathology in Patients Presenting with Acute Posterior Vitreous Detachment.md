Timing of Delayed Retinal Pathology in Patients Presenting with Acute Posterior Vitreous Detachment in the IRIS Registry (Intelligent Research in Sight)

Gautam Vangipuram, MD, 1 Charles Li, BA, 2 Siying Li, MS, 2 Lynn Liu, MS, 2 Linda D. Harrison, PhD, 3 Flora Lum, MD, 2 Gaurav K. Shah, MD 4

Objective: To determine the timing of delayed retinal pathology in eyes presenting with acute posterior vitreous detachment (PVD).

Design: Retrospective database study.

Subjects: Patients in the Intelligent Research in Sight (IRIS) registry found to have acute PVD based on the International Classi fication of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision, codes were followed.

Methods: Patients coded to have a PVD from 2013 to 2018 along with common procedural technology coding of extended ophthalmoscopy were included. Ocular baseline characteristics included visual acuity, lens status, presence or absence of vitreous hemorrhage, myopia, lattice degeneration, and subspecialty training of the treating physician.

Main Outcome Measures: Timing (days) to delayed retinal break or detachment

Results: A total of 434 046 eyes met inclusion/exclusion criteria, and 10 518 eyes (2.42%) presented with a delayed retinal break or detachment after initial PVD. The median time to retinal break and detachment after initial PVD was 42 (range, 1 e 365) days and 51 (range, 1 e 365) days, respectively. Eyes with vitreous hemorrhage (hazard ratio [HR], 9.30; 95% con fidence interval [CI], 8.50 e 10.2), history of retinal break/retinal detachment in the fellow eye (HR, 3.91; 95% CI, 3.64 e 4.20), lattice degeneration (HR, 2.61; 95% CI, 2.35 e 2.90), and myopia (HR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.33 e 1.53) were found to be at a higher risk of developing delayed break or detachment.

Conclusions: Follow-up examination after initial PVD is necessary to diagnose delayed or missed retinal pathology. In eyes with no initial pathology, providers should consider repeat examination at least once within 6 weeks, and sooner for eyes with higher-risk features.

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is a common ocular event presenting to both general ophthalmology and retinal clinics that can result in serious ocular pathology. The rate of an associated retinal tear upon presentation with symptomatic PVD is study dependent but is quoted between 8% and 22%. 1 e 4 Due to the evolving nature of a PVD, retinal pathology may have been missed or may develop after the initial examination; however, uncertainty and variability still exist regarding when the optimal time is to reevaluate patients after the initial diagnosis of an acute PVD. Therefore, the timing of repeat evaluation after a symptomatic PVD is important so as not to miss pathology with potentially serious visual consequences. 5 e 7 Guidelines on the timing of a repeat examination after a PVD evaluation vary depending on examination findings, clinician preference, and other ocular risk factors. # Methods

This was a nonrandomized retrospective cohort study that was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Since the IRIS Registry contains de-identi fied patient data, this study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval. For this study, the December 18, 2020 version of the database was used.

We included patient eyes that were documented with a PVD (International Classi fication of Disease, Ninth Revision [ICD-9], 379.21; ICD, Tenth Revision [ICD-10], H43.81) from July 1, 2013, to December 31, 2018, along with common procedural technology codes of extended ophthalmoscopy 92225/92226 2 weeks from the index event (documentation of initial PVD diagnosis). We excluded patients who were aged < 18 years, had < 6 months of prior data in the IRIS Registry, had < 1 year of follow-up after the index event, and patients that did not have a recorded National Provider Identi fier attributed to their extended ophthalmoscopy. We limited the analysis to only include eyes that did not have a retinal break (ICD, Ninth Revision, 361.3; ICD, Tenth Revision, H33.30, H33.31, and H33.32) or detachment (ICD, Ninth Revision, 361.0, 361.81, and 361.89; ICD, Tenth Revision, H33.01, H33.02, H33.03, H33.04, H33.05, and H33.4) on the same date as the index event. Eyes with a history of previous vitrectomy or previously treated retinal breaks were also excluded. Ocular baseline characteristics were analyzed and included presenting visual acuity (VA), lens status, laterality, presence or absence of vitreous hemorrhage, myopia, history of retinal break or detachment in the fellow eye, and lattice degeneration. Other baseline characteristics included patient age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, the geographic region of practice, insurance, and the subspecialty status of the treating physician (retinal specialist vs. nonretinal specialist). The determination of “ retinal specialist ” is by the self-designation of the ophthalmologist in the American Academy of Ophthalmology membership database. The primary outcome of the study was the time (days) to a delayed/missed break or detachment after the initial PVD. This outcome was also strati fied by the level of specialty care (retinal specialist vs. nonretinal specialist). Secondary outcomes included the incidence of delayed/ missed retinal breaks or detachments within 1 year after the index event and the final VA closest to 1 year after the index event ( 3 months).

## Statistical Analysis

A multivariable shared frailty survival model (clustered on patients) was constructed with baseline covariates to examine evidence for risk factors of delayed retinal break or detachment within 1 year after the initial diagnosis of PVD. The level of signi ficance for outcomes was set at P 0.05. To test for statistically signi ficant differences in means, we used an independent, 2-tailed t test.

### Results

A total of 12 803 515 unique eyes were documented with a PVD between July 1, 2013, to December 31, 2018. After the application of additional inclusion and exclusion criteria, 434 046 eyes were included ( Table 1 ). Notably, the mean age for all eyes was 70.6 years (range, 18 e 106). The mean VA of all eyes was 0.23 0.32 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution. The majority of patients in this data set were White (75.6%) and women (61.6%). Of the eyes included, 10 518 (2.42%) went on to be diagnosed with a delayed/missed retinal break ( n = 7241) or detachment ( n = 4173) within 1 year of the initial PVD ( Table 2 ). The median time to a delayed/missed retinal break and/or detachment was 42 and 51 days, respectively. Among eyes examined by a retinal specialist, the median time to a delayed RB or RD was 42 and 49 days, respectively, compared to 37 and 56 days, respectively, among eyes examined by a nonretinal specialist ( P < 0.001, P = 0.39, respectively). A multivariable shared frailty survival analysis was performed to examine evidence for risk factors of delayed/missed retinal break or retinal detachment. Adjusting for patient age, gender, and race/ ethnicity, 4 ocular covariates included in the multivariable model exhibited a hazard ratio (HR) > 1: (1) vitreous hemorrhage (HR, 9.30; 95% CI, 8.50 e 10.2), (2) history of retinal break/retinal detachment in the fellow eye (HR, 3.91; 95% CI, 3.64 e 4.20), (3) lattice degeneration (HR, 2.61; 95% CI, 2.35 e 2.90), and (4) myopia (HR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.33 e 1.53). An exploratory analysis was conducted on these “ high-risk ” ocular characteristics to determine the time to delayed retinal pathology in these eyes.

Of all eyes meeting inclusion into the study, 61 700 (14.2%) were noted to meet “ high-risk ” criteria, having at least 1 of the ocular characteristics of vitreous hemorrhage, a history of fellow eye retinal break/retinal detachment, lattice degeneration, and myopia. Of these, 4177 (6.77%) were observed to present with delayed retinal break or detachment after initial PVD ( Table 4 ). In these eyes, the median time to break ( n = 2954) or detachment ( n = 1644) in “ high-risk ” eyes was 34 and 39 days, respectively. Conversely, the median time to delayed retinal break or detachment in all other “ non e high-risk ” eyes was 51 and 62 days, respectively ( Figs 1 and 2 ).

### Discussion

Acute symptomatic PVD is a common and critical ocular event that can lead to serious visual consequences if not managed appropriately. Aside from symptomatic flashes (photopsias) and floaters (myodesopsias), a potential retinal tear and detachment are pathologic sequelae that can lead to permanent visual compromise. Usually, retinal tears develop at the vitreous base, a circumferential zone 2 mm anterior and 4 mm posterior to the ora serrata. Visualizing this region of the posterior segment can be dif ficult and the standard of care examination entails slit lamp biomicroscopy, indirect ophthalmoscopy with scleral depression, and B-scan ultrasonography if visualization is inadequate. If no associated break is found, clinical decision making is needed on when to see the patient back for follow-up examination, as PVD may be associated with delayed onset of peripheral retinal breaks/detachments or missed findings of such pathology on initial examination. Previous studies have shown a rate between 1% and 3% in the incidence of delayed retinal pathology after an initial PVD. 8 e 11 Our study aligns with these previous findings, as 2.42% of patients in our study population presented with a delayed retinal break or detachment after a symptomatic PVD.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology Preferred Practice Pattern Guidelines published in 2019 advocated for repeat examination within 2 months of initial presentation and within 1 to 2 weeks in eyes with vitreous hemorrhage or after treatment of an acute retinal tear. 12 However, in a recent study by Seider et al 13 that analyzed 7522 patients diagnosed with an acute symptomatic PVD, the median time to delayed retinal break or detachment was 22 days, suggesting that patients may need to be reevaluated sooner than the generalized guideline of 2 months. Risk factors for a delayed retinal break or detachment in that study included male sex, age < 60 years, vitreous hemorrhage, lattice degeneration, history of retinal tear or detachment in the fellow eye, and previous cataract surgery.

In PVD eyes with at least 1 ocular risk factor for vitreous hemorrhage, lattice degeneration, fellow eye retinal break/retinal detachment, or myopia, the median time to retinal break or detachment is reduced to 34 and 39 days, respectively. In eyes documented only with vitreous hemorrhage, the median time to retinal break speci fically is further reduced to 14 days.

Vitreous hemorrhage in the context of an acute symptomatic PVD has been known to have increased rates of retinal tear, ranging between 70% and 80%. 15 Vitreous hemorrhage occurs in this setting due to tractional avulsion of the super ficial retinal vasculature or rupture of the retinal vasculature bridging a tear. Delayed presentation of a retinal break or detachment in these eyes may be due to the evolving nature of the PVD or inadequate visualization of the entire vitreous base. Supplementing B-scan ultrasonography with a detailed clinical examination may be useful in determining the presence of a retinal break; however, the sensitivity and positive predictive value of this test vary widely (24% e 100%) and is technology and examiner dependent. 11 Frequent re-examination and consideration of early vitrectomy are advised in these patients to ensure that worsening pathology does not develop.

Myopia has frequently been cited as a risk factor for the development of retinal detachment, with higher degrees of myopia increasing the rates of retinal pathology. This is in part due to the increased thinning of the peripheral retina in these eyes as well as premature vitreous liquefaction causing PVD. 16 e 19 Importantly, in a study by Vitale et al, 20 the prevalence of moderate myopia ( 2.0 to 7.9 D) in the United States nearly doubled from 1972 (11.4%) to 2004

(22.4%), whereas the prevalence of high myopia (de fined as more than 8.0 D) increased 8-fold during the same time frame. Given this trend, knowledge of a patient ’ s precorrected refractive error is prudent in risk strati fication of potential retinal pathology in those with an acute PVD.

Turning to our analysis strati fied by subspecialty training, we did note a statistically signi ficant difference in timing to delayed retinal break in non-retinal specialists (median = 37 days) versus retina specialists (median = 42 days) ( P < 0.001). No signi ficant difference, however, was found in timing of delayed retinal detachment in this subset (median = 49 days for retinal specialists vs. median = 56 days for nonretinal specialists) ( P = 0.39). Differences in timing to delayed pathology may be a result of variations in counseling regarding symptoms and return precautions between specialists versus generalists as well as established practice follow-up patterns.

There are several limitations to this study. First, inaccuracies in the data extracted from the IRIS Registry may exist because of ICD or common procedural technology coding errors, resulting in the incorrect exclusion or inclusion of patients. The diagnosis of acute PVD was limited to ICD coding and was unable to be individually verified by chart review, OCT, or ultrasonographic imaging. This may have led to selection bias of providers coding for extended ophthalmoscopy only in eyes they are more suspicious of the underlying pathology, leading to eyes that were incorrectly excluded. However, we felt that this methodology produced a more specific cohort than including eyes solely by ICD code for PVD or eyes coded to have PVD with previous examinations void of PVD coding for an arbitrary time frame. For this latter situation, inaccuracies may have arisen if, for example, several providers had seen the patient over time and missed coding for a PVD or did not dilate the patient to appropriately check. Second, details on the severity of certain conditions were also unavailable in the data. For example, the number of retinal tears diagnosed, severity of vitreous hemorrhage, and degree of lattice degeneration and myopia were unable to be quantified. The stratification of these variables based on severity may have shed more light on disease incidence and timing and helped guide clinical decision making. As mentioned earlier, retinal pathology discovered after the initial PVD may have been present at the first examination and was not noted by the examiner. However, we feel this limitation is not respective solely of a large retrospective database review. Furthermore, we were unable, through this methodology, to assess for pathologic symptoms (photopsias and myodesopsias) or noncodable examination features, such as vitreous pigment.
