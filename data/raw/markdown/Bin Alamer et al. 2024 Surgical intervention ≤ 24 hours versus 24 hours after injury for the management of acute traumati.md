Surgical intervention ≤ 24 hours versus > 24 hours after injury for the management of acute traumatic central cord syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Othman Bin-Alamer, MBBS, 1 Jumanah Qedair, 2 Hussam Abou-Al-Shaar, MD, 1 Arka N. Mallela, MD, MS, 1 Kishore Balasubramanian, MS, 3 Nada Alnefaie, MD, 4 Abdul Rahman Abou Al-Shaar, MBBS, 5 Tritan Plute, BS, 1 Victor M. Lu, MD, PhD, 6 David J. McCarthy, MD, MSc, 1 Daryl P. Fields, MD, PhD, 1 Nitin Agarwal, MD, 1 Peter C. Gerszten, MD, 1 and D. Kojo Hamilton, MD 1

1 Department of Neurological Surgery, University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania; 2 College of Medicine, King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia; 3 Texas A&M University College of Medicine, Houston, Texas; 4 Department of Adult Neurosurgery, National Neurosciences Institute, King Fahad Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia; 5 College of Medicine, AlMaarefa University, Dariyah, Saudi Arabia; and 6 Department of Neurological Surgery, University of Miami School of Medicine, Miami, Florida

OBJECTIVE The objective was to evaluate the efficacy, outcomes, and complications of surgical intervention performed within 24 hours (≤ 24 hours) versus after 24 hours (> 24 hours) in managing acute traumatic central cord syndrome (ATCCS).

METHODS Articles pertinent to the study were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane. The authors performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of treatment procedures and outcomes according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRIMSA) guidelines.

RESULTS Seven articles comprising 488 patients were included, with 188 (38.5%) patients in the ≤ 24-hour group and 300 (61.5%) in the > 24-hour group. Significant differences were not found between groups in terms of demographic characteristics, injury mechanism, spinal cord compression level, neuroimaging features, and the American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) motor score at admission. Both groups had a similar approach to surgery and steroid administration. The surgical complication rate was significantly higher in the > 24-hour group (4.5%) compared to the ≤ 24-hour group (1.2%) (p = 0.05). Clinical follow-up duration was similar at 12 months (interquartile range 3–36) for both groups (p > 0.99). The ≤ 24-hour group demonstrated a not statistically significant greater improvement in ASIA motor score, with a mean difference of 12 (95% CI −20.7 to 44.6) compared to the > 24-hour group.

CONCLUSIONS The present study indicates potential advantages of early (≤ 24 hours) surgery in ATCCS patients, specifically in terms of lower complication rates. However, further research is needed to confirm these findings and their clinical implications.

KEYWORDS central cord syndrome; traumatic spinal cord injury; time of intervention; ASIA; motor score; complications; trauma

A cute traumatic central cord syndrome (ATCCS) constitutes a significant proportion of spinal cord injuries and presents a unique management challenge. 1–3 Characterized by disproportionate weakness in the upper limbs compared to the lower limbs, along with sensory loss and bladder dysfunction, ATCCS often results from hyperextension injuries in patients with underlying cervical spondylosis or stenosis. 2,4 The management

Emerging evidence from clinical studies suggests that early surgical intervention (within 24 hours) may improve neurological outcomes and reduce duration of hospital stay. 6,7 Conversely, other studies advocate for delayed surgical intervention (beyond 24 hours), proposing that this

Such a comprehensive analysis could be instrumental to guiding clinical decision-making and setting evidence-based guidelines regarding the timing of surgery in ATCCS.

# Methods

## Literature Search

A systematic review and meta-analysis was performed following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).10 Comprehensive searches were performed in the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases from their commencement up to September 2022. We employed Medical Subject Headings along with keyword searches in each database by using Boolean operators such as “OR” and “AND.” The included search terms were (Central Cord Syndrome) AND (Surgery OR Surgical OR Management OR Treatment OR Intervention).

### Study Selection

The criteria for inclusion and exclusion were set in advance. Studies qualified for inclusion if they 1) conducted retrospective or prospective research on ATCCS patients; and 2) provided data on clinical characteristics, therapeutic approach, and treatment outcomes. The exclusion criteria included studies that 1) were meta-analyses, reviews, editorials, letters, books, or book chapters; 2) reported fewer than 5 cases; 3) lacked adequate clinical details, specifically missing information on patient demographic characteristics, treatment methods, and/or outcomes; and 4) were not written in the English language.

The titles and abstracts of all gathered papers were independently evaluated by two authors (J.Q. and K.B.) using the preset criteria. For those studies that matched the criteria, their full texts were further examined by the same authors. Any disagreements were addressed and settled by a third author (O.B.A.). Additionally, the reference lists of the chosen papers were checked to identify any additional pertinent studies.

### Data Extraction

Data from the included articles were retrieved by one researcher (J.Q.) and independently verified by another (O.B.A.) to guarantee accuracy. The variables extracted included the authors, year of publication, study design, sample size, age and sex of the patients, type of injury, injury mechanism, injury level, American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) motor scores at presentation and last follow-up, management approach, and complications.11 Absent data were either not reported by the original author or presented in a manner that made them indistinguishable from other data.

### Data Synthesis

The main outcomes studied were the clinical characteristics, surgical treatment, and functional results of ATCCS patients who underwent surgery. The evidence quality of each paper was assessed on the basis of the 2011 Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine guidelines.12

### Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and RStudio version 2021.09.0 (Posit Software). For metaanalyses, heterogeneity tests, publication bias assessment, and subgroup analyses, the meta package in R was utilized. Categorical data were presented as either frequencies with percentages or weighted frequencies along with 95% CI. Continuous data, on the other hand, were presented as either weighted mean with 95% CI or median (interquartile range [IQR]) with differences assessed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Using the meta package, a proportional meta-analysis was conducted for aggregated proportions. In contrast, a meta-analysis of means consolidated the pooled means. Subgroup analyses were executed to determine statistical differences between groups. The mean differences were calculated for ASIA motor score changes at the last follow-up because all included articles used a unified scoring system. All proportions were logit transformed before being pooled during the meta-analysis to sidestep potential skewness and exaggerated precision, in line with the recommendations by Lipsey and Wilson.13 We considered p values less than 0.1 from 2-tailed tests to indicate statistical significance. Data from all studies were combined using random-effects models. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the chi-square test and the Higgins I2 test.14 To detect any publication bias, we utilized funnel plots and the Egger’s test, with p values < 0.05 suggesting bias presence.15,16 All studies were checked for visual symmetry, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. None of the endpoints had a sufficient number of articles (n ≥ 9) to undergo testing via Egger’s test, in accordance with the guidelines proposed by Sterne et al.17

## Results

After a comprehensive search across the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases, we initially identified 1800 potential articles. After duplicate removal, 1326 unique articles remained. A total of 1292 articles were ruled out after title and abstract assessment, leaving 34 articles for full-text evaluation. Subsequent detailed full-text screening led to the exclusion of 27 articles as they did not meet the set inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 7 articles, with similar clinical definitions of central cord syndrome, each classified as level IV evidence, were included in this study as per our inclusion and exclusion requirements (Supplementary Table 1).18–24

### Patient Demographic Characteristics

Overall, the cohort size consisted of 188 patients (38.5%) in the ≤ 24-hour intervention group and 300 patients (61.5%) in the > 24-hour intervention group, with 73.8% and 79.7% of patients being male, respectively (p = 0.15) (Table 1). The weighted mean age of the patients in the ≤ 24-hour group was 52.4 years (95% CI 44.9–61.2 years), while in the > 24-hour group it was 55.2 years (95% CI 49.3–61.8 years), with no significant difference (p = 0.47).

A total of 165 and 236 patients in the ≤ 24-hour and > 24-hour groups, respectively, had available data on the mechanism of injury (Table 1). The groups were not different in terms of the mechanism of injury (p = 0.32), and falls were the predominant cause of injury in both the ≤ 24-hour (49.1%) and > 24-hour (60.2%) groups. Motor vehicle accidents ranked as the second most common cause in both groups, accounting for 32.7% and 28.4% of cases, respectively. Sports-related trauma represented the third most common cause, with 3.6% and 1.7% in the ≤ 24-hour and > 24-hour groups, respectively.

Among the patients with available data on spinal cord compression (n = 130 in the ≤ 24-hour group and n = 152 in the > 24-hour group), the highest proportion of patients with compression in the ≤ 24-hour group was observed at the C4–5 level (78.5%), followed by the C3–4 level (13.1%) (Table 1). In the > 24-hour group, most compression was seen at the C4–5 level (71.1%), followed by the C3–4 level (16.4%) (p = 0.35).

### Intervention and Patient Outcomes

Among the patients with available data on steroid administration (n = 129 in the ≤ 24-hour group; n = 152 in the > 24-hour group), 53.5% in the ≤ 24-hour group and 42.8% in the > 24-hour group received steroids (p = 0.72) (Table 2). The surgical approach was comparable (p

= 0.14) among patients for whom data were available (n

= 49 in the ≤ 24-hour group; n = 136 in the > 24-hour group): the anterior approach was used in 44.9% of the ≤ 24-hour group and 37.5% of the > 24-hour group, whereas the posterior approach was utilized in 34.7% and 50% and a combined approach in 20.4% and 12.5%, respectively.

Among patients with available data on surgical complications (n = 172 in the ≤ 24-hour group; n = 266 in the > 24-hour group), complications were significantly higher in the > 24-hour group (4.5%) compared to the ≤ 24-hour group (1.2%, p = 0.05) (Table 2). In the > 24-hour group, complications included postoperative hematoma (0.8%), surgical site infection (1.5%), venous thromboembolism (0.8%), and sepsis (1.1%). In the ≤ 24-hour group, surgical site infection (0.6%) and venous thromboembolism (0.6%) were the most reported complications. There were no incidences of postoperative hematoma or sepsis recorded in the ≤ 24-hour group. The median duration of clinical follow-up was comparable at 12 months (IQR 3-36) for both groups (p > 0.99), as all the included articles consistently applied a fixed duration for their last follow-up in both groups. At a mean of 12 months, the group categorized as ≤ 24 hours showed a higher average improvement in the ASIA motor score compared to the > 24-hour group. However, this improvement was not statistically significant. The mean difference in score improvement between the groups was 12 (95% CI -20.7 to 44.6, p = 0.33).

## Discussion

In the present analysis, the timing of surgical intervention demonstrated no statistically significant correlation with superior outcomes based on ASIA motor scores. Notably, a pattern emerged where early (≤ 24 hours) surgical intervention corresponded to a lower rate of complications. This finding deviates from the prevailing understanding that often associates early surgery with a higher risk of surgical complications. However, caution remains essential when formulating a surgical management plan, considering the highly individualized characteristics of ATCCS injuries. The diversity in clinical presentations among ATCCS patients calls for an individualized, patient-centric approach.

Our cohort showed male predominance in both groups, accounting for 73.8% and 79.7% of patients in the ≤ 24hour and > 24-hour groups respectively, with no statistically significant sex disparity observed (p = 0.15). ### Intervention and Patient Outcomes

Among the patients for whom data on steroid administration were available, 53.5% in the ≤ 24-hour group and 42.8% in the > 24-hour group received steroids, reflecting a nonsignificant difference in steroid utilization between the two groups (p = 0.72). Steroid administration in acute spinal cord injury remains a topic of ongoing debate and controversy, with some studies in the literature advocating for its use for neuroprotection, while others caution about its potential complications; however, the value of steroids in spinal cord injury is beyond the scope of this paper and is investigated elsewhere in the literature.34–36

The surgical approach, either anterior, posterior, or combined, varied among patients but did not significantly differ between the two groups (p = 0.14). The decision regarding the surgical approach often hinges on factors such as injury location, pattern, morphological features, ligamentous status, patient characteristics, and surgeon preference, which further underlines the individualized nature of surgical management in spinal cord injury.37–39

In the analysis of surgical complications, the > 24-hour group experienced a significantly higher complication rate (4.5%) compared to the ≤ 24-hour group (1.2%) (p = 0.05). Similarly, Liu et al.40 conducted a meta-analysis of the value of early surgical decompression in traumatic spinal cord injuries and found a significantly higher rate in the late (≥ 24 hours) group (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.40–0.91, p = 0.02). Additionally, Fehlings et al.41 conducted a multicenter, international, prospective cohort study to investigate the role of early (< 24 hours) versus late (≥ 24 hours) surgical intervention in acute spinal cord injury. Although not statistically significant, a higher rate of complications was observed in the late group (30.5%) compared to the early group (24.2%). These findings challenge the prevalent perception associating early surgery with an increased risk of complications, further fueling the ongoing discourse on the relationship between the timing of surgery and complication rates in spinal cord injury.40–42 In the present study, the complications reported in the ≤ 24-hour group were surgical site infection and venous thromboembolism, while the complications in the > 24hour group ranged from postoperative hematoma to surgical site infection and sepsis, suggesting a more severe and broader spectrum of complications associated with delayed surgery. The lower rate and absence of severe complications in the ≤ 24-hour group of our study suggest a potential safety benefit of early intervention. This may be due to reduced spinal cord compression duration and the impact of the secondary injury, which minimized the risk of postoperative hematoma or decreased the infection risk through shorter preoperative stay. However, the causes re-main speculative, warranting further exploration and research. These findings should be cautiously interpreted, emphasizing that individualized patient approach should guide surgical decisions, despite the observed trends favoring early surgery.

We found that the median (IQR) duration of clinical follow-up (12 [IQR 3–36] months for the ≤ 24-hour group vs 12 [3–36] months for the > 24-hour group, p > 0.99) remained comparable between the groups. Although the data available in our study were insufficient for analysis, Liu et al.40 conducted a meta-analysis of traumatic spinal cord injury and reported their findings on the time of intervention and length of stay.40 The authors found that early surgical intervention (within 24 hours) was associated with shorter length of stay (mean day difference −4.76 days, 95% CI −9.19 to −0.32, p = 0.04) and a lower risk of complications (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.40–0.91, p = 0.02). These results support the notion that early surgical intervention reduces complication rates and length of stay, partially concordant with our findings.

Finally, at a weighted mean time of 12 months after surgical decompression, the ≤ 24-hour group showed a higher yet not statistically significant improvement in ASIA motor scores compared to those of the > 24-hour group, with a mean difference of improvement of 12 (95% CI −20.7 to 44.6) in ASIA motor score (p = 0.33). Corroborating this, the meta-analysis of Liu et al. on traumatic spinal cord injuries revealed a significant association between early surgery and improved neurological outcomes (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.19–2.31, p < 0.01).40 In a similar vein, Fehlings et al.41 found in their 6-month follow-up study that patients who received early surgery were 2.8 times more likely to experience at least an improvement of 2 grades in the ASIA motor score, a finding that was adjusted for preoperative neurological status and steroid use, with an OR of 2.83 (95% CI 1.10–7.28, p = 0.03). Similar to these results, 27.2% of patients with traumatic spinal cord injuries achieved at least an improvement of 2 grades on the ASIA scale when they underwent surgical decompression within 24 hours, as opposed to only 3% when the procedure was performed after this time frame (p = 0.01).43 This narrative is strengthened by additional studies that similarly suggested an association between enhanced neurological outcomes and prompt surgical intervention.41,42,44

The inability of our study to validate the significant differences in outcomes noted in the spinal cord injury literature can primarily be ascribed to the specific methodological approach and particular inclusion/exclusion criteria implemented, concentrating explicitly on ATCCS and the specified intervention time frames. Our methodology, though it refined our scope, facilitated a nuanced exploration of the impacts of early surgical intervention in ATCCS, potentially leading to our distinctive, not statistically significant findings. This deviation does not challenge the wider consensus on the advantages of early intervention but instead offers a nuanced viewpoint in managing ATCCS. The role of early surgery in enhancing neurological recovery remains an area of active research and is garnering increasing attention. Taken together with the complication rates, our study in fact bolsters the compelling evidence for early (≤ 24 hours) intervention and the emerging consensus and advocates for early surgery as a pivotal strategy in enhancing patient outcomes and minimizing complications for patients with spinal cord injury.45–47

### Limitations

The design of our systematic review could have been prone to publication heterogeneity to some extent, as well as variability in the quality of the studies included. The accuracy of our findings is reliant on the quality of the original studies and the data provided. All the articles we included provided level IV evidence. Although a statistically significant difference existed in the rates of complications, the occurrence rate was low. Most of the included articles did not report comorbidities, constraining our ability to discuss their potential impact on delaying surgical management; it is plausible that a higher prevalence of comorbidities in the > 24-hour group could have served as a confounding factor. Any missing or inconsistently reported data in these studies could have influenced our analyses. Heterogeneity among studies, in terms of the patient populations, surgical techniques, and outcome measures, can impact the comparability of the results.

## Conclusions

The present study challenges the notion that early surgical intervention in ATCCS leads to higher complications. Instead, a statistically significant lower complication rate was observed in the ≤ 24-hour group, implying a potential safety benefit of early surgical intervention. Our findings did not show a statistically significant difference in neurological outcomes between early (≤ 24 hours) and delayed (> 24 hours) surgery. Surgical decisions should be individualized, considering each patient’s unique clinical characteristics and injury patterns.
