A B S T R A C T

Pediatric low-grade gliomas are the most common brain tumours in childhood and adolescence. Despite the excellent prognosis, pediatric low-grade glioma survivors may suffer from variable long-term complications and may require repeated therapies, implying that this is a chronic disease. The current review describes the European Standard Clinical Practice recommendations for low-grade gliomas at primary diagnosis, that were developed on behalf of SIOPe BTG LGG Working Group within the framework of European Reference Network PaedCan. The manuscript describes the diverse spectrum of pediatric low-grade gliomas in terms of location, age, underlying cancer predisposition syndromes, and special circumstances, such as infantile chiasmatic hypothalamic glioma and diencephalic syndrome, as well as current diagnostic criteria and indications for treatment. Furthermore, it provides current knowledge in histopathology and molecular pathology. Finally, the review focuses on the need for a multidisciplinary approach and treatment indications providing a guide on current treatment modalities, used as first-line therapy in Europe along with information on adverse effects, and followup.

## 1. Introduction

Paediatric low-grade gliomas (pLGG) form a heterogeneous group of different WHO grade 1 and 2 tumours. They are the most common brain tumours in childhood, accounting for approximately 35 % during the first year of life and up to 50 % in older children [1] . Prognosis is generally excellent, with a 10-year overall survival (OS) between 85 % and 96 % [2 – 4] . However, pLGG survivors often suffer from chronic neurological, visual, endocrine, cognitive, and/or functional complications as a result of either their disease and/or treatment. For patients with tumours that cannot be fully resected this can lead to a chronic progressive disease both due to further tumour progression and re-treatment and also further progressive tumor-related brain injury without actual radiological tumour progression. Surgical resection, when feasible, is considered the mainstay of treatment. The CCG9891/POG9130 study reported a 5-year PFS and OS of 94 % and 99 % respectively in children with confirmed gross total resection [4]. However, a relevant amount of tumours are not amenable to complete resection because of anatomical location and /or metastatic disease.

The carboplatin and vincristine regimen remains the standard firstline systemic treatment for patients with pLGG, with the SIOP LGG 2004 Trial showing a 5-year OS and progression-free survival (PFS) of 89 % and 46.1 % respectively [2]. Carboplatin and vincristine using a different schedule and dosages have also been validated by the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) resulting in similar findings (39 % event-free survival (EFS) for non-NF1 patients) [6]. Vinblastine monotherapy as first-line treatment for patients with pLGG needing chemotherapy is considered the standard of care for the Canadian Group (5-year PFS 42 % in non-NF1 patients) [7–9]. Nowadays, vinblastine is also used as a first-line treatment in many European centers.

Historically, radiotherapy was the treatment of choice for symptomatic patients with unresectable pLGG. The 5-year PFS reported for the subgroup with first-line radiotherapy in the German SIOP LGG 2004 trial is 80 % which has to be balanced against the risk of treatmentrelated toxicity [10,11]. Indeed, in the context of a low-grade tumour, potential sequelae associated with radiotherapy were a major concern, especially in young children, demonstrating serious neurocognitive, neurologic, endocrine or vascular abnormalities as well as risk of second malignancy [12–15]. The risk of late effects depends on patients age, the tumour location and radiation field as well as relevant dose to the organs at risk like left temporal lobe, left hippocampus, or the hypothalamic-pituitary region [16]. Furthermore, Fisher’s series showed that the overall survival of the patients receiving radiotherapy immediately following incomplete resection and those with radiation following progression was the same after 5 and 10 years [17]. Moreover, PFS did not differ by management (P:0.32). PFS after 3 and 5 years was 58 %±5 % and 48 %±5 % for observation alone, 56 %±6 % and 48 % ±6 % with immediate radiotherapy, and 43 %±13 % and 36 %±13 % with immediate chemotherapy[17]. Additionally, a Canadian population-based retrospective study of 1202 pLGG patients (median follow-up 12.73 years) raised major concerns regarding the association of upfront radiotherapy with delayed increased overall and tumour-related late deaths[18].

During the past decade, the involvement of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPk) pathway in pLGG became evident and allowed the investigation of targeted therapies [19–22]. MEK inhibitors (including selumetinib and trametinib) and recently also RAF-inhibitors have been investigated in phase 1 and 2 trials [23], and are continuing to be studied in randomized phase 3 trials [24,25]. Recently, the combination of dabrafenib-trametinib resulted in significantly more responses and longer PFS in pLGG patients with BRAFV600 mutations when compared to standard chemotherapy (carboplatin plus vincristine) in the first-line non-surgical therapy setting [26]. Following on from this trial, there are at present at least four ongoing clinical trials for BRAF altered or other pLGG types with some of them excluding V600E BRAF mutation, that are randomizing between standard chemotherapy and targeted therapy as first-line non-surgical therapy for LGGs (LOGGIC-Firefly 2 [27], PLGG-METRIC in France, COG trials ACNS1831 for NF1-related LGGs and ACNS1833).

Several strategies for tumour control for pLGG exist and a key aspect of overall management is to preserve neurological, visual, endocrinological, and functional outcome.

## 2. Background of European Standard Clinical Practice Project and methodology

European Reference Networks (ERNs) are virtual networks aiming to bring together healthcare providers across Europe to improve the management of complex or rare medical conditions requiring highly specialized treatment and concentration of knowledge and recourses. PaedCan is one of the 24 ERNs approved by the ERN Board of Member States in December 2016. Its main goal is to reduce inequalities in childhood cancer survival by providing high-quality, accessible, and cost-effective cross-border healthcare to children and adolescents with cancer in Europe, regardless of where they live.

The European Standard Clinical Practice (ESCP) Project is the result of a collaboration between the ERN PaedCan and the European Society for Paediatric Oncology (SIOP Europe’s or SIOPe) Clinical Trial Groups, aiming to provide clinical recommendations to reflect the current best practices for each common childhood cancer type. The development of ESCP Protocols for brain tumours was initiated by the SIOPe Brain Tumor Group (BTG) and for each tumour type the relevant working group was involved. The ESCP recommendations for primary pLGG were initially developed by the authors of the current review. After approval by the members of the whole LGG WG, the manuscript was reviewed by ERN PaedCan and SIOPe board members and finally approved for publication in the SIOPe members’ portal, which is exclusive to SIOP Europe members.

## 3. Patient groups

Patients diagnosed with LGG can be subdivided into different categories according to:

## ● Location of the tumour

Low-grade tumours characteristically are clustered anatomically to midline supratentorial structures in 39–40 %, including the hypothalamus, optic chiasm and nerves, ventricular system, and other midline structures; the cerebellum in 32–36 %; the cerebral cortex in 13.1–18.0 %; the brainstem in 8.3–10 %; and the spinal cord in 3.5–4.5 % [3,28].

Tumour location in combination with the grade of the tumour and the potential risk of permanent neurological deficit determines the degree of resectability.

Hemispheric and cerebellar pLGGs are usually amenable to surgery, however, there are considerations such as possible neurological sequelae if the tumour is located in eloquent areas i.e. the perirolandic region, the mesial structure in the temporal lobes, or dominant language areas [29,30]. Patients with cerebellar LGGs may develop fine motor skills deficits, uncoordinated movement, sensory or oculomotor deficits, and cognitive and learning disabilities postoperatively [31–33]. It should be underlined that patients who undergo resection of posterior fossa pLGGs although less frequent than patients with medulloblastoma, might also experience cerebellar mutism syndrome, especially if the tumor involves the middle cerebellar peduncle, the dentate nucleus, or is localized in the floor of the fourth ventricle [32–36]

Chiasmatic hypothalamic gliomas (CHG) and other optic pathway gliomas are sub-divided into NF1-related and sporadic tumours, with differences in the natural history and also imaging features. NF1-related tumours generally have a better PFS, and many do not require therapy. Both NF1 and sporadic tumours may lead to visual loss, endocrinopathies, hypothalamic symptoms such as diencephalic syndrome in young children, and also hydrocephalus if third ventricle obstruction occurs. They are generally not amenable to complete resection via neurosurgery, however well accessible exophytic tumour parts may be partially resected to achieve better tumour control.

Thalamic pLGGs are rare, 1–2 %, often presenting with hemiplegia and/or hemianopsia [37,38].

Tectal plate gliomas are pLGGs occurring predominantly in children and constitute 5 % of paediatric brainstem tumours. They present with symptoms of raised intracranial pressure and usually require CSF diversion at diagnosis, which can often be achieved by endoscopic third ventriculostomy[39].

<!-- page: 3 -->

Cervico-medullary pLGGs are slow-growing tumours presenting with lower brainstem symptoms with high long-term survival and high risk of progression/recurrence, even late ones. Therapy should be directed at achieving local tumour control while preserving and /or even restoring neurological function [40].

Spinal pLGGs are rare and characterized by insidious nature. They can cause clear neurological deficits but also vague, generalized symptoms in young children. Thus, their diagnosis can be delayed until the intensity of symptoms becomes significant or debilitating [41,42]. Infiltration and indistinct margins with normal tissues often make complete resection difficult and if indicated must be performed under intraoperative neuromonitoring. Gross total resection when feasible appears to be the most important factor for PFS, (repeated) chemotherapy may contribute to tumour control and reducing morbidity and seems to result in comparable tumour control compared with radiotherapy [42,43].

## ● Age

In infants, pLGGs carry a unique clinical profile and additional management challenges [44,45]. In general, infantile gliomas are more common in the hypothalamic or optic pathway area (61–67 %) [3,46], which limits resectability and they are more likely to be metastatic (23 %) and progressive than LGG in older children [46]. Notably, infants less than 1 year of age with LGGs have significantly worse overall survival than older children (10-year OS of 71.4 % versus 91.6 % respectively) [44].

Infant CHG refers to children< 1 year of age at diagnosis with any LGG histology and chiasmatic hypothalamic glioma location, independent of neurologic and/or visual symptoms.

Diencephalic syndrome is defined as a syndrome of emaciation with normal to accelerated linear growth and BMI or weight for length ≤2 SD in young children with a tumour in the chiasmatic-hypothalamic region considered as an additional unfavorable factor for both OS and EFS [28].

## • Underlying cancer predisposition syndromes

Cancer predisposition syndromes associated with pLGG development are:

- 1. Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) accounts for 10–20 % of all p LGGs, mainly optic pathway gliomas [28,47].
- 2. Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) associated with Subependymal Giant Cell Astrocytoma (SEGA) development, accounting for 1–2 % of pLGGs [28,48,49].
- 3. RASopathies, other than NF1, such as Noonan syndrome in which patients have an increased risk of developing pLGGs, mainly dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors [50,51].
- 4. Constitutional mismatch repair syndrome (CMMRD) can lead to malignant gliomas, but also LGGs mimicking pleomorphic xanthoastrocytomas [53]. Importantly a cumulative likelihood of transformation to high-grade gliomas is 100 % in 6 years [52].

## • Presence of metastasis

Leptomeningeal dissemination is seen in 4–12 % of children with LGG either at diagnosis or progression and results in worse outcomes (5, 10, and 15-year OS of 80.7 %, 63 %, and 50.9 % respectively) [54,55]. It should be mentioned that a spine MRI is typically recommended in infants, but otherwise only when there is a concern for metastatic disease and in some cases where the patient is enrolled in a clinical trial [56]. The same applies to CSF examination for the presence of neoplastic cells. In case of leptomeningeal enhancement on MRI, the differential diagnosis should always include diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumour (DLGNT) [57].

All patients with LGG form two groups according to indication for management Observation group and Group needing treatment.

## 4. Diagnostic criteria

### 4.1. Clinical presentation

It is highly dependent on the localization of the lesion. The symptomatology may change over time and often is atypical or attributed to other diagnoses, thus diagnosis could be delayed [60]. Patients with pLGG can present with one or a combination of the following:

#### 4.1.1. Symptom-free via screening or other reason for MRI

The rate of incidentalomas, which are incidentally detected brain tumours in children without compatible symptoms is growing, due to the availability and widespread use of neuroimaging for various indications, such as traumatic injury or headache work-up [58,61]. They are found in 0.2 – 5.7 % of brain images and can pose a considerable burden for patients, their families, and caregivers. Although mostly presumed LGG, incidentalomas are usually radiologically defined tumors without histological verification, and they are thus not within the scope of the current clinical practice recommendation [58,59]. Diagnosis of a pLGG can also result from surveillance imaging of patients with cancer predisposition syndromes. It is estimated that ~15 % of children with NF1 are found to have an optic-pathway glioma often via screening, with almost 50 % of them remaining asymptomatic or nonprogressive [62].

- 4.1.2. Neurology [63] pLGGs typically may present with:

- -Raised ICP and obstructive hydrocephalus where lesions obstruct the ventricular outflow, mainly cerebellar and tectal lesions. Headache and nausea/vomiting are the most common complaints. In infants, an increase in head circumference can be the only presenting sign.
- -Focal Deficits such as motor weakness, sensory loss, language difficulty, cognitive difficulty, change of personality, and decline in academic or athletic performance. Cortical tumours usually present with focal neurological deficits including seizures. Brain stem gliomas can present with fatigue, ataxia, and multiple cranial neuropathies, whereas cerebellar tumours usually present with cerebellar signs with coordination or balance disturbance,signs of increased intracranial pressure, and /or palsy of the 6th cranial nerve. Some posterior fossa tumours may involve both the brain stem and cerebellum usually via the cerebellar peduncles, and vomiting without other signs of raised ICP may also be caused by tumours involving the area postrema.
- -Epilepsy: tumour location influences the risk for epilepsy. Tumours involving the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes are more commonly associated with seizures compared to those involving occipital lesions. Cortical tumours have a higher incidence of associated epilepsy compared to non-cortical deeper lesions. Certain histological types are correlated with chronic drug-resistant epilepsy i.e. gangliogliomas and dysplastic neuroepithelial tumours [64,65]. Infratentorial tumours rarely cause seizures.

- 4.1.3. Vision

Varying signs or symptoms of visual dysfunction from asymptomatic to severe visual loss can be observed in optic pathway gliomas. Decreased visual acuity is the most common symptom observed. Furthermore, various visual field defects can be detected, such as central scotoma, bitemporal hemianopsia, and peripheral contraction.

- • Monocular vision loss can be observed in the case of an optic nerve glioma.
- • Unilateral vision loss, strabismus, and/or proptosis are described in tumours located in the optic tract, more common in NF1 patients,

whereas homonymous hemianopia can be observed in tumours located in the optic radiations.

• Bilateral vision deterioration, visual field loss, nystagmus, and eventually loss of visual acuity might be observed in patients with chiasmatic tumours.

Visual assessment (visual acuity, visual fields, fundoscopy, and increasingly optical coherence tomography (OCT)) plays a critical role in the management of these patients and presents significant challenges in young children and uncooperative patients. (see also section 2.1.5.2)

Risk factors for the visual deficit are considered younger age at presentation, optic nerve atrophy at presentation, and postchiasmatic involvement [66,67].

#### 4.1.4. Endocrine disorders

Abnormalities in weight, growth, precocious or delayed puberty, and/or very rarely polydipsia/polyuria with diabetes insipidus may also be the presenting symptoms of LGG with hypothalamic involvement. Although rare, intracranial germ cell tumors can primarily involve the anterior visual pathways and should be considered in differential diagnosis, especially in older children and / or the setting of diabetes insipidus, work-up should include germ cell tumour markers and knowledge of imaging characteristics of GCTs vs LGG [68,69].

In diencephalic syndrome in suprasellar midline LGG as described above, a disordered metabolism results in failure to thrive, severe emaciation, and near absence of subcutaneous fat, which mainly occurs in young patients.

### 4.2. Imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging is the gold standard modality for evaluating central nervous system neoplasms. Contrast-enhanced MR should be obtained at the time of tumour diagnosis. MRI studies of grade 1 pLGG show well-circumscribed lesions, with T1-hypointensity and T2hyperintensity. Grade 1 astrocytomas often show homogenous enhancement with gadolinium administration, while grade 2 diffuse astrocytomas are typically non-enhancing, and possibly less circumscribed.

Based on the recently published imaging guidelines for pLGG, the following are recommended [56]:

## At diagnosis

- • All patients should have a presurgical baseline MRI scan to assess the tumour, and a postoperative MRI scan within 24–72 h after surgery for the evaluation of the amount of residual tumour unless a suitable intra-operative or immediate post-operative MRI was made.
- • In cases where surgery is not indicated or only a small biopsy sample is taken, the diagnostic or prebiopsy MRI scan could serve as baseline scan pretreatment.
- • In situations where extensive parenchymal postoperative changes could obscure a residual tumour with urgent indication to treat, a second MRI approximately 2–3 weeks after surgery might better define residual disease according to RAPNO Working Group recommendations[56].
- • A spine MRI for patients with primary pLGG in their brain is recommended, in infants, and when there is a concern for metastatic disease (i.e. symptoms that can indicate spinal disease or infants), before surgical intervention, or 10–14 days after [56].
- • For spinal LGG, a baseline brain MRI should be performed.

The safest available contrast agents (currently, macrocyclic gadolinium-based contrast agents) should be used, if possible, to reduce the risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis and to minimize tissue deposition (i.e. brain deposition) [56,70]. Also, if possible, the same contrast agent should be used in the patients throughout their surveillance.

Currently, the standard practice is to use contrast agents during follow-up of LGG patients regardless of time since diagnosis, complete resection, histological diagnosis, or contrast enhancement of the tumour. Nevertheless, as per the RAPNO working group comment, in the rare situation that a pLGG is completely non-enhancing, imaging without contrast can be considered in follow-up surveillance [56].

For further details, please refer to ESCP Brain Tumour Groupoverview and discipline groups-Imaging Working Group (standardclinical-practice-recommendations-brain-tumour-group-overview-anddiscipline-groups.pdf (siope.eu, exclusively for SIOPE members), and also the SIOPe MRI guidelines for imaging patients with central nervous system tumours [71].

### 4.3. Histopathology

LGG are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that primarily encompass tumours of glial histology, including astrocytic and/or oligodendroglial, and tumours of mixed neuronal-glial morphology. These tumours were considered grades I and II according to the previous WHO classification where they were distinguished from high-grade glioma based on specific morphologic features or, in the case of diffuse glioma, based on the absence of necrosis, mitoses, and microvascular proliferation [72].

According to the new 2021 WHO Classification for the CNS tumours, pLGG are now classified by histological and histogenetic similarities, even though molecular signatures vary, within the group Gliomas, Glioneuronal Tumours, and Neuronal Tumours [72,73].

Pathology work-up for histomorphological assessment of tissue for LGG includes:

- -stainings of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) material with HE-, PAS-, Alcianblue- and Reticulin-staining,
- -standard immunohistochemistry (IHC) encompasses a wider panel depending upon tumour differentiation (glial markers [GFAP, Olig2, S100 protein, vimentin], neuronal epitopes [synaptophysin, NeuN, chromogranin, neurofilaments; in case of glioneuronal differentiation], proliferation markers [Ki-67, phosphor-histone3], CD 68, CD 34, p53, Synaptophysin, p53, Neurofilament; Chromogranine and NeuN in case of glioneuronal differentiation. phospho-ERK, phosphor-mTOR, and phosphor-AKT in selected cases) [1].

Additional work-up includes:

- • IDH 1(R132H) and BRAFV600E to distinguish gliomas with possible different biologic behaviour,
- • H3K27M in case of midline tumours or spinal location to distinguish less expected high-grade tumours.

The morphological features of pilocytic astrocytomas are distinctive and characterized by extensive vascular proliferation, biphasic architecture, Rosenthal fibers, eosinophilic granular bodies, and occasionally, regions of limited calcification.

Gangliogliomas are another LGG (grade 1) of mixed neuronal-glial subtype, also characterized by granular bodies and regions of calcification., that also exhibit binucleated neurons and cystic degeneration. They are genetically defined by alterations that activate the MAP kinase signaling pathway in the vast majority of cases, either via the BRAF p. V600E mutation or one of the other genetic alterations including alternative BRAF fusion, RAF1 fusion, KRAS mutation, NF1 mutation, or FGFR mutations or fusion [74].

Angiocentric gliomas which occur predominantly in children and young adults with refractory epilepsy as the leading clinical symptom, were initially included in the 2007 WHO classification. Histopathologically, they are characterized by monomorphous bipolar cells, an angiocentric growth pattern, and immunoreactivity for EMA, GFAP, S100 protein, and vimentin, but not for neuronal antigens. MYB-QKI fusion is identified in 80–90 % of angiocentric gliomas [73,75,76].

Diffuse leptomeningeal glioneuronal tumors (DLGNT) are rare CNS neoplasms with a male predilection and usually slow progression.

Radiologically, they are characterized by leptomeningeal enhancement on MRI, although they may not necessarily present with gross leptomeningeal dissemination. Patients with DLGNT have been found to have a loss of chromosomal arm 1p and may harbor genetic alterations of the MAPK/ERK pathway activation, most frequently KIAA1549:BRAF fusion (55 %) [77,78] Currently, two molecular subgroups have been identified showing 1p deletion in both, named DLGNT-MC-1 and DLGNT-MG-2. The MC-1 group is characterized by lower age at diagnosis, 19q co-deletion, and less aggressive outcome whereas all patients in DLGNT-MC-2 group displayed a gain of chromosomal arm 1q [77].

For further details and other rare LGG subtypes, please refer to ESCP Brain Tumour Group-overview and discipline groups-Neuropathology (standard-clinical-practice-recommendations-brain-tumour-groupoverview-and-discipline-groups.pdf (siope.eu, exclusively for SIOPE members) and also the 2021 WHO classification of tumors, 5th edition, central nervous system tumors [79].

4.4. Molecular pathology [22,76,80,81]

Classification of pLGG can be supported via methylation profiling. As the comprehensive molecular landscape of pLGG provides clinicians with significant data that improve the management of different molecular subtypes of pLGGs [22], nowadays molecular pathology is considered a standard of care.

The majority of pLGGs are driven by a single genetic event resulting in up-regulation of the RAS/MAPK pathway. The most common alterations in pLGGs described so far are:

- • Neurofibromatosis type 1: the first indication of RAS/MAPK involvement in LGG came from NF1 patients of whom 10–15 % develop a low-grade glioma within the optic pathway and an additional 3–5 % arising outside the optic pathway. A recent study revealed that patients with NF1 and pLGG often harbor an additional somatic genetic alteration in the MAPK pathway in addition to the germline NF1 alteration (second-hit) [82].
- • KIAA1549-BRAF fusion is the most frequent molecular alteration in pLGG and is significantly enriched in pilocytic astrocytoma and in tumours arising in the posterior fossa/cerebellum [52].
- • BRAFp.V600E mutation: the prevalence varies notably depending on histology and location and is frequently associated with additional alterations, most commonly deletion of CDKN2A. Patients with BRAFp.V600E mutated tumours have worse progression-free survival compared to those with KIAA1549-BRAF [22]. Tumors with BRAFpV600E mutation and CDKN2A deletion have an increased likelihood of malignant behavior [83].
- • FGFR1 alterations including point mutation and kinase domain duplications are more frequent in dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors, other glioneuronal tumors, and midline brain tumors. After BRAF it is the second most commonly altered gene.
- • Other BRAF or RAF fusions
- • A variety of relatively rare alterations of NTRK, RAF1, ALK, PTNP11, and others are also found in pLGG. The impact of these alterations on prognosis and response to treatment is still under investigation.
- • Alterations of MYB/MYBL1, often seen in angiocentric glioma, seem to define a class of pediatric-type diffuse gliomas.
- • Germline mutations in TSC1 and TSC2 are closely associated with subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, which occurs in up to 20 % of patients with tuberous sclerosis complex [84].

To distinguish unexpected high-grade tumours, testing for the absence of H3K27M mutations in brain stem and midline thalamic tumours and H3G34R/H3G34V in cerebral hemisphere tumors, which are found in high-grade gliomas is recommended [85,86].

Finally, testing for IDH1 mutations -most frequently at position p. R132- can be considered, especially in adolescents. IDH1 mutations although common in almost 70–90 % of adult WHO grade II and III gliomas and 85 % of secondary GBMs [87], are rare in pLGG accounting for only 0–17 % of the cases [76]. COG reported a 16 % incidence of IDH1 mutations in adolescents over the age of 14 years [88]. Recently, Ryall et al. reported the presence of IDH1p.R132H mutations in 0.8 % of 1000 patients with pLGG with a median age of diagnosis at 15.7 years [22]. The clinical impact of these mutations in children is not yet clearly understood but might be that these tumours will not behave as most other pLGG over the long term and could represent adult-type malignancies that have been identified early [89].

Recent integrated molecular profiling has facilitated the delineation of additional diagnostic subclasses such as polymorphous low-grade neuroepithelial tumour of the young (PLNTY), and isomorphic diffuse glioma [90]. Methylation profiling may aid in the prediction of clinical behavior as recently supported by Sexton-Oates et al [91]. Furthermore, Sturm et al. showed that molecular profiling improved the identification of histological high-grade gliomas displaying lower-grade molecular profiles [92].

Devising a simple recommendation concerning molecular testing/ profiling for pLGG can be difficult. Ultimately, as proposed by Miklja et al., there are two primary approaches to the problem:

- • Sequential testing of specific alterations in a based tier-based approach or
- • Upfront NGS panels optimized for pLGG [85].

In general, it is advised -if possible- to join the LOGGIC Core Bioclinical Data Bank currently recruiting data and tumor tissue of patients with pLGG across Europe [93].

For further details, please refer to ESCP Brain Tumour Groupoverview and discipline groups-Neuropathology (standard-clinicalpractice-recommendations-brain-tumour-group-overview-and-discipline-groups.pdf (siope.eu, exclusively for SIOPE members).

### 4.5. Neurological, visual and endocrine function

#### 4.5.1. Neurologist

Neurological examination at baseline is crucial since it defines an indication for observation or treatment, as well as epilepsy control. Subsequently, this baseline information is used to determine neurological treatment response: stable functioning, worsening, or improvement.

#### 4.5.2. Ophthalmologist

Visual functioning testing is determined by age-appropriate techniques for examination of visual acuity and visual field assisted by fundoscopy and OCT, especially in the case of tumours involving the optic pathway.

Visual symptoms/signs of OPG are influenced by factors like tumour extension, tumour volume, patient age, associated raised intracranial pressure and the presence/absence of NF1. The current recommendations used and published by the REiNS (Response Evaluation in Neurofibromatosis and Schwannomatosis) and pLGGs International Consensus paper should be followed when assessing visual outcomes [94].

This baseline ophthalmologic information is used to determine the indication for treatment as well as the response over time: stable functioning, worsening, or improvement of vision. Special consideration should be taken in systematic clinical examination of the different types of eye movements including eye position, range of eye movements, smooth pursuit, saccades, gaze-holding functions, and optokinetic nystagmus as well as testing for different types of nystagmus (i.e. isolated dysfunction of vertical eye movements is due to a midbrain lesion). Infratentorial tumours may present with eye movement disorders related to cranial nerve failure.

#### 4.5.3. Endocrinologist

## 5. Treatment details

### 5.1. Multidisciplinary tumor board

Decisions on treatment and/ or follow-up in pLGG need multidisciplinary information, approach, and interpretation. The multidisciplinary tumour board should consist of neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, neurology, neuroradiology, radiation oncology, and neuropathology expertise and should meet at regularly scheduled intervals to optimize diagnosis, follow-up, and treatment [96,97] . Involvement of other specialties including ophthalmology, audiology, endocrinology and neuropsychology is extremely important.

### 5.3. Treatment modalities

#### 5.3.1. Surgery

In general, surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment in LGG. When feasible surgical management is guided by anatomical location, demarcation on imaging and histology [96] . Modern technology such as microsurgery, neuronavigation, intraoperative imaging (iUS or iMRI), intraoperative neuromonitoring have become essential tools to be applied in pediatric neurooncological surgery. If resection is not recommended by an experienced pediatric neurosurgeon, biopsy should always be thoroughly considered for symptomatic tumors excepting

NF1-related OPG. Even in cases where the plan is observation, knowledge of molecular pathology may in future guide the observation strategy. Surgical options for biopsy include neuroendoscopy, stereotactic or robot-assisted biopsies and can provide all necessary molecular information.

A multidisciplinary tumour board is essential for making decisions concerning biopsy or extent of resection at the time of diagnosis and progression, in the context of an expected long-term survival in the majority of cases and preserving quality of life the potential risk of surgery vs the therapeutic benefit as well as elucidating the histologic and molecular subtype of the tumour. Gross total resection is the initial therapeutic approach, if applicable, due to the risk of cerebellar mutism syndrome, cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, and cranial nerve dysfunction and should also be addressed in the MDT setting, in posterior fossa location. For tumours located in the supratentorial midline region, the optic pathway/hypothalamus, and the brainstem, partial resection or biopsy is often a more appropriate approach, considering the eloquent areas of tumor location. Thus, it is recommended that biopsy or surgical resection of tumours in surgically high-risk brain regions should only be undertaken by paediatric neurosurgeons with experience operating in these regions.

Currently, typical intrinsic optic pathway tumours in association with NF1, do not require biopsy at the time of diagnosis, unless they are being managed in a clinical trial that involves a relevant biological stratification/question or they present with atypical features [98] . NF1-LGG outside the optic pathway especially if progressing or atypical by imaging should considered to be biopsied if possible.

For cerebral low-grade epilepsy-associated tumours (LEATs) the decision for surgical treatment is made based on radiological appearance, possible surgical approaches for safe tumor resection, and refractoriness of epilepsy [99] . Shorter duration of symptoms, partial/focal seizures, and gross total excision were predictors of a good seizure outcome [99 – 101] . This patient group also is treated in dedicated neurosurgical teams for epilepsy neurosurgery [102] .

Regarding surgery of pLGG with relation to eloquent areas, neurosurgical strategies such as the use of intraoperative MRI or monitoring may be appropriate and surgical second opinion/discussion may be relevant.

### 5.3.2. Chemotherapy first-line treatment regimens

## Carboplatin-Vincristine regimen [103]

Carboplatin-Vincristine is the most commonly used regimen. The duration of therapy is 81 weeks as per the European schedule, consisting of an induction phase followed by a consolidation phase(Annex A).

For Carboplatin-vincristine regime details, please refer to the ESCP LGG document (CRCTU-PRT-QCD-001 (siope.eu).

Treating physicians should be aware of carboplatin hypersensitivity reaction (HSR) which increases with repeated exposure to carboplatin and reported rates between 6 % and 32 % [104]. Alternative treatment choices to finish first-line treatment are cisplatin/vincristine (A) with cyclophosphamide/ vincristine (B) every 6 weeks [LGG2004 protocol] (Annex A) or a switch to weekly vinblastine monotherapy (Annex A).

## Vinblastine regimen (Annex A)[8]

First-line treatment with Vinblastine is applied until a total duration therapy of 70 weeks [105]. The following should also be taken into consideration:

- • Vinblastine monotherapy is used as first-line treatment in several centers internationally, initially in NF1 and older patients.
- • The recommended dosage for vinblastine in the published series was 6 mg/m2 although in almost 70 % of the patients, a reduced dosage was required, due to hematological toxicity[8]. The proposed starting dosage, although not tested in protocols with published data is 0, 17 mg/kg BW or 5 mg/m2 (max:10 mg/dose) iv push weekly for BSA<0,6 m2 or BSA≥0.6 m2 respectively.

For further details, please refer to the ESCP LGG document (CRCTUPRT-QCD-001 (siope.eu), and Lassaletta et al. article [8].

### 5.3.3. Targeted therapy

Until recently, targeted therapy as first-line therapy for pLGG was only acceptable in the context of clinical trials. Taking into consideration recently published data regarding dabrafenib plus trametinib for patients with pLGG and BRAF V600 mutations, FDA and EMA have approved the use of this combination as first-line treatment for pediatric patients ≥1 year of age with low-grade glioma and a BRAF V600E mutation requiring systemic therapy (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/me dicines/human/EPAR/finlee, updated on 05/12/2023). Furthermore, tovorafenib-a type II RAF inhibitor-was recently granted rare pediatric disease designation for the treatment of pLGG harboring activating RAF alteration that disproportionately affects children and is now included in the LOGGIC Firefly 2 trial [27]. Tovorafenib was also granted FDA approval for LGG with RAF alteration relapsing after first-line therapy (https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs

/fda-grants-accelerated-approval-tovorafenib-patients-relapsed-or-refra ctory-braf-altered-pediatric). It should be highlighted that the main unknown issues of targeted treatments remain optimal duration as well as potentially unknown very late effects, thus, additional studies are required.

• Everolimus for patients with SEGA

Like in other pLGG, multidisciplinary team decision should be performed to decide on medical or surgical therapy for SEGA. Everolimus dosage for patients with SEGA and TSC is 7 mg/m2 BSA po, once daily if their age is ≥1 year and < 3 years or for patients> 3 years at 4.5 mg.m2 BSA po, once daily aiming at trough levels of 5–15 ng/ml.

A detailed summary of dosages, known adverse events, dose modifications, as well as supportive care can be found at the SIOP ESCP LGG document (CRCTU-PRT-QCD-001 (siope.eu)

#### 5.3.4. Radiotherapy

The introduction of chemotherapy aims to delay or obviate the need for radiotherapy to minimize cognitive, endocrine, and vascular consequences obviously dependent on tumour location [106] and the development of radiation-induced malignant neoplasms [83]. Patients with panhypopituitarism already have deficient HPA axis. Patients younger than 5 years of age seem to be at highest risk of sequelae following conformal radiotherapy with a higher risk of moya-moya, increasing in supratentorial midline locations deficits over time, and greatest decline in cognition[12,106]. Furthermore, due to the neurocognitive decline observed in children with LGG younger than 7 years old and tumour location causing a higher volume and/ or dose to the left temporal lobe or hippocampus even in proton therapy regardless of NF1 status, younger age is considered as a limiting factor for choosing radiotherapy as first-line therapy [16,107,108]. Age (<10 years) has also been found to be a factor predisposing to vasculopathies [109]. Thus, radiotherapy is especially delayed in young patients.

Radiotherapy had previously been the mainstay of treatment for incompletely resected pLGG but nowadays remains a standard as salvage treatment or as primary treatment in selected cases in which surrounding normal tissue can be optimally preserved. Modern highprecision radiotherapy techniques, including proton therapy, have the potential to limit the development of long-term toxicities. Therefore a need for prospective studies exists to compare the efficacy and safety of modern radiotherapy with systemic treatment in pLGGs [110]. Radiotherapy dose between 50.4 and 54 Gy is recommended [111]. Lowering the dose to under 54 Gy could help reduce the risk of developing vasculopathies [112].

Up-front radiotherapy may be considered following multidisciplinary tumour board agreement in:

- • tumours with concerns regarding a high risk of significant morbidity in the event of minimal tumour growth
- • tumours with a low risk of radiotherapy-related neurocognitive effects
- • in children with disseminated disease craniospinal irradiation may be a treatment option when chemotherapy has failed [1].

Radiotherapy should be avoided as much as possible for children with NF1, as there is an increased risk of both second malignancy and vascular late effects in this patient group. Older patients with unresectable diffuse astrocytoma histology or midbrain/thalamus tumour location might have a suboptimal long-term survival and might benefit from early radiotherapy [113].

## 8. Patient follow-up

Follow-up of patients with LGG depends on patient-related factors such as age and underlying diagnosis of NF1 and on tumour-related factors such as primary site, extent of resection, integrative pathological diagnosis, type of treatment, history, and time from diagnosis in case of relapse, whereas late relapses although rare may occur [18,28,45, 115] . Krishnatry et al. reported that radiation therapy was associated with late all-cause and tumour-related deaths in 1202 adult pLGG survivors [18] . Recently, Zaazoue et al. suggested a less frequent imaging protocol for patients with gross total resection [116] . In this context, less frequent imaging follow-up than the proposed one in Table 3 described by Gnekow et al [1] . after the 5th year can be considered in patients with gross total resection.
