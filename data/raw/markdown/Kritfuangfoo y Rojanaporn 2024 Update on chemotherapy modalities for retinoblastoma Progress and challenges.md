A B S T R A C T

Retinoblastoma stands as a paradigm of success in treating malignancies among pediatric patients. Over recent decades, the approach to managing retinoblastoma has evolved significantly, transitioning from the preservation of patients' lives to the preservation of eyes and vision while minimizing treatment-related complications. Chemotherapy, administered through diverse routes, has solidified its role as the cornerstone of retinoblastoma treatment. In addition to intravenous chemotherapy (IVC), alternative administration routes, including intraarterial (IAC), intravitreal, intracameral, and periocular delivery, have emerged as promising modalities for retinoblastoma management. Numerous studies have demonstrated outstanding outcomes, achieving nearly 100% salvage rates for eyes classified under groups A–C. However, for advanced intraocular retinoblastoma (groups D and E eyes), IAC appears to offer superior local control rates compared to IVC. Intravitreal injection of chemotherapeutic agents, when administered in a controlled and secure manner, holds promise in averting the need for enucleation and radiotherapy in advanced retinoblastoma cases presenting with vitreous seeds. The optimal chemotherapy strategy remains meticulously tailored based on numerous factors. This review provides a comprehensive update on chemotherapy across various routes, encompassing key considerations, dosages, administration methods, treatment outcomes, and potential complications. Furthermore, it explores emerging potential treatments and outlines future directions aimed at enhancing treatment outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Retinoblastoma, the most common ocular malignancy in childhood, affecting approximately 1 in 15,000–20,000 live births, 1 demands prompt treatment due to its lethality if left unaddressed. In high-income countries, detection typically transpires before infiltration into the optic nerve, choroid, sclera, or orbit, resulting in nearly 100% disease-free survival rates. Conversely, in low-and-middle-income countries, particularly within the African context, this malignancy often manifests with indications of invasive disease, elevating the risks of both local and distant metastases and consequently contributing to substantially diminished survival rates. 2–5

Treatment strategies for this complex disease are constantly evolving, and although management protocols may vary across centers worldwide, the core objectives remain universal: preserving life, preventing metastasis, salvaging the globe, and optimizing vision. Prior to the 1990 s, enucleation and external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) were the mainstay of retinoblastoma treatment. However, these approaches were encumbered by significant drawbacks, including vision loss and severe side effects. The past few decades have witnessed a paradigm shift in retinoblastoma management, with the emergence of treatments offering improved outcomes in terms of disease-free survival, globe salvage, and even vision preservation in select cases. Focal therapies such as plaque radiotherapy, cryotherapy, laser photocoagulation, and transpupillary thermotherapy have played crucial roles in this evolution. 6–8 However, the transformational impact has undoubtedly been chemotherapy, administered through diverse routes, which we will delve into further in this review.

We undertook a thorough PubMed search for English language articles dating back to January 1, 1960, targeting articles from the period of January 1, 1990, to January 31, 2024. The search employed keywords included retinoblastoma, chemotherapy, intravenous chemotherapy, chemoreduction, intra-arterial chemotherapy, ophthalmic artery chemosurgery, intravitreal chemotherapy, intracameral chemotherapy, and periocular chemotherapy to ensure a comprehensive exploration of relevant literature. In this review, our objective is to summarize the current knowledge and provide updated insights into emerging trends regarding chemotherapy in retinoblastoma.

## 2. Intravenous chemotherapy

Following the initial observation by Kingston et al. in the early 1990 s that a repurposed neuroblastoma chemotherapy protocol showed efficacy in retinoblastoma, numerous studies confirmed its potential.7–9 Consequently, the utilization of systemic intravenous chemotherapy (IVC) remains integral to retinoblastoma treatment. Referred to as 'chemoreduction' due to its consistent efficacy in diminishing tumor size, IVC typically involves the administration of 2, 3, or 4 chemotherapeutic agents on a monthly basis, extending over 6–9 consecutive cycles.3 The commonly adopted treatment protocol, often incorporating vincristine, etoposide, and carboplatin (VEC), is widely utilized. In enhancing tumor control, focal consolidation through thermotherapy or cryotherapy is commonly incorporated. IVC proves to be effective not only in controlling intraocular retinoblastoma but also in preventing metastasis, reducing the occurrence of long-term second cancers, and diminishing the development of pineoblastoma.10–12

The criteria guiding the selection of diverse chemotherapy protocols exhibit variability among retinoblastoma treatment centers. Generally, systemic IVC is typically administered to patients meeting specific criteria, including those with bilateral retinoblastoma, familial retinoblastoma, germline mutation retinoblastoma, individuals aged 4 months or younger where IAC is challenging, as well as those presenting with suspicious evidence of early optic nerve or choroidal invasion.3 Additionally, the consideration for IVC extends to instances where IAC is unavailable or upon consideration of familial preferences.

Furthermore, subsequent to enucleation, a meticulous examination of the globe’s histopathological features becomes imperative.13,14 The presence of high-risk features, such as extraocular extension, extensive choroidal involvement (≥3 mm peripapillary), or post-laminar optic nerve invasion (≥1.5 mm), significantly increases the risk of metastasis. Studies by the Children's Oncology Group (COG) support this, emphasizing the importance of accurate histopathology for risk stratification.15 Their findings suggest that eyes with less than 3 mm choroidal involvement and any prelaminar/laminar optic nerve invasion have minimal recurrence risk and may not require adjuvant chemotherapy. Conversely, cases with extensive choroidal invasion and significant post-laminar optic nerve invasion exhibit poorer outcomes, necessitating a more intensive adjuvant IVC regimen.15

The efficacy of systemic IVC for retinoblastoma control and globe salvage is well-established. In 2006, Shields et al. reported on a large cohort of 249 cases treated with intravenous vincristine, etoposide, and carboplatin (VEC). They achieved globe salvage with 100% tumor control in group A eyes, 93% of group B, 90% of group C, and 47% of group D eyes. However, group E necessitated enucleation.16 Subsequently, in a 2020 publication, Shields et al. expanded their investigation to a larger cohort, comprising 964 eyes from 554 patients treated with similar intravenous chemotherapy. Kaplan-Meier analysis showcased favorable outcomes, revealing globe salvage and retinoblastoma control following a 6-cycle VEC regimen, resulting in tumor control and obviating the need for enucleation or EBRT in groups A (96%), B (91%), C (91%), D (71%), and selected cases of E (32%) within 3 years, remaining generally stable over 20 years.13 While the standard IVC regimen comprises six cycles of VEC with dosages adjusted based on disease stage, various modifications have been explored. Reduced regimens employing only two drugs (such as VE, VC, or EC) aim to minimize side effects, while enhanced regimens incorporating additional (potentially more toxic) second-line agents or extending treatment cycles beyond the standard six aim to improve globe salvage rates. A meta-analysis by Daniels et al. demonstrated the impact of chemotherapy regimen on success rates. They concluded that twodrug regimens were less effective for all retinoblastoma stages. Enhanced regimens exhibited higher efficacy compared to standard IVC for eyes with vitreous seeds, suggesting their potential role in such scenarios, particularly when IAC is unavailable.17 The outcomes and associated complications of these diverse IVC approaches have been documented in numerous studies, summarized in Table 1.

Moreover, the inclusion of additional intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC) and plaque radiotherapy emerged as valuable contributors,18,19 resulting in a substantial increase in globe salvage by 5–28%.13 A recent significant development involves the crucial role of intravitreal and intracameral chemotherapy, elevating local seed control and contributing significantly to overall globe salvage.20–22 Combining IVC with additional intravitreal melphalan may serve as an effective therapeutic strategy while also reducing the necessity for multiple examinations under anesthesia.23

IVC exhibits a favorable safety profile in retinoblastoma treatment. Systemic toxicity is minimal, with long-term hearing and renal complications being uncommon, especially when administered precisely.24,25 In a relevant report, documented side effects included hearing loss (including pre-existing cases, 4%), growth abnormalities (primarily associated with 13Q syndrome, 6%), transient vincristinerelated neuropathy (2%), and expected hematological responses like neutropenia, anemia, or thrombocytopenia.13 Notably, the study found no significant differences in systemic adverse events across retinoblastoma severity groups (ICRB A–E).13 Additionally, there is no evidence in the published literature suggesting that IVC negatively impacts fertility.25

While extraocular retinoblastoma is infrequent in developed countries, it is more commonly observed in the developing world, exhibiting poorer visual outcomes and survival rates compared to intraocular retinoblastoma. Contemporary management strategies predominantly employ a multimodal protocol, incorporating high-dose neoadjuvant chemotherapy, followed by enucleation or exenteration surgery, EBRT, and adjuvant chemotherapy.26–28 The existing literature on extraocular retinoblastoma is limited (Table 2), and treatment approaches continue to evolve.

## 3. Intraarterial chemotherapy

Intra-arterial chemotherapy (IAC), alternatively termed super-selective ophthalmic artery chemotherapy or chemosurgery, has gained widespread recognition as a primary treatment modality for retinoblastoma on a global scale. The groundbreaking work by Kaneko et al. in 1998 introduced a safe and effective technique for locally administering melphalan through the ophthalmic artery into eyes afflicted with retinoblastoma.29 Their method involved introducing a micro-balloon catheter through the transfemoral approach to the cervical segment of the internal carotid artery, distal to the ophthalmic artery ostium. This technique was aptly termed "selective ophthalmic artery infusion".

In 2006, Gobin et al. introduced a more selective technique involving direct catheterization of the ophthalmic artery using a guide wire. This advancement, termed "super-selective intra-ophthalmic artery chemotherapy," significantly enhanced the delivery of the chemotherapeutic agent.19 Subsequently, Abramson et al. reported encouraging initial results in 2008, noting that a majority of patients could achieve globe salvage with minimal side effects and local toxicity.30 IAC quickly emerged as a frontline management option for retinoblastoma.

Early IAC regimens for retinoblastoma primarily utilized melphalan as a single agent. This choice stemmed from its established potency against retinoblastoma,31 further supported by an in vivo study in rabbits demonstrating excellent vitreous and retinal penetration of the drug during IAC.32 While alternative regimens, such as the two-drug approach explored by Francis et al.,33 were investigated, a critical leap forward came with the introduction of a triple-drug combination by Abramson et al. and Marr et al., incorporating carboplatin and topotecan alongside melphalan.19,34 This combination therapy quickly gained popularity due to its potential for enhanced efficacy while maintaining a favorable safety profile.35

The application of IAC is generally indicated for patients undergoing primary treatment. Specifically, for unilateral retinoblastoma, it encompasses groups B, C, and D (cT1b, cT2, cT3, cT4), while for bilateral retinoblastoma, it involves groups D and E (cT3). 35 Furthermore, the consideration of IAC extends to secondary therapy in cases of prior treatment failure, encompassing recurrent tumors, persistent tumors, subretinal seeds, or a combination of these factors. 35 Additional uses for IAC involve employing tandem therapy (simultaneous bilateral IAC) in advanced bilateral cases with minimal exposure (less than two cycles). 43 The utilization of tandem therapy, however, remains a subject of controversy. 44 Concerns arise regarding potential heightened vascular toxicity in the better-seeing eye, uncertainties regarding its impact on preventing pineoblastoma, and limited efficacy in addressing pre-existing metastases, which could potentially contribute to increased child mortality. 3

IAC demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy in retinoblastoma. For Group B and C eyes, IAC achieves excellent outcomes comparable to IVC, with globe salvage rates ranging from 95% to 100%. 39 For Group D eyes, a meta-analysis revealed IAC's superiority over IVC, with a globe salvage rate of 79.5% versus 55% ( P < 0.001). 45 However, in Group E eyes, IAC and IVC showed no significant difference in globe preservation (51% versus 43%, respectively). 45

Further improvement in outcomes has been achieved through combining IAC with intravitreal chemotherapy. This combined approach significantly reduces the need for enucleation compared to IAC alone (15% versus 44%, P = 0.01), especially for Group E eyes (27% versus 73%, P = 0.04). 18 Shields et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of IAC alone in controlling local tumor recurrence. Following an average of three IAC sessions, they achieved complete control of solid tumors in 88%, subretinal seeds in 82%, and vitreous seeds in 67%. 46 Notably, recurrence and metastasis rates after IAC (15% and 2.7%, respectively) were not significantly different from those observed with IVC (15% and 0.6%, respectively). 45 Additionally, Liang et al. reported the effectiveness of primary IAC for advanced unilateral retinoblastoma, demonstrating a significantly higher 3-year ocular survival rate in group D compared to group E (97% versus 76%; P < 0.01). Vitreous hemorrhage was a common ocular complication, with no reported deaths or severe systemic complications. 47 Various studies reported treatment regimens and outcomes of IAC, as detailed in Table 3 .

Overall, IAC demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy in retinoblastoma. However, technical challenge is inevitable. IAC stands as a procedure demanding technical precision and significant financial resources, necessitating specialized facilities. Special endovascular expertise, especially with experience in intra-arterial drug delivery specific to retinoblastoma patient, is crucial for the treatment success, which may contribute to variable treatment outcomes across different centers. In contrast to IVC, IAC offers distinct advantages in terms of systemic side effects. IAC's shorter treatment duration minimizes overall exposure to chemotherapy, resulting in significantly reduced systemic toxicity. Although neutropenia has been documented in association with IAC, 35 its occurrence is notably lower compared to that observed with IVC. The occurrence of femoral artery occlusion and blue toe syndrome, potential complications of IAC, can be managed and reversed through anticoagulation. 19,50 Moreover, IAC carries inherent risks due to its neuroinvasive nature. The catheterization into femoral artery, aorta, and carotid artery may cause hematoma or arterial occlusion. Risks of potential stroke or transient ischemic attack has been reported. 40,51

A key difference between IAC and IVC lies in their systemic impact. Unlike IVC, IAC's localized delivery bypasses the systemic circulation, thereby eliminating the potential for chemoprotective effects in other organs. Consequently, the risk of developing systemic micro-metastases persists in retinoblastoma patients with advanced intraocular or extraocular diseases. Furthermore, IAC presents its own set of challenges. Successful implementation requires specialized endovascular expertise, particularly experience tailored to intra-arterial drug delivery in retinoblastoma patients. This expertise is crucial for optimizing treatment outcomes and minimizing complications. Therefore, thorough assessment of potential risks and benefits against the critical role of vision salvage becomes paramount in tailoring the optimal treatment strategy for each retinoblastoma patient.

## 4. Intravitreal chemotherapy

While diverse treatment protocols exist for retinoblastoma, vitreous seeding represents the primary obstacle to globe salvage in advanced intraocular cases. 3,52,53 This phenomenon often accompanies endophytic proliferation of large, undifferentiated tumors. Notably, pathological examination of vitreous seeds typically reveals necrotic tumor cells or viable cells posing a risk of further tumor growth. 54 Consequently, successful management of vitreous seeding remains a significant challenge in retinoblastoma treatment. 3,46,52 Although conventional therapies like systemic IVC, IAC, and radiotherapy may prove effective in certain cases, they often demonstrate limitations in eradicating established vitreous seeds. 3,55

Intravitreal chemotherapy (IvitC) for retinoblastoma emerged in the 1960 s, with thiotepa serving as early agent. However, achieving sustained success with IvitC remained inconsistent. 56,57 In 2003, Inomata and Kaneko conducted a crucial study, evaluating the efficacy of 12 anti-cancer drugs for potential eye-preserving treatment. Their findings revealed melphalan to be the most effective agent. 58 Building upon this, Suzuki and Kaneko performed a large-scale study, administering 896 melphalan injections in 237 eyes, with only one (0.4%) experiencing extraocular extension following injection with dense vitreous seeds and anterior chamber involvement. 59 In 2012, Munier et al. reported a notable safety profile and high success rate for IvitC in controlling tumors with vitreous seeding and minimal side effects, eliminating the need for external beam irradiation in all treated eyes. 20,60 These findings spurred further research on optimizing IvitC indications, techniques, and dosage to maximize both efficacy and safety.

Munier et al. described profiling safety of intravitreal injections for retinoblastoma using an anti-reflux procedure and sterilization of the needle track, which included anterior chamber paracentesis in order to create a transient hypotony prior to injection of intravitreal chemotherapy with a 32 G needle, and followed by three cycles of freeze and thaw cryotherapy at the injection site at the time of removal of the needle.60 Gentle jiggling of the eyeball with forceps ensures even drug distribution, reducing local toxicity. Subsequently, copious sterile saline irrigation of the ocular surface follows, along with topical steroid/antibiotic application. Patching and additional eye drops are typically not required. Family instruction focuses on avoiding eye contact for one week.53

A novel approach termed precision intravitreal chemotherapy (pIvitC) emerged in 2018 for treating localized vitreous seeding in retinoblastoma.62 This technique aims to optimize local delivery of melphalan directly to target seeds, maximizing efficacy while minimizing drug exposure to healthy tissues. Trans-pars plana needle insertion delivers the chemotherapy within 3 mm of the target seeds under indirect ophthalmoscopic guidance. Unlike traditional IvitC, eye movement is avoided post-injection to prevent unwanted drug dispersion. Instead, the patient's head is positioned with the seeds inferiorly, utilizing gravity to aid in drug localization and minimize exposure to the macula or other non-target areas.62 A study of 8 eyes treated with pIvitC demonstrated promising results. Complete resolution of vitreous seeds (100%) was achieved after an average of 2.6 injections of 20 μg melphalan, with no recurrence reported at a mean follow-up of 10.0 months.62 However, this challenging technique is not widely adopted, and the number studied is limited.

Furthermore, Shields et al. strengthened the evidence for IvitC efficacy by analyzing a larger case series of 40 consecutive eyes with persistent vitreous seeding after standard retinoblastoma treatment. Their study employed intravitreal melphalan (20–30 μg) with additional topotecan (20 μg) as needed, achieving a remarkable 100% tumor control with vitreous seed regression at a median 3-year followup after a total of 192 injections.63 In 2023, Abramson and Francis reported the safety and effectiveness of 90 µg/0.18 cc of intravitreal topotecan in treating recurrent solid retinal tumors in three retinoblastoma cases. They observed complete resolution of recurrent retinal tumors following a single injection of 90 µg of intravitreal topotecan, without any associated toxicity.64 Various studies reported IvitC outcomes, encompassing different chemotherapeutic agents and dosages, with associated complications detailed in Table 4.

Ocular side effects of IvitC are predominantly localized, with retinal pigment mottling at the injection site being the most frequent occurrence, having been reported in 18–43% of cases.20,65 Higher doses, particularly 50 μg of melphalan, have been associated with rare but severe complications like phthisis bulbi and hypotony, occasionally necessitating enucleation.66 Less frequently encountered effects include iris atrophy, chorioretinal atrophy, transient vitreous hemorrhage, and retinal detachment.67 Additionally, several studies have raised concerns about potential increased melphalan toxicity in eyes with deeper pigmentation, prevalent in Asian populations.21,68,69 This speculation is based on the possibility of higher melphalan absorption within more pigmented eyes, potentially leading to greater retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) toxicity and subsequent retinal and choroidal damage.70

The risk of extraocular tumor spread following IvitC is remarkably low. A meta-analysis by Smith et al. analyzed 1304 injections in 315 eyes of 304 patients from 1960 to 2013, revealing an extremely rare incidence of 0.007% for potential extraocular dissemination attributed to IvitC.71 A recent multicenter cohort study from 10 centers during 1999 to 2017 by Francis et al. observed no instances of extraocular extension of tumor events among 3553 injections delivered to 704 eyes. Applying the rule of three suggests a risk of extraocular extension was no greater than 0.08% per injection.72 This finding suggests that meticulous patient selection, strict adherence to safety-enhanced techniques, and appropriate dosage can effectively mitigate this minimal risk, offering promising outcomes in advanced retinoblastoma with vitreous seeding.

## 5. Intracameral chemotherapy

Management of advanced retinoblastoma, particularly cases with aqueous seeding, has advanced through newer treatment modalities, improving globe salvage rates. In 2017, Munier et al. introduced a novel intracameral chemotherapy (ICC) technique for aqueous seed control in unilateral retinoblastoma Group E, used adjunctively with other chemotherapeutic routes, demonstrating promising visual outcomes.73 This technique offers an alternative to enucleation or anterior chamber plaque radiotherapy, previously the only options for such cases due to the difficulty in delivering effective chemotherapeutic doses to the anterior chamber.

Munier et al.'s original bicameral injection technique targets both AC and posterior chamber (PC) seeding.73 This approach involves injecting the chemotherapeutic agent into both chambers through a small-gauge needle at the corneal periphery. One-third of the drug is injected into the AC, followed by perforation of the iris root in a tumorfree area and injection of the remaining two-thirds into the PC. Cryotherapy at the injection site concludes the procedure.73 Preoperative ultrasound bio-microscopy can assist in selecting the appropriate injection site. However, some authors involve injecting only a small portion of the drug into the anterior chamber, with or without paracentesis.22,74–76

Clear corneal or limbal injections of melphalan (15–20 μg/0.05 ml) and topotecan (7.5 μg/0.015 ml) into anterior and/or posterior chamber demonstrate efficacy in managing aqueous seeding in retinoblastoma. These studies report minimal anterior segment toxicity and no evidence of extraocular tumor spread.22,73,77 A retrospective study by Munier et al. showed success in 6 out of 11 patients, with an average of 4.3 injections. Disruption of the anterior hyaloid face emerged as the key risk factor for globe preservation failure. Adverse effects include iris heterochromia and progressive cataracts.77 Borroni et al.’s one-step aspiration and injection technique further showcases the potential of this approach.76 These findings establish ICC as an emerging, effective treatment for aqueous seeding. However, cautious application by trained and experienced ocular oncologists in carefully selected patients remains crucial.

## 7. Future perspectives

A Phase I clinical trials are currently evaluating a sustained-release topotecan episcleral plaque (Chemoplaque) for treating active residual or recurrent retinoblastoma following first-line therapy (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT04156347 and NCT04428879). These studies will primarily assess safety, with efficacy as a secondary outcome.

Despite advancements in chemotherapy by various routes, as discussed previously, some patients experience treatment failure. The mechanisms underlying this resistance are complex and likely differ from resistance to individual drugs. For metastatic retinoblastoma, treatment failure is particularly detrimental, leading to limited therapeutic options and a poor prognosis.89

The primary focus of traditional anti-tumor chemotherapeutic agents lies in disrupting cell division, impacting the dynamics of microtubules crucial for the mitotic spindle and DNA replication.90 On the other hand, targeted therapeutic agents exert their anti-cancer effects through diverse mechanisms, encompassing the inhibition of proliferation, triggering apoptosis, suppressing metastasis, modulating the immune response, and even reversing multi-drug resistance.91 Emerging strategies for addressing retinoblastoma include molecularly targeted therapies, tubulinmodifying molecules, immunotherapy, high-mobility group A (HMGA) protein, vitamin D analogs, angiogenesis inhibition, neurotransmitter pathway disruption, arsenic trioxide, EDL-155, gene therapy, assessment of aqueous humor markers, exploration of exosomes, and utilization of MLN4924 (pevonedistat).92,93 Furthermore, a recent study using retinoblastoma organoid models has identified sunitinib as a promising candidate, exhibiting potent cytotoxicity against both classical RB1-deficient and novel MYCN-amplified retinoblastoma organoids while inhibiting proliferation and inducing differentiation.94

Local drug delivery systems, particularly nanotechnology-based approaches, have revolutionized retinoblastoma therapy by enhancing bioavailability and enabling site-specific targeting.95 NPs exemplify this progress, enhancing ocular penetration of drugs like melphalan while maintaining stability, reducing dosing frequency, and enabling targeted delivery for long-term therapeutic effects.96 Beyond NPs, innovative injectable systems like fibrin glue are under investigation for chemotherapy delivery.92,97 Additionally, a novel dual-layer hydrogel implant shows promise for controlled release of low-molecular-weight hydrophilic drugs such as topotecan and vincristine.92

The field further expands with the exploration of diverse intraocular carriers like dendrimers, liposomes, biodegradable polyesters, mesoporous silica, and gold NPs.95 Each material exhibits unique properties, paving the way for personalized and targeted therapies in retinoblastoma.

While advancements in targeted therapies and molecular biology have demonstrably improved patient and ocular survival in retinoblastoma, further investigation into the long-term implications of these novel treatment approaches remains essential.

## 8. Conclusion

Retinoblastoma management necessitates a complex, multi-modal approach, strategically integrating methods like enucleation, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, and thermotherapy. Of these, chemotherapy has emerged as the mainstay therapy for several decades, demonstrably improving visual outcomes and survival rates.

Chemotherapy is currently the first-line globe salvage treatment for retinoblastoma. However, the optimal choice of delivery route hinges on specific factors including age of the patient, disease staging, disease laterality, germline status, the availability of treatment, financial issue, and the family preference. In cases of bilateral or germline retinoblastoma, IVC plays a crucial role as the primary therapy for controlling intraocular disease, preventing metastasis, and reducing the risk of long-term second malignant neoplasms. In situations involving unilateral or non-germline retinoblastoma, IAC often becomes the preferred first-line treatment due to its targeted delivery to the affected eye, potentially minimizing systemic side effects compared to IVC. Furthermore, IAC serves as a potent salvage therapy following the failure of chemoreduction or other interventions. Moreover, IvitC, ICC, and periocular chemotherapy are adjunctive techniques to optimize outcomes in specific cases with appropriate technical expertise.

Anticipating future advancements in chemotherapy treatments and the implementation of patient-targeted molecular therapies, alongside gene therapy, holds promise for enhancing visual outcomes in retinoblastoma patients, especially those with advanced cases.
