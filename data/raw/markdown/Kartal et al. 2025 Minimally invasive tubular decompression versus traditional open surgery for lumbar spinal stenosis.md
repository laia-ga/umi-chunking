## 70 Abstract

ARTICLE IN PRESS 71 Minimally invasive (MI) tubular decompression seeks to reduce soft‑tissue 72 trauma compared with open laminectomy for lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS). 73 We performed a PRISMA‑compliant systematic review and meta‑analysis of 74 comparative studies (2015–April 2025) identified in PubMed, Embase, and 75 CENTRAL. Adults with symptomatic degenerative LSS undergoing MI 76 tubular decompression or open laminectomy were included. Evaluated 77 outcomes included surgical parameters (e.g., overall complications; dural 78 tears, surgical site infections, reoperation), perioperative and postoperative 79 complications, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), and hospital 80 length of stay (LOS). Heterogeneity was measured using I². Random-effects 81 models were primary; PROMs were also analyzed in a common-effect 82 sensitivity analysis. Nine studies involving ten cohorts and a total of 2,860 83 patients were analyzed from 2,853 records. Random-effects models pooled 84 odds ratios (OR) and mean differences (MD); PROMs were additionally 85 examined with a common-effect model as a sensitivity analysis. MI tubular 86 surgery was associated with fewer overall complications (OR 0.42, 95% CI 87 0.21–0.86), fewer surgical site infections (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.11–0.69), lower 88 EBL (MD −46.65 mL, 95% CI −80.89 to −12.41), and shorter LOS (MD 89 −1.39 days, 95% CI −1.99 to −0.79). Reoperation, operative time, dural 90 tears, and PROMs did not differ significantly. 91 Overall, our results suggest that MI tubular-assisted decompression may 92 offer advantages over open surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis. It was 93 associated with lower perioperative morbidity and resource utilization – 94 including lower overall complication rates, fewer surgical-site infections, 95 reduced blood loss, and shorter hospital stays – while achieving similar 96 patient-reported outcomes. Given between-study heterogeneity and the 97 predominance of retrospective cohorts, these results should be interpreted 98 with appropriate caution; nevertheless, when feasible and in appropriately 99 selected patients and settings, tubular-assisted decompression may be 100 considered a preferable option.

## 1 Introduction

ARTICLE IN PRESS 2 Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is characterized by narrowing of the central 3 spinal canal, lateral recesses, or neural foramina, leading to compression of 4 neural structures in the lumbar spine, which may result in pain, 5 radiculopathy, or neurologic claudication. 1,2 LSS affects about 11% of U.S. 6 adults and approximately 103 million people worldwide. 3,4 LSS is a 7 commonly cited reason for spine surgery in adults over the age of 65 8 because advancing age causes the degenerative changes associated with 9 this pathology. 1,5,6 Conservative treatment for LSS involves physiotherapy 10 with core-focused exercises, oral medications such as NSAIDs, and epidural 11 steroid injections if pain persists. 7 Failure of conservative measures 12 prompts surgical intervention, with or without fusion, depending on other 13 radiographic pathology. Open laminectomy has been the gold standard for 14 treating LSS by totally or partially removing the lamina, as well as the 15 ligamentum flavum and partial medial facets (when indicated), to 16 decompress neural structures. 8-13 While this approach provides symptom 17 relief, it requires extensive retraction and dissection of the paraspinal 18 muscles and soft tissues, and disruption of the posterior tension band, 19 which increases the risk of shortand long-term postoperative 20 complications and prolonged recovery. 8,14,15 21 Minimally invasive (MI) techniques, particularly tubular microsurgical 22 decompression (often performed as a unilateral laminotomy for bilateral 23 decompression, ULBD), overcome many of the limitations of traditional 24 open laminectomy. Tubular decompression creates a working corridor using 25 a muscle-sparing approach and sequential dilators for placement of a 26 tubular retractor system, allowing for targeted decompression of neural 27 elements under surgical microscope visualization. 16-19 MI techniques, such 28 as tubular decompression, have been shown to provide symptom relief with 29 less estimated blood loss, shorter operative times, and lower reoperation 30 rates, lower infection rates, and faster hospital discharge, while preserving 31 spinal stability and minimizing muscle and soft tissue disruption. 8,14,15 

<!-- page: 9 -->

ARTICLE IN PRESS 1 Meta-analyses comparing MI tubular decompression to conventional open 2 laminectomy have previously been limited by small sample sizes, 3 inconsistent outcome measures, and outdated data. 20 Additionally, many 4 studies aggregated various MI techniques, often including endoscopic 5 approaches or failing to explicitly specify the use of tubular retraction 6 systems. 14,21,22 In recent years, advancements in tubular instrumentation 7 and the growing use of MI spine surgery in outpatient ambulatory settings 8 have significantly expanded surgical options for patients. 16-18 Despite these 9 advancements, the need for a high-quality, up-to-date synthesis specifically 10 comparing conventional open laminectomy and MI tubular decompression 11 remains, contributing to uncertainty over the optimal surgical modality for 12 treating LSS. 13 Our study addresses this significant gap by exclusively evaluating tubular14 based MI decompression rather than aggregating various MI techniques. By 15 incorporating recent evidence that reflects advancements in tubular 16 instrumentation and increased surgical proficiency, we provide an updated 17 assessment of the effectiveness and safety profile specific to MI tubular 18 decompression. To accomplish this, we performed a systematic review and 19 meta-analysis on adults with symptomatic degenerative lumbar spinal 20 stenosis, comparing MI tubular decompression to traditional open 21 laminectomy in terms of clinical outcomes, perioperative parameters, and 22 complication rates.

## 2 Study Selection and Data Extraction

ARTICLE IN PRESS 3 Three reviewers independently conducted a systematic screening of titles 4 and abstracts to ascertain eligibility for inclusion. Studies identified as 5 irrelevant were excluded at this preliminary stage. Eligible studies 6 subsequently underwent a detailed full-text assessment. Additionally, 7 bibliographies of the included studies were reviewed to identify any 8 potentially missed studies, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Further 9 search strategies included reviewing trial registries and examining all 10 articles that cite papers included in the review ("snowballing"). Publications 11 authored by identical investigators or originating from the same 12 collaborative international research consortium were excluded to mitigate 13 the risk of duplicated patient cohorts affecting data integrity and 14 analysis. Any discrepancies were resolved through consultation with the 15 senior author. 16 For data extraction, two authors independently populated a standardized 17 Excel sheet, ensuring cross-verification with the original data sources. Any 18 unresolved inconsistencies were adjudicated by the senior author. 19 Kumar et al. (2022) analyzed the data by splitting it into two groups. They 20 reported results separately for patients who had minimally invasive tubular 21 discectomy (MITDS, n = 32) and those who underwent minimally invasive 22 tubular decompression (MITD, n = 8), each compared to their own open23 surgery control group. 24 Treating these cohorts separately preserves the 24 study’s internal comparisons and reflects the distinct surgical indications 25 and techniques evaluated. Only the decompression cohort was used for LSS26 specific PROMs, with perioperative outcomes additionally checked in 27 sensitivity analyses excluding the discectomy cohort. 28 The collected data encompassed key study details, including author names, 29 publication year, sample size, and selected demographic, procedural, and 30 clinical outcomes.

ARTICLE IN PRESS 13 Discussion 14 In summary, tubular decompression was associated with fewer overall 15 complications and surgical site infections, lower EBL, and shorter LOS, with 16 comparable PROMs to open laminectomy, despite notable heterogeneity in 17 several outcomes. The noticeable variability across EBL, LOS, and operative 18 time probably results from differences in surgical experience, tools used, 19 and perioperative procedures among the studies. Kumar et al. pointed out 20 that both MITDS and MITD have a steep learning curve, with complications 21 more common at the start, and that the length of surgery tends to decrease 22 as surgeons gain experience. 24 Variations in retractor design were also 23 observed, ranging from a 22 mm three-blade tubular retractor system 24 (Globus MARS 3V) to 16-20 mm tubular retractors. 24,38 Evaniew et al. also 25 observed considerable variability in institutional practices, noting that no 26 aspect of care, including peri-operative management, was consistent across 27 settings. 32 Additionally, postoperative disc herniation was more prominently 28 observed after multi-level procedures, suggesting that increased surgical 29 complexity may play a role in the variations seen across studies. 36 30 Our results also indicate a significantly reduced EBL and lower rates of 31 surgical site infection with MI tubular decompression compared to open ARTICLE IN PRESS 1 laminectomy, consistent with prior reports. 35,39 Additionally, lower overall 2 complication rates underscore the clinical significance of minimizing tissue 3 disruption, particularly in older populations where comorbidities can 4 exacerbate postoperative risks. Reduced blood loss and tissue trauma help 5 preserve existing immunity in patients, which can aid in preventing 6 infection and promoting optimal healing. 40 7 PROMs also show possible advantages of the tubular approach in reducing 8 back pain as measured by VAS-B, and comparable outcomes regarding VAS9 L and functional disability as assessed by the ODI. Patients presenting 10 predominantly with low back pain should, however, be counseled clearly 11 that the primary goal of this procedure remains the alleviation of leg 12 symptoms and improved walking capacity, rather than directly addressing 13 back pain. 41 Typically, low back pain arises from multifactorial conditions 14 such as facet arthropathy, scoliosis, and sagittal malalignment. 42 One 15 proposed mechanism is that neural decompression reduces compensatory 16 forward flexion, allowing patients to adopt a more upright posture, thereby 17 decreasing muscle strain on the lower back and potentially alleviating back 18 pain. 43 Collectively, these observed benefits support the notion that 19 minimally invasive tubular decompression places a lower physiological 20 burden on patients and reduces hospital resource utilization. 20,44-46 21 Although dural tears trended lower in the MI tubular group, the difference 22 did not reach statistical significance. This finding might reflect a learning 23 curve effect or procedure-specific nuances in dural handling. Aspalter et al. 24 reported a comparable incidence of incidental durotomy between MI and 25 open spine surgery. 47 Similarly, Boukebir et al. identified incidental 26 durotomy in 31 of 313 cases treated with microtubular surgery. 48 However, 27 Sharma et al. found that the MI approach was associated with a 28 significantly lower incidence of dural tears compared to the open 29 approach. 49 However, by restricting our study to tubular approaches only, 30 we reduce technique heterogeneity that may have obscured signals in 31 broader MIS vs open comparisons. 49 It is important to conduct separate ARTICLE IN PRESS 1 analyses of other MIS methods, such as endoscopic procedures, rather than 2 grouping them all together under MIS, to prevent heterogeneity in results. 3 Although evidence across studies indicates that MI tubular decompression 4 does not consistently reduce durotomy rates, it also does not seem to 5 increase the risk. Therefore, minimally invasive tubular decompression can 6 be considered at least non-inferior regarding dural integrity. Moreover, the 7 reduced surgical dead space associated with this technique may decrease 8 the likelihood of patients developing large pseudomeningoceles if 9 cerebrospinal fluid leaks persist. Furthermore, various instruments have 10 been developed and described to facilitate primary dural repair through 11 tubular retractors. 48 When combined with other known advantages, 12 minimally invasive tubular decompression continues to be a favorable 13 surgical option. 22 14 Meanwhile, reoperation rates were comparable between the two groups, 15 affirming that short-term gains do not come at the cost of higher long-term 16 surgical revision. The most significant controversy in minimally invasive 17 lumbar decompression surgery centers around the management of patients 18 with Grade 1 spondylolisthesis who do not exhibit clear instability - 19 specifically, whether fusion should accompany decompression. Open 20 laminectomy disrupts the musculature and posterior tension band 21 extensively, potentially increasing the risk of future slip progression and 22 instability. In contrast, minimally invasive techniques preserve these critical 23 stabilizing structures, potentially reducing the risk of future slip 24 progression and diminishing the need for subsequent fusion procedures. 50,51 25 This reinforces the value proposition of less invasive approaches, as they 26 achieve similar levels of decompression and symptomatic relief compared to 27 their more invasive counterparts. 28 Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported inconsistent 29 outcomes when comparing MI tubular spine surgery to conventional open 30 approaches. 11,39,52 In contrast, this study specifically evaluated the tubular 31 decompression technique, capturing contemporary advances in ARTICLE IN PRESS 1 instrumentation and surgical proficiency, to provide clear evidence of 2 improved perioperative and clinical outcomes. Our focus on tubular 3 decompression prevents the aggregation of disparate MIS techniques. A 4 recent network meta-analytic work in lumbar disc herniation similarly 5 underscores the value of technique-specific comparisons: Liu et al. found 6 tubular microdiscectomy achieved efficacy comparable to open 7 microdiscectomy, while contextualizing outcomes against transforaminal 8 endoscopic discectomy in a Bayesian framework. 53 Although the indication 9 differs from LSS, this multi-arm, granular approach parallels our conclusion 10 that well-defined technique-specific syntheses yield clearer clinical signals. 11 Chin et al. found that endoscopic decompression may offer - 4 Limitations

ARTICLE IN PRESS 5 This meta-analysis has several limitations that require cautious 6 interpretation of the findings. Firstly, after screening, nine studies 7 comprising ten cohorts were included in the analysis, which limits statistical 8 power and generalizability. Additionally, for several outcomes (e.g., ODI, 9 VAS-L, VAS-B, surgical time, LOS, and EBL), data from one study (Kumar et 10 al.) were presented as two separate cohorts, potentially inflating the 11 influence of a single source. 24 This methodological choice may have 12 introduced bias, as results from one center or group of authors are 13 disproportionately weighted, thus limiting the robustness and 14 generalizability of pooled findings. 15 Furthermore, most included studies were retrospective, which naturally 16 increases the risk of selection bias, confounding factors, and inconsistent 17 data quality. Additionally, variations in follow-up durations across these 18 studies further hinder direct comparisons, especially regarding long-term 19 outcomes. The relatively small number of studies also restricts statistical 20 power, limiting the ability to detect smaller yet clinically important 21 differences – a limitation exemplified by the near-significant findings 22 observed for dural tear incidence. 23 Although random-effects models were utilized to account for variability, 24 substantial heterogeneity persisted across multiple outcomes, notably 25 overall complication rates (I² = 64.4%), EBL (I² = 96.8%), LOS (I² = 91.9%), 26 and surgical time (I² = 97.2%). This heterogeneity likely arises from 27 significant clinical and methodological diversity, including variations in 28 patient demographics, surgical techniques, surgeon expertise, and 29 postoperative care protocols. 30 Only four studies contributed to the VAS‑B analysis; with few studies, I² can 31 be imprecise, yet the observed I² (~72%) still indicates substantial

- 2 MI tubular decompression surgery offers significant perioperative 3 advantages over conventional open laminectomy for LSS, including lower 4 overall complication rates, reduced surgical site infections, decreased EBL, 5 and shorter hospital LOS.
