# Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus Uveitis: Onset and Complications

JAY J. MEYER, KEVIN LIU, HELEN V. DANESH-MEYER, AND RACHAEL L. NIEDERER

• PURPOSE: To describe the timing of uveitis onset and frequency of associated complications in individuals with herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO).

• DESIGN: Retrospective, cohort study.

- METHODS: Individuals with acute HZO seen at the Auckland District Health Board from 2006 to 2016 were studied. The primary outcome measures were the proportion who developed uveitis and time to diagnosis of uveitis following the onset of HZO. Secondary outcome measures included complications of HZO uveitis and effects of prompt antiviral (within 72 hours) on outcomes.
- RESULTS: A total of 869 patients with HZO were included for analysis, of whom 413 (47.6%) developed uveitis. Median time from onset of rash to diagnosis of uveitis was 10 days (IQR 6-14). Of the 658 individuals examined within the first week following rash onset (days 0 through 7), 17.6% (116/658) were diagnosed with uveitis at that initial presenting examination, with an additional 24.9% (164/658) diagnosed with uveitis at a subsequent visit. Complications were higher in eyes with uveitis, including moderate or severe vision loss, corneal scarring, neurotrophic keratitis, band keratopathy, corneal melt, elevated intraocular pressure, glaucoma, and cataract (all P < .01). Prompt antiviral was associated with a lower rate of moderate vision loss among eyes with uveitis ( P = .02).

• CONCLUSIONS: Uveitis occurred in approximately half of individuals with HZO and was most frequently diagnosed during the second week following rash onset. Eyes with uveitis were more likely to have other ocular complications and loss of vision. (Am J Ophthalmol 2024;268: 409–415. © 2024 The Author(s). Published by Elsevier Inc. ## INTRODUCTION

H ERPES ZOSTER OPHTHALMICUS (HZO) IS DUE TO reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus along the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve resulting in a characteristic painful, vesicular rash along the dermatome. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus may involve the eye in over half of cases, most frequently causing inflammation of the anterior segment such as conjunctivitis, keratitis, and/or uveitis. 1–4 There are many potential subsequent complications, including corneal scarring, glaucoma, and recurrent inflammation, that may lead to temporary and permanent loss of vision. 5

Patients diagnosed with HZO are often immediately referred to an eye specialist to evaluate for ocular involvement. However, it has been previously noted in a population-based study that many individuals may not be diagnosed with eye involvement at the initial visit with an ophthalmologist, and many complications such as uveitis and elevated intraocular pressure may not occur until weeks or months after the initial HZ diagnosis, although the specific timing of these complications has not been reported. 1

It is important for ophthalmic and nonophthalmic clinicians to be aware of the typical timing and potential complications of ocular manifestations of HZO. This study evaluated the timing of uveitis and frequency of associated complications, including any influence of initial antiviral therapy.

## METHODS

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Auckland Health Regional Ethics Committee (#000027) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were identified by a search for the key words on discharge paperwork for patients reviewed at the emergency eye clinic, Auckland District Health Board, Auckland, New Zealand, between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2016, with follow-up concluded on June 1, 2019, or on patient death or moving care to another center. The visual acuity outcomes of this large cohort of patients with HZO have previously been reported. 5 Patients were considered eligible for inclusion in the study if they were diagnosed with their first episode of HZO with a classic shingles rash in the V1 distribution and seen at the department of ophthalmology, Auckland District Health Board, for their initial ophthalmic examination. Patients were excluded if they had HZV sine herpetica (absence of classic shingles rash), if the diagnosis was not clear, or if they were seen at another eye clinic during the initial HZO onset and later referred or had their initial presentation before the study period.

- • PARTICIPANTS: Participants were considered eligible for inclusion in the study if they were diagnosed with their first episode of HZO with a classic shingles rash in the V1 distribution and seen at the department of ophthalmology, Auckland District Health Board, for their initial ophthalmic examination. Ocular involvement of HZO was defined as any involvement of structures of the eye/globe. Involvement of only the eyelid or only a cranial nerve palsy was not considered ocular involvement. Patients were excluded if they had HZ sine herpete (absence of classic shingles rash) or if the presentation was atypical and the diagnosis was not clear. Patients were generally referred to the ophthalmology department for an ocular evaluation by their general practitioner, urgent care clinic, emergency department, optometrist, or inpatient clinician, if hospitalized. Patients were excluded if they were not seen during the initial episode of HZO, such as those referred with chronic complications from HZO from another eye care center.
- • OUTCOME MEASURES: The primary outcome measures were the proportion of individuals who developed uveitis and the time to diagnosis of uveitis following the onset of HZO. Secondary outcome measures included: patient demographics associated with uveitis, clinical presentation, and complications occurring in those with uveitis as well as any influence of prompt antiviral treatment on outcomes. Corneal involvement was defined as the presence of epithelial keratitis (punctate epitheliopathy, pseudodendritic epitheliopathy, or epithelial defect), stromal keratitis, or disciform keratitis. Moderate visual loss was defined as bestcorrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≤ 20/50 and severe vision loss was vision ≤ 20/200.6 Visual loss was classified as permanent visual loss owing to HZO if visual acuity did not improve by the final visit after resolution of inflammation, or as visual loss owing to non-HZO causes or nonpermanent visual loss (such as cataract).
- • STATISTICAL ANALYSIS: Data were entered into an Excel database and analyzed in Stata, version 15. Categorical data are reported as n (%) and continuous data as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Continuous variables were compared with the Mann-U-Whitney test, categorical variables were compared with the chi square test. A P value of < .05 was considered significant.

## RESULTS

Clinical charts were reviewed for 1010 patients, of whom 141 were excluded because of the final diagnosis being herpes simplex (26 patients) or chickenpox (9 patients), first episode outside of study period or reviewed at a different location (76 patients), herpes zoster sine herpete (9 patients), non V1 dermatome (7 patients), diagnosis uncertain (9 patients), and alternative diagnosis (5 patients). 869 patients with HZO were included for analysis, of whom 413 (47.6%) developed uveitis. Eighty-three subjects (9.6%) were immunosuppressed at presentation, with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs used in 36 subjects (4.1%) and oral corticosteroid in 35 subjects (4.0%), and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection in 5 subjects (0.6%). No subjects reported or had a record of having previously received vaccination for the prevention of shingles. Oral or intravenous antiviral therapy (acyclovir or valacyclovir) was started in 765 subjects (88.0%; 741 oral, 24 intravenous followed by oral), not used in 95 (10.9%), and not documented in 9 subjects (1.0%). Median time from onset of rash to commencing oral antiviral therapy was 3 days (IQR 1-5 days). Intravenous antiviral was given to 24 subjects (2.8%). Four hundred and sixty-eight subjects (53.9%) received antiviral therapy ≤72 hours after the onset of rash (termed “prompt antiviral” for study purposes).

The demographics of subjects with and without uveitis are reported in Table 1. The cohort that developed uveitis was older, had worse presenting best corrected visual acuity, higher presenting intraocular pressure, as well as a higher proportion of corneal involvement and cranial neuropathy.

The median time from onset of rash to diagnosis of uveitis was 10 days (IQR 6-14). The presence of uveitis according to presentation and subsequent visits is reported in Table 2, with 14 individuals excluded from this analysis due to lack of definitive documentation of the date of rash onset. The majority of patients (77.0%; 658/855) presented for their initial evaluation within the first week (days 0 through 7) following the rash onset, while 12.5% (107/855) presented during the second week, 4.8% (41/855) during the third week, and 5.7% (49/855) after the third week. Of those individuals examined within the first week following rash onset, 17.6% (116/658) were diagnosed with uveitis at that initial presenting examination, while an additional 24.9% (164/658) were diagnosed with uveitis at a subsequent visit. Of the 197 individuals examined for an initial visit after the first week following rash (day 8 or later), 59.9% were diagnosed with uveitis, while only 4.6% were diagnosed with uveitis at a subsequent visit. The vast majority (93.0%) were diagnosed with uveitis within the first 30 days following the rash onset. Of those eyes with uveitis, all had anterior uveitis except one eye that had panuveitis.

## DISCUSSION

Uveitis is a common complication of HZO, occurring in nearly half of individuals with acute HZO in this study, and over half of those with ocular involvement. Other smaller studies have reported variable proportions of eyes that developed uveitis ranging from 6% to 61% of those with acute HZO, or 17% to 70% of those with ocular involvement of HZO and are summarized in Table 5 . The marked variability in the reported occurrence of uveitis could be attributed to the study setting as referral centers, such as the present study site, would be expected to have higher proportions than studies involving health delivery systems where more total cases of HZO without ocular involvement might be captured. Variations in the ethnic backgrounds of the study populations could potentially influence the development of complications of HZO as White ethnicity may be associated with severity of disease. 5 , 7 In addition, it is possible that variations in prior vaccination rates could influence the severity of disease among different study populations. Finally, variations in the proportion of patients with HZO who were examined by ophthalmologists may also account for variations in the detection of uveitis between studies. The population of patients in the present study was predominantly Caucasian, unvaccinated, and all patients were examined by an eye care clinician (optometrist, ophthalmologist, or trainee) with a slit lamp examination, all factors that could potentially increase the proportion of those with detected uveitis.

Early commencement of systemic antiviral therapy ( < 72 hours after onset of rash) has been associated with a reduced period of viral shedding, faster healing of lesions, and less pain during the acute period. 15 , 16 More recently, a retrospective study from our research group identified a reduced risk of CVA following HZO in those who received early antiviral treatment. 17 The current study identified a lower proportion of individuals with permanent moderate visual loss ( ≤20/50) due to HZO in subjects with uveitis who received prompt antiviral therapy (10.9% v 18.1%, P = .023). There were no other significant differences in the proportions of patients with uveitis who had complications when comparing those who did and did not receive prompt antiviral treatment. Despite evidence that prompt antiviral treatment may be beneficial, there is evidence that antiviral treatment is frequently not prescribed following diagnosis of HZO. A large study of claims data in the U.S. found that only approximately 50% of patients received systemic antivirals within 7 days of HZO diagnosis. 18

In this study, uveitis was most frequently diagnosed during the second week following rash onset. Among those seen within the first week of rash onset but without uveitis at that visit, approximately one in three (164/542; 30.3%) later developed uveitis that was diagnosed at a subsequent visit. This suggests that for patients with HZO examined within the first week of rash onset who do not have uveitis, clinicians should consider either scheduling a repeat visit to evaluate for later development of uveitis, or review warnings signs of uveitis with the patient that would prompt their return.

Another study prospectively evaluated 64 patients with HZO at day 1, week 1, week 2, week 4, month 3, and month 6 following the diagnosis of HZO, with the initial examination not more than 72 hours after the rash onset. Interestingly, in that study, the majority were diagnosed with uveitis at day 1 or week 1 after the initial diagnosis. 11 However, because timing was reported from the date of diagnosis rather from the date of rash onset, the findings are not directly comparable with the present study.

Keratitis may also occur in eyes with uveitis either preceding, during, or following an episode of uveitis. In this study, 73% of those with uveitis also had corneal involvement in the form of epithelial, stromal, or disciform keratitis. Similarly, anterior uveitis has been associated with keratitis in 52%-77% of eyes with zoster related anterior uveitis in other studies. 10 , 19 Uveitis has been associated with vision loss in this cohort and another study. 5 , 11 It should be noted, however, that vision loss in eyes with uveitis is often due to the associated complications and attendant manifestations rather than due to the uveitis itself. 20 Uveitis is associated with several other vision-threatening complications, and uveitis on presentation of HZO increases the risk of recurrent and chronic disease. 12 Intraocular pressure elevation was common in patients with uveitis, with measurements 24 mmHg or higher in approximately onefourth of eyes at presentation, and one-third at any time point. Additionally, the intraocular pressure was 30 mmHg or higher in approximately one-fourth of eyes at any time point during follow-up. These findings are similar to another study that reported elevated intraocular pressure of 23 mmHg or higher in 40% of eyes with zoster related uveitis. 9 While intraocular pressure may be acutely elevated on presentation, a corticosteroid related elevation may also occur after starting initial treatment of the uveitis. One study found that elevated intraocular pressure was associated with corticosteroid use of 30 days or more in 92% of cases of herpes zoster eye disease with elevated intraocular pressure. 1 Ocular hypertension has been reported as a risk factor for recurrent or chronic eye disease. 12 In addition, secondary glaucoma occurred in over half of eyes in one series with trabeculectomy required in one fourth of those eyes. 20

The current study was retrospective with the inherent limitations of retrospective data. Because examinations were not conducted according to a set schedule following HZO onset, it is possible that uveitis could have been present and diagnosable prior to the times of diagnosis presented in this study. Also, the timing and administration of corticosteroid medication is a potential confounder that was not included in the analysis of the effects of prompt antiviral. There is also the possibility of referral bias, as it is possible that some individuals with HZO were not referred for an ophthalmic examination. However, the vast majority of patients in our community who are referred for an evaluation are referred to our clinic, as it is the only department with a designated acute eye service for a catchment population of over 1 million. In addition, the study period was prior to the availability of a publicly funded vaccine to reduce shingles in New Zealand and may not reflect disease severity in populations with high proportions of vaccinated individuals. A strength of the present study was the review of all charts of patients with herpes zoster by an eye specialist, rather than relying on diagnosis codes during data collection. Additionally, this study did not include patients with zoster sine herpete as the time course following onset of disease in these patients cannot be reliably established.

In summary, uveitis is a common complication of herpes zoster ophthalmicus and is associated with other ocular complications. Prompt antiviral did not appear to prevent onset of uveitis in this cohort but was associated with a lower risk of vision loss in those that did develop uveitis. The diagnosis of uveitis was most frequently made during the second week following the rash onset. Individuals examined during the first week after onset of HZO rash may still develop uveitis after that visit, and a follow up examination within the first month should be considered or they should be warned of the symptoms of uveitis that would prompt a repeat examination.
